Section 4: Metric space topology

Open and closed sets, interior, closure, boundary, continuity through open sets, density, separability, homeomorphisms, equivalence of metrics, product spaces and subspaces. This is the most examinable section.

Open sets and interior

Definition (open set)

$U\subset E$ is open (in $E$) if $\forall p\in U\ \exists r>0:\ B_r(p)\subset U$. In words: wherever you are in $U$, there is a ball centred at your location and contained entirely in $U$. $\mathcal O(E)$ denotes the collection of open sets.

Example (open balls are open sets)
Proof. Let $p\in B_R(a)$ and $r=R-d(p,a)>0$. If $d(x,p)<r$, then $d(x,a)\le d(x,p)+d(p,a)<r+d(p,a)=R$. So $B_r(p)\subset B_R(a)$. ∎

In $\R$ (standard metric), the open balls are exactly the bounded open intervals: $B_r(x)=\,]x-r,x+r[$, and $]c,d[=B_{(d-c)/2}(\frac{c+d}2)$.

Proposition (The family of open sets)

(a) $\emptyset,E\in\mathcal O$; (b) arbitrary unions of open sets are open; (c) the intersection of two (hence, by induction, finitely many) open sets is open.

(a) Immediate: for $\emptyset$ the condition is vacuous; for $E$ every ball lies in $E$. (b) If $p\in\bigcup\mathcal U$, then $p\in U$ for some $U\in\mathcal U$, and $B_r(p)\subset U\subset\bigcup\mathcal U$. (c) If $p\in U\cap V$ with $B_r(p)\subset U$ and $B_s(p)\subset V$, take $t=\min\{r,s\}$. ∎
PitfallInfinite intersections of open sets need not be open: $\bigcap_n\,]-\frac1n,\frac1n[=\{0\}$ is not open in $\R$. The proof of (c) breaks because $\inf_n r_n$ may be $0$.
Proposition

Every open set is a union of open balls: $U=\bigcup_{x\in U}B_{r(x)}(x)$.

Interior

$p$ is an interior point of $A$ if $\exists r>0:\ B_r(p)\subset A$. The interior $\operatorname{Int}A$ is the set of interior points, and $\operatorname{Int}A\subset A$.

Example: $\operatorname{Int}\Q=\emptyset$ in $\R$, because every interval $]x-r,x+r[$ contains irrationals (density of $\R\setminus\Q$).

Proposition (Interior = largest open subset)

$\operatorname{Int}A=\bigcup\{U\subset A:U\text{ open}\}$. Hence (a) $\operatorname{Int}A$ is open; (b) $A=\operatorname{Int}A\iff A$ is open; (c) $\operatorname{Int}(\operatorname{Int}A)=\operatorname{Int}A$.

If $p\in\operatorname{Int}A$, then $B_r(p)$ is an open subset of $A$ containing $p$. Conversely, if $p\in U\subset A$ with $U$ open, then $B_r(p)\subset U\subset A$. ∎

Continuity

Continuity between metric spaces

$f:E\to E'$ is continuous at $p$ if $\forall\eps>0\ \exists\delta>0:\ f(B_\delta(p))\subset B_\eps(f(p))$. It is sequentially continuous at $p$ if $x_n\to p\Rightarrow f(x_n)\to f(p)$. Continuous means continuous at every $p$.

Proposition: continuous at $p$ ⟺ sequentially continuous at $p$
(⇒) Given ε, take δ, then $N$ with $d(x_n,p)<\delta$ for $n\ge N$. Then $f(x_n)\in B_\eps(f(p))$. (⇐, contrapositive) If $f$ is not continuous at $p$, there is $\eta>0$ such that for every $n$ some $x_n\in B_{1/n}(p)$ has $d(f(x_n),f(p))\ge\eta$. Then $x_n\to p$ but $f(x_n)\not\to f(p)$. ∎
Proposition (Lipschitz ⇒ continuous)

$0\le d(f(x_n),f(a))\le L\,d(x_n,a)\to0$. Alternatively, $f(B_r(p))\subset B_{Lr}(f(p))$, so take $\delta=\eps/L$ (any δ works if $L=0$). So $C_{\text{Lip}}(E;E')\subset C(E;E')$.

Theorem (Global continuity via open sets). KEY.

$f$ is continuous ⟺ $f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $E$ for every open $V$ in $E'$.

(⇒) Let $p\in f^{-1}(V)$. Since $V$ is open, $B_\eps(f(p))\subset V$ for some ε. Continuity gives δ with $f(B_\delta(p))\subset B_\eps(f(p))$. So $B_\delta(p)\subset f^{-1}(f(B_\delta(p)))\subset f^{-1}(B_\eps(f(p)))\subset f^{-1}(V)$.

(⇐) $f^{-1}(B_\eps(f(p)))$ is open (balls are open) and contains $p$, so it contains some $B_\delta(p)$. Then $f(B_\delta(p))\subset f(f^{-1}(B_\eps(f(p))))\subset B_\eps(f(p))$. ∎

Uses: $f^{-1}(f(A))\supset A$ and $f(f^{-1}(B))\subset B$ (Exercise 4.3).

PitfallIt is inverse images of open sets that are open. Direct images of open sets under continuous maps need not be open: a constant map $\R\to\R$ sends $\R$ to a single point, which is not open.

Closed sets, closure, boundary

Proposition (Point of closure)

TFAE for $A\subset E$, $p\in E$: (i) $\forall r>0:\ B_r(p)\cap A\ne\emptyset$; (ii) some sequence $(a_n)$ in $A$ has $a_n\to p$; (iii) $\operatorname{dist}_A(p)=0$.

(iii)⇒(ii): approximation of the infimum by a sequence. (ii)⇒(i): $a_N\in B_r(p)\cap A$ for large $N$. (i)⇒(iii): for every ε, some $a\in A$ has $d(a,p)<\eps$, so $\operatorname{dist}_A(p)<\eps$. ∎
Closure, closed

$p$ is a point of closure of $A$ if (i) holds. The closure is $\overline A=\{\text{points of closure of }A\}$, and $A\subset\overline A$.

$A$ is closed if $\overline A=A$. Equivalently: whenever a sequence in $A$ converges in $E$, its limit lies in $A$. This is important to grasp and remember. $\mathcal C(E)$ denotes the closed sets.

Facts about closures
  • $\overline{\overline A}=\overline A$, so closures are closed. Proof: if $p\in\overline{\overline A}$, pick $v\in\overline A$ with $d(v,p)<r/2$, then $a\in A$ with $d(a,v)<r/2$; so $d(a,p)<r$.
  • $\overline{B_r(a)}\subset B_r[a]$. Proof: if $x_n\to p$ with $d(x_n,a)<r$, then $d(p,a)=\lim d(x_n,a)\le r$ (convergence of metric values, order preservation).
  • In general $B_r(a)\subset\overline{B_r(a)}\subset B_r[a]$, and both inclusions can be strict. Discrete space: $\overline{B_1(a)}=B_1(a)=\{a\}$, but $B_1[a]=E$.
  • In a normed space, $\overline{B_r(a)}=B_r[a]$.
Theorem (Closed = complement of open)

$A$ is closed ⟺ $A^\complement=E\setminus A$ is open.

(⇒) If $p\notin A=\overline A$, then $r:=\operatorname{dist}_A(p)>0$, so $B_r(p)\subset A^\complement$. (⇐, contrapositive) If $A$ is not closed, pick $p\in\overline A\setminus A$. Then $p\in A^\complement$, but every ball around $p$ meets $A$. So $A^\complement$ is not open. ∎
MOST SETS IN A GENERAL METRIC SPACE ARE NEITHER OPEN NOR CLOSED

Examples: $[c,d[$ in $\R$, and $\Q$ in $\R$. "Not open" does not mean "closed". Some sets are both ($\emptyset$, $E$, and every subset of a discrete space).

Corollary (The family of closed sets)

(a) $\emptyset,E$ are closed; (b) arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed; (c) finite unions of closed sets are closed. Proof: de Morgan applied to the open-set properties.

Infinite unions can fail: $\bigcup_n[\frac1n,1]=\,]0,1]$ is not closed.

Proposition (Closure = smallest closed superset)

$\overline A=\bigcap\{F\supset A:F\text{ closed}\}$.

Let $H$ be the right side. $\overline A$ is a closed set containing $A$, so $H\subset\overline A$. Conversely, if $p=\lim a_n$ with $a_n\in A\subset H$, then since $H$ is closed, $p\in H$. ∎

Corollary: $(\overline A)^\complement=\operatorname{Int}(A^\complement)$ and $(\operatorname{Int}A)^\complement=\overline{A^\complement}$.

Topological boundary

$\partial A:=\overline A\setminus\operatorname{Int}A=\overline A\cap\overline{A^\complement}$. It is closed (an intersection of two closed sets). $\operatorname{Int}A$, $\partial A$ and $\operatorname{Int}A^\complement$ form a partition of $E$ (allowing empty pieces).

Examples in $\R$: $\partial[0,1[=\{0,1\}$; $\partial\Q=\R$ (closure $\R$, interior $\emptyset$).

Density and separability

Dense, separable

For $D\subset A\subset E$: $D$ is dense in $A$ if $\overline D\supset A$. Test: TFAE (i) $D$ is dense in $A$; (ii) $\forall a\in A\ \forall R>0:\ B_R(a)\cap D\ne\emptyset$; (iii) every $a\in A$ is a limit of a sequence in $D$.

$E$ is separable if it has a countable dense subset.

Example: $\Q\cap I$ and $(\R\setminus\Q)\cap I$ are both dense in $I$, yet they are disjoint.

Proposition (Dense ∩ open-dense = dense)

If $U$ is open and dense and $A$ is dense, then $U\cap A$ is dense.

Let $p\in E$ and $R>0$. (1) Density of $U$ gives $u\in B_R(p)\cap U$. (2) $B_R(p)\cap U$ is open, so some $B_r(u)\subset B_R(p)\cap U$. (3) Density of $A$ gives $a\in B_r(u)\cap A\subset B_R(p)\cap U\cap A$. ∎ (Openness of $U$ is essential: $\Q$ and $\R\setminus\Q$ are both dense, but their intersection is empty.)
Examples of separable spaces

(1) $\ell^p(N)$ for every $p\in[1,\infty]$: $\Q^N$ is countable and dense. (2) $(C(I;\R),d_{\sup})$: polynomials are dense by the Weierstrass Approximation Theorem, and polynomials with rational coefficients approximate them uniformly. A second proof uses piecewise linear functions (Exercise 4.11).

Open subsets of ℝ

Theorem (In ℝ, open ≡ countable union of open intervals)

Let $\mathcal Q=\{]q,r[:q,r\in\Q,q<r\}$, which is countable (it injects into $\Q^2$). For any $A\subset\R$, let $\mathcal Q_A=\{J\in\mathcal Q:J\subset A\}$. Then $\bigcup\mathcal Q_A=\operatorname{Int}A$.

(⊂) Each $J$ in $\mathcal Q_A$ is an open subset of $A$. (⊃) If $]x-R,x+R[\subset A$, density of $\Q$ gives rationals $x-R<q<x<r<x+R$, so $x\in\,]q,r[\in\mathcal Q_A$. For open $U$, $U=\operatorname{Int}U=\bigcup\mathcal Q_U$, a countable union of open intervals. ∎

Challenge (ℝN): every open set in $\R^N$ is a countable union of open balls with rational centres and rational radii (or of open boxes with rational corners). The same proof works using density of $\Q^N$.

Homeomorphisms and equivalence of metrics

Homeomorphism

$f:E\to E'$ is a homeomorphism if (H1) $f$ is continuous, (H2) $f$ is bijective, (H3) $f^{-1}$ is continuous.

  • (H3) does not follow from (H1) and (H2). Example: $\mathrm{id}:(\R,d_{\text{discrete}})\to(\R,|\cdot|)$ is a continuous bijection (every map out of a discrete space is continuous), but its inverse is not continuous ($\{0\}$ is open in the discrete space but not in $\R$).
  • Bi-Lipschitz maps are homeomorphisms.
  • $\tan:]-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2[\to\R$ is a homeomorphism whose inverse is Lipschitz.
  • For a continuous bijection, TFAE: it is a homeomorphism; it maps open sets to open sets; it maps closed sets to closed sets.
Topological vs Lipschitz equivalence of metrics

Let $d_1,d_2$ be metrics on $S$.

(a) They are topologically equivalent if $\mathrm{id}_S:(S,d_1)\to(S,d_2)$ is a homeomorphism.

(b) They are Lipschitz equivalent if $\mathrm{id}_S$ is bi-Lipschitz, i.e. $\exists L,K>0$ with $d_2\le Ld_1$ and $d_1\le Kd_2$.

Lipschitz equivalent ⇒ topologically equivalent (the converse is false). Equivalent metrics have the same open sets.

Proposition (Equivalence via convergence)

TFAE: (i) $d_1,d_2$ are topologically equivalent; (ii) for all $p$ and $(x_n)$: $x_n\xrightarrow{d_1}p\iff x_n\xrightarrow{d_2}p$; (iii) $d_1$-closed ⟺ $d_2$-closed. (Also (iv): $d_1$-open ⟺ $d_2$-open, Exercise 4.7.)

Proposition (Lipschitz equivalence preserves boundedness and Cauchy)

If $d_2\le Ld_1$, then $d_2\text{-}\operatorname{diam}A\le L\,d_1\text{-}\operatorname{diam}A$ and $d_2(x_n,x_m)\le Ld_1(x_n,x_m)$. Apply this both ways.

Topological equivalence does NOT preserve boundedness or Cauchyness

Boundedness: $d$ and $\min\{d,1\}$ are topologically equivalent on $\R$, but $\R$ is unbounded for $d$ and bounded for $\min\{d,1\}$.

Cauchy: $d_{NS}(x,y)=|\tan^{-1}y-\tan^{-1}x|$ is topologically equivalent to the standard metric on $\R$. But $x_n=n$ is $d_{NS}$-Cauchy (since $\tan^{-1}n\to\pi/2$ converges) and not Cauchy in the standard metric. So the two metrics are not Lipschitz equivalent.

An unbounded metric cannot have a Lipschitz equivalent bounded metric: if $d\le Kd'$ and $d'\le M$, then $d\le KM$.

Examples you must know

All $\ell^p$ metrics on $\R^N$ are Lipschitz equivalent, since $\|x\|_\infty\le\|x\|_p\le N^{1/p}\|x\|_\infty$. (Proof: $|x_j|^p\le\sum|x_i|^p\le N\max|x_i|^p$.)

Corollaries: for $A\subset\R^N$, $p,q\in[1,\infty]$: ℓp-open ⟺ ℓq-open; ℓp-closed ⟺ ℓq-closed; ℓp-bounded ⟺ ℓq-bounded. A sequence is ℓp-convergent to $a$ iff it converges coordinatewise ($x_i^{(n)}\to a_i$ for each $i$). The same holds for Cauchy, and bounded means $\exists L:\ |x_i^{(n)}|\le L$ for all $i,n$.

Proof of the coordinatewise criterion (likely exam question)

For every $i$: $|x_i^{(n)}-a_i|\le\|x^{(n)}-a\|_\infty\le\|x^{(n)}-a\|_p\le N^{1/p}\|x^{(n)}-a\|_\infty\le N^{1/p}\sum_j|x_j^{(n)}-a_j|$.

If $\|x^{(n)}-a\|_p\to0$, each coordinate converges (Sandwich). If every coordinate converges, the finite sum $\sum_j|x_j^{(n)}-a_j|\to0$ (algebra of limits), so $\|x^{(n)}-a\|_p\to0$.

The Cauchy and bounded statements follow in the same way, replacing $a$ by $x^{(m)}$ or by $0$. ∎

Proposition (Conditions for α∘d to be an equivalent metric)

Suppose $\alpha:[0,\infty[\to[0,\infty[$ is (i) definite ($\alpha(s)=0\iff s=0$), (ii) increasing, (iii) subadditive, and (iv) continuous at 0. Then for every metric $d$, $\alpha\circ d$ is a topologically equivalent metric.

If moreover (ii)′ α is strictly increasing on some $[0,\eta]$, then $d$-Cauchy ⟺ $(\alpha\circ d)$-Cauchy.

Same convergent sequences. If $d(x_n,a)\to0$, then $\alpha(d(x_n,a))\to0$ by continuity at 0. If $x_n\not\to a$ in $d$, there are $\eta>0$ and a subsequence with $d(x_{n_i},a)\ge\eta$. Then $\alpha(d(x_{n_i},a))\ge\alpha(\eta)>0$ (increasing and definite), so $x_n\not\to a$ in $\alpha\circ d$. Apply Equivalence via convergence.

Cauchy part. (⇒): given $\eps$, continuity at 0 gives $\eta$ with $s<\eta\Rightarrow\alpha(s)<\eps$. Apply the $d$-Cauchy condition with $\eta$. (⇐): given $\eps>0$, $\alpha(\eps)>0$. Choose $N$ with $\alpha(d(x_n,x_m))<\alpha(\eps)$ for $n,m\ge N$. If $d(x_n,x_m)\ge\eps$, then $\alpha(d(x_n,x_m))\ge\alpha(\eps)$ (increasing), a contradiction. So $d(x_n,x_m)<\eps$. ∎

Remarks: these conditions make α continuous everywhere, since $0\le\alpha(s+t)-\alpha(s)\le\alpha(t)$. Conditions (i), (iii), (iv) are also necessary; perhaps surprisingly, (ii) is not.

Standard examples: $\alpha_0(s)=cs$, $\alpha_1(s)=\min\{s,1\}$, $\alpha_2(s)=\frac s{1+s}$, $\alpha_3(s)=\tan^{-1}s$, $\alpha_4(s)=s^{1/p}$.

Corollary: every metric $d$ has a topologically equivalent bounded metric with the same Cauchy sequences (use $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$ or $\alpha_3$).

Products and metric subspaces revisited

Proposition (Convergence in countable product spaces)

Let $d_i'$ be topologically equivalent to $d_i$ with $\sum_id_i'\text{-}\operatorname{diam}S_i<\infty$, and let $d(x,y)=\sum_id_i'(x_i,y_i)$ on $S=\prod S_i$.

(a) $x^{(n)}\to a$ ⟺ $x_i^{(n)}\to a_i$ for every $i$ (coordinatewise convergence).

(b) If, in addition, $d_i'$ and $d_i$ have the same Cauchy sequences, then $d$-Cauchy ⟺ coordinatewise $d_i$-Cauchy.

(⇐) Given ε, choose $K$ with the tail $\sum_{k>K}d'_k\text{-}\operatorname{diam}<\eps$. Then choose $N$ so that the first $K$ coordinates are each within $\eps/K$. This gives $d(x^{(n)},a)<2\eps$. (⇒) $d_i'(x_i^{(n)},a_i)\le d(x^{(n)},a)$. ∎

Finite products with the sum metric: convergence and Cauchyness are both coordinatewise.

Balls in a metric subspace

In $\tilde A=(A,d|_{A\times A})$: $\tilde B_r(a)=A\cap B_r(a)$ and $\tilde B_r[a]=A\cap B_r[a]$.

Openness/closedness is always "in" a space

$A=[0,1[$ is both open and closed in $\tilde A$ (it is the whole space), but neither open nor closed in $\R$.

In $\tilde{\Z}\subset\R$, every subset is both open and closed, because $\tilde B_1(k)=\{k\}$ makes singletons open.

Theorem (Closed and open sets of a metric subspace)

For $X\subset A$: $X$ is closed in $\tilde A$ ⟺ $X=A\cap F$ for some $F$ closed in $E$. Likewise, $X$ is open in $\tilde A$ ⟺ $X=A\cap U$ for some $U$ open in $E$ (Exercise 4.10).

(Closed, ⇒) Take $F=\overline X$ (closure in $E$). Then $X\subset A\cap F$. If $p\in A\cap F$, some $x_n\in X$ has $x_n\to p$. Since $p\in A$ and $X$ is closed in $\tilde A$, $p\in X$. (⇐) If $x_n\in X$ and $x_n\to a\in A$, then $(x_n)$ lies in $F$, which is closed in $E$, so $a\in F$. Hence $a\in A\cap F=X$. ∎

Exercises for Section 4, with full solutions

Exercise 4.1 (Open subsets of discrete spaces) 5 marks
Solution

Every subset is open. For $p\in U$, $B_1(p)=\{x:d(x,p)<1\}=\{p\}\subset U$. (Any radius $r\le1$ works.) ∎

Exercise 4.2 (Open minus finite is open) 10 marks
Solution

Let $p\in U=V\setminus F$. Since $V$ is open, $B_{r_0}(p)\subset V$ for some $r_0>0$. Let $r=\min(\{r_0\}\cup\{d(p,q):q\in F\})$. This is a minimum of finitely many positive numbers ($p\notin F$, so $d(p,q)>0$), hence $r>0$. If $x\in B_r(p)$, then $x\in V$, and $x\ne q$ for every $q\in F$ (as $d(x,p)<r\le d(q,p)$). So $B_r(p)\subset U$. ∎

Alternative: finite sets are closed (finite unions of closed singletons), so $V\setminus F=V\cap F^\complement$ is an intersection of two open sets. "Finite" is essential: $\R\setminus\Q$ is not open.

Exercise 4.3 (Inverse images of direct images and vice versa) 10 marks
Solution

(a) $f^{-1}(f(A))=\{s:\exists a\in A,\ f(s)=f(a)\}\supset A$ (take $s=a$). $f(f^{-1}(B))=B\cap\operatorname{Ran}f\subset B$.

(b) $f(x)=x^2$ on $\R$. With $A=[0,1]$: $f^{-1}(f(A))=[-1,1]\supsetneq A$. With $B=[-1,1]$: $f(f^{-1}(B))=[0,1]\subsetneq B$.

(c) If $f$ is injective, $f(s)=f(a)$ forces $s=a$, so $f^{-1}(f(A))=A$. Conversely, if equality holds for all $A$, take $A=\{s_1\}$: if $f(s_2)=f(s_1)$ then $s_2\in f^{-1}(f(\{s_1\}))=\{s_1\}$. So $f$ is injective.

(d) If $f$ is surjective, $B\cap\operatorname{Ran}f=B$. Conversely, take $B=T$: $T=f(f^{-1}(T))=f(S)=\operatorname{Ran}f$. ∎

Exercise 4.4 (Closed subsets of discrete spaces) 5 marks
Solution

Every subset is closed: its complement is open by Exercise 4.1. So in a discrete space every set is both open and closed. ∎

Exercise 4.5 (Global continuity via closed sets) 10 marks
Solution

Use $f^{-1}(E'\setminus C)=E\setminus f^{-1}(C)$ (Exercise 1.9) and "closed ⟺ complement open".

(i)⇒(ii): if $C$ is closed, $E'\setminus C$ is open, so $f^{-1}(E'\setminus C)=E\setminus f^{-1}(C)$ is open (global continuity). Hence $f^{-1}(C)$ is closed.

(ii)⇒(i): if $V$ is open, $E'\setminus V$ is closed, so $E\setminus f^{-1}(V)$ is closed and $f^{-1}(V)$ is open. Global continuity via open sets gives continuity. ∎

Exercise 4.6 (Equivalences of metrics are equivalence relations) 10 marks
Solution

Reflexive: $\mathrm{id}:(S,d)\to(S,d)$ is an isometry, hence bi-Lipschitz (constant 1) and a homeomorphism.

Symmetric: the inverse of $\mathrm{id}:(S,d_1)\to(S,d_2)$ is $\mathrm{id}:(S,d_2)\to(S,d_1)$. Both definitions require the map and its inverse to be continuous (resp. Lipschitz), which is symmetric in $d_1,d_2$.

Transitive: if $d_1\sim d_2$ and $d_2\sim d_3$, then $\mathrm{id}_{13}=\mathrm{id}_{23}\circ\mathrm{id}_{12}$. Compositions of continuous maps are continuous: $x_n\to p\Rightarrow\mathrm{id}(x_n)\to p\Rightarrow\dots$, or via preimages of open sets $(g\circ f)^{-1}(V)=f^{-1}(g^{-1}(V))$. Compositions of Lipschitz maps are Lipschitz: $d_3\le L_2d_2\le L_2L_1d_1$. Apply the same to the inverses. ∎

Exercise 4.7 (Equivalence of metrics and comparison of balls)
Solution

(a) (i)⇒(ii): $\mathrm{id}:(S,d_1)\to(S,d_2)$ is continuous at $p$, so for $R>0$ some $r_1$ has $B^1_{r_1}(p)\subset B^2_R(p)$. Symmetrically, some $r_2$ has $B^2_{r_2}(p)\subset B^1_R(p)$. Take $r=\min\{r_1,r_2\}$.

(ii)⇒(iii): let $A$ be $d_1$-open and $p\in A$. Then $B^1_R(p)\subset A$ for some $R$, and (ii) gives $r$ with $B^2_r(p)\subset B^1_R(p)\subset A$. So $A$ is $d_2$-open, and symmetrically the other way.

(iii)⇒(i): the preimage under $\mathrm{id}:(S,d_1)\to(S,d_2)$ of a $d_2$-open $V$ is $V$, which is $d_1$-open by (iii). By global continuity, $\mathrm{id}$ is continuous. The same argument applies to its inverse.

(b) (i)⇒(ii): suppose $d_2\le Ld_1$ and $d_1\le Kd_2$ with $L,K>0$. If $d_1(x,p)<R/L$ then $d_2(x,p)<R$, so $B^1_{mR}(p)\subset B^2_R(p)$ with $m=1/L$. If $d_2(x,p)<R$ then $d_1(x,p)<KR$, so $B^2_R(p)\subset B^1_{MR}(p)$ with $M=K$.

(ii)⇒(i): fix $x,p$. For every $R>d_1(x,p)/m$ we have $x\in B^1_{mR}(p)\subset B^2_R(p)$, so $d_2(x,p)<R$. Letting $R\downarrow d_1(x,p)/m$ gives $d_2\le\frac1md_1$. Similarly, for every $R>d_2(x,p)$, $x\in B^1_{MR}(p)$, so $d_1(x,p)<MR$, giving $d_1\le Md_2$. Hence $\mathrm{id}$ is bi-Lipschitz. ∎

Exercise 4.8 ($d_{TGV}$ is not equivalent to the standard metric on ℝ) 10 marks
Solution

(a) For $x\ne y$: $|x-y|\le|x|+|y|=d_{TGV}(x,y)$. So $g=\mathrm{id}:E_2\to E_1$ is Lipschitz with constant $\le1$.

(b) For $x\ne0$, $d_{TGV}(x,0)=|x|+0=|x-0|$. Given ε, take δ=ε: $|x|<\delta\Rightarrow d_{TGV}(x,0)<\eps$. So $f$ is continuous at 0.

(c) For $a\ne0$, let $x_n=a+\frac1n\to a$ in $E_1$. Then $d_{TGV}(x_n,a)=|x_n|+|a|\to2|a|\ne0$, so $f(x_n)\not\to f(a)$, and $f$ is not (sequentially) continuous at $a$. (Equivalently, $B^{TGV}_{|a|}(a)=\{a\}$, so $\{a\}$ is open in $E_2$ but not in $E_1$.)

Deduce: $\mathrm{id}:E_1\to E_2$ is not continuous, so the metrics are not topologically equivalent, and hence not Lipschitz equivalent either. ∎

Exercise 4.9 (Continuity of the metric)
Solution

Apply the Rearranged Triangle Inequality twice: $|d(x,y)-d(u,v)|\le|d(x,y)-d(u,y)|+|d(u,y)-d(u,v)|\le d(x,u)+d(y,v)=d'((x,y),(u,v))$. So $d$ is Lipschitz with constant ≤ 1, and Lipschitz ⇒ continuous. ∎

(i)⇒(ii): for each $p\in X$ choose $r_p>0$ with $\tilde B_{r_p}(p)\subset X$, and let $U=\bigcup_{p\in X}B_{r_p}(p)$. $U$ is open in $E$ (a union of open balls). Then $A\cap U=\bigcup_p(A\cap B_{r_p}(p))=\bigcup_p\tilde B_{r_p}(p)$. This contains every $p\in X$ and is contained in $X$, so it equals $X$. ∎

Alternatively, use complements together with the closed-set theorem: $A\setminus X$ is closed in $\tilde A$, so $A\setminus X=A\cap F$; then $X=A\cap F^\complement$.

Exercise 4.11 (Separability of $C(I;\R)$, again) 10 marks

$A=\bigcup_{n\ge0}A_n$, where $A_n$ is the set of continuous $g$, linear on each $[k/2^n,(k+1)/2^n]$, with $g(j/2^n)\in\Q$.

Solution

(a) The map $A_n\to\Q^{1+2^n}$, $g\mapsto(g(0),g(1/2^n),\dots,g(1))$, is injective: a function that is linear on each piece is determined by its values at the nodes (linear interpolation). $\Q^{1+2^n}$ is countable, so $A_n$ is countable. A countable union of countable sets is countable, so $A$ is countable.

(b) Let $f\in C(I;\R)$ and $\eps>0$. By the Uniform Continuity Theorem there is $\delta$ with $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\eps$. Choose $n$ with $2^{-n}<\delta$, and rationals $q_j$ with $|q_j-f(j/2^n)|<\eps$ (density of ℚ). Let $g\in A_n$ interpolate the $q_j$.

For $x\in[x_k,x_{k+1}]$ (with $x_k=k/2^n$), write $x=(1-\lambda)x_k+\lambda x_{k+1}$ with $\lambda\in[0,1]$. Then $$|g(x)-f(x)|=|(1-\lambda)(q_k-f(x))+\lambda(q_{k+1}-f(x))|\le(1-\lambda)|q_k-f(x)|+\lambda|q_{k+1}-f(x)|.$$ Each term satisfies $|q_k-f(x)|\le|q_k-f(x_k)|+|f(x_k)-f(x)|<2\eps$ (since $|x-x_k|\le2^{-n}<\delta$). So $|g-f|<2\eps$ everywhere, and $d_{\sup}(f,g)\le2\eps$. Hence every ball meets $A$, and $A$ is dense.

A countable dense subset exists, so $E$ is separable. ∎

Exercise 4.12 (Uncountability of ℝ, again) 10 marks

$\psi:\R\to\{0,1\}^\N$, $x\mapsto(F(\lfloor10x\rfloor),F(\lfloor10^2x\rfloor),\dots)$, where $F(m)=0$ for $m$ even and $1$ for $m$ odd. (a) Show ψ is surjective. (b) Deduce there is no surjection $\N\to\R$.

Solution

Here $\lfloor p\rfloor=\max\{m\in\Z:m\le p\}$, the integer part.

(a) Given $a=(a_k)\in\{0,1\}^\N$, let $x=\sum_{k\ge1}a_k10^{-k}$. Then $$10^nx=\underbrace{\sum_{k\le n}a_k10^{n-k}}_{=:m_n\in\Z}+\underbrace{\sum_{k>n}a_k10^{n-k}}_{=:r_n\in[0,1/9]}.$$ Since $0\le r_n\le\sum_{j\ge1}10^{-j}=\frac19<1$, we get $\lfloor10^nx\rfloor=m_n$. Also $m_n=a_n+10(\dots)$, so $m_n\equiv a_n\pmod2$ and $F(m_n)=a_n$. Hence $\psi(x)=a$, and ψ is surjective.

(b) If $\sigma:\N\to\R$ were surjective, then $\psi\circ\sigma:\N\to\{0,1\}^\N\cong\F(\N;\{0,1\})$ would be a surjection, contradicting Cantor's Theorem (no surjection $S\to\F(S;\{0,1\})$). Since $\R\ne\emptyset$, this means $\R$ is not countable. ∎

In-text: why every subset of $\tilde{\Z}$ is open (and closed)
Answer

Every subset $X\subset\Z$ is a union of singletons, $X=\bigcup_{k\in X}\{k\}$. Each singleton is the open ball $\tilde B_1(k)$, and unions of open sets are open. So $X$ is open; and since $\Z\setminus X$ is open too, $X$ is also closed. This explains the "It suffices to show… (Why?)" step in the notes.
