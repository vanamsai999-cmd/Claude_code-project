6 Chapter 1: Linear systems and their description

In linear systems, we consider a machine made up of several components,
which are connected together. We take t > 0 to be time and consider
the evolution of the system through time. The machine has an input
u = u(t), and output y = y(t) and the internal state of the machine is
described by a state x = x(t), and we take u, x, y to be vector-valued
functions of t. The state of a system is a set of variables whose values,
together with the input and the equations describing the dynamics, will
describe the future state and output of the system. Usually, we want to
know how y(t) depends upon u(t). The component parts of the machine
are represented by various linear operators. We consider a special class of
linear systems that we can analyze by means of linear algebra. We obtain:
• general results which enable us to classify and describe a significant
class of linear systems, called (A, B, C, D);
• specific methods for solving these linear systems;
• results that are in a form that allows effective and explicit computation
of solutions, usually using computers.

11 Block Diagrams

Let V be the space of infinitely differentiable functions f : (0, ∞) → C.
Let u ∈ V be the input, y ∈ V be the output. A diagram is a graph built
up from vertices u, y and others chosen from
                                               ∫︂
                         u, y, δ0 , ⊕, ·, d/dt, , a, h
                       {︂                              }︂


which are connected by directed edges, drawn as arrows.
(1) u is the input and y is the output. The vertices u and y have degree
one; whereas all other vertices have degree two or three with one or two
arrows pointing into the vertex, and one or two pointing out.
(2) The graph is simple, so there are no loops based at vertices or
multiple edges between vertices. All vertices lie on some directed path of
consecutive arrows from u to y.
(3) If the diagram contains a circuit, then we say that there is feedback.
(4) If the diagram does not contain a circuit, then the system is ‘open
loop’ or ‘straight through’.

13 Feedback


[See video: V1 feedback block diagram]
Open loop Here the input u is subject to linear operations and produces
an output y. For example

                         y(t) = atu(t) + u ′′ (t).

Some machines are open loop, for instance rockets, or primitive turbines.
Closed loop or feedback systems Here we take the input, subject it to
linear operations, and also take the output, feed it back into the system,
apply linear operations to the output and then add the modified input
and the modified output. Most modern appliances involve some feedback
or control systems. For instance, car engines, wind turbines involve
feedback systems to control their rate of rotation.

proportional to the number of birds, and birds can come and go by flying

                             dx

Example 2. (Turbocharger) A car engine has a turbocharger. The faster
the engine revolves, the more exhaust it produces, so the turbine forces
more air into the engine, so the engine goes faster and so on.
Most practical devices incorporate some negative feedback so that they
do not damage themselves or their users.

Example 3. (Black’s amplifier) Let 0 < θ < 1. The output y is fed back
into the input u, after multiplication by θ, so y = θy + u, hence

whereas the tap is beside the output.

23 Damped harmonic oscillator

Damped harmonic oscillators are important, because they (i) arise in
various physical and electrical systems; (ii) exhibit the effects that
describe more general linear systems. So this example will reappear.
Let t be time and y displacement from rest of a mass on a spring. The
velocity is y ′ and acceleration y ′′ , the mass is driven by an external
driving force u(t). The equation is

                           y ′′ + βy ′ + γy = u

where β and γ are constants; usually γ > 0 and β ≥ 0. This models the
suspension of a car moving along a road. Here u(t) represents the force
imparted on the car by the road; the suspension involves a spring which
gives a restoring force −γy, while the shock absorbers give a damping
force −βy ′ . We can write this as a feedback system, using the formula
                            ∫︂        ∫︂ ∫︂    ∫︂ ∫︂
                     y = −β y − γ           y+       u.

40 Adjoint of a matrix


Definition (i) The transpose of a n × m matrix A = [ajk ] is the m × n
matrix A⊤ = [akj ], obtained by interchanging rows with columns.
(ii) We define the adjoint of a n × m matrix A = [ajk ] to be the m × n
matrix A′ = [ākj ] given interchanging the rows and columns and taking
the complex conjugate. If A is real, then A′ = A⊤ , the transpose. We
have ⟨Ax, y⟩ = ⟨x, A′ y⟩. This is MATLAB-style notation commonly used
in linear systems engineering; in linear algebra the notation A∗ is used for
adjoint, and in physics A† is used. The adjoint is often called the
Hermitian conjugate.

complex polynomials, with h(s) not the zero polynomial. Then

                               f (s) =
                                         h(s)

is said to be a rational function. The set of all complex rational functions

addition, division and differentiation.

Proposition

Proof. The characteristic polynomial det(sI − A) has degree n, and

104 Lerch’s Uniqueness Theorem


Theorem
Suppose that f and g satisfy (E ) and that there exists s0 such that

                     L(f )(s) = L(g)(s)       (s > s0 ).

Then f (x) = g(x) for all x > 0.

Here the Laplace transforms of f and g are equal on the line segment
(s0 , ∞) of infinite length. The proof of this is rather difficult, hence is
omitted. There is an inversion formula, credited to Bromwich, which
recovers f from its Laplace transform F via a contour integral;
unfortunately, it is rather hard to use. So if one knows that F (λ) occurs
as L(f )(λ) for some f , then the best way to find f is by comparing F
with known Laplace transforms in tables, then invoking the uniqueness
theorem.

(i) Each eigenvalue λj has algebraic multiplicity nj , where nj is the

(ii) For each eigenvalue λj , there is an eigenvector vj . Let

λj is the dimension of E (λj ).
(iii) For each λj , the geometric multiplicity is the number of Jordan
blocks that involve λj , so the geometric multiplicity is less than or equal
to the algebraic multiplicity.

eigenvectors and generalised eigenvectors. A generalised eigenvector is

The Bode plot is the logarithm of the gain log Γ in decibels versus ω in
radians per second, with ω > 0 plotted on a log scale. Going from left to
right on the Bode plot, ω increases from 0 to ∞, and iω goes up the
imaginary axis. Recall that 360◦ is equivalent to 2π radians, and be
careful with the scales on the plots. The phase (or phase shift) ϕ(ω) is
the change in phase of the signal between input and output at a given
input frequency. Phase ϕ in degrees is plotted against ω in radians per
second, with ω > 0 on a log scale.
i) If ϕ(ω) > 0, there is phase gain, so the output runs ahead of the input.
ii) If ϕ(ω) < 0, there is a phase lag, so the output runs behind the input.
iii) If ϕ = 0, then the input and output are said to be in phase.
iv) If ϕ = π (or 180◦ ), then the input and output are in anti-phase so
input peaks emerge as output troughs. Anti-phase signals are used in
noise-cancelling headphones, where a sound is cancelled by a sound in
anti-phase. Anti-phase is also used in optical lens coatings, to reduce
reflections.

Definition (Stable polynomials)
A polynomial h(x) is said to be stable if all of its zeros are in the open

Problem
Given a monic polynomial, find necessary and sufficient conditions on the

Vehicles are usually under human control.
Example 1. An airliner starts at rest on the runway and then is
accelerated until it achieves lift-off. To achieve this, the engines are run
almost flat out. Once the aircraft reaches cruising altitude, the pilot will
reduce the amount of fuel going into the engines, and fly at a steady

Example 2. A car on the motor way is not run flat out. Instead, the
driver regulates the speed by means of the accelerator, so that when the

the amount of fuel going into the engine, and thus slow down the car.
Likewise the driver can speed up the car by allowing more fuel to go into
the engine. Regulating speed thus involves the continual attention of the
driver; so can we automate this?
180 Feedback controllers


In 1788, the engineer James Watt produced various controllers
(governors) for steam engines. In 1868, Maxwell presented a general
theory of feedback control. The idea is to feed the output from a
machine through a controller back into the input, and thereby regulate
the output. Consider a plant given by a linear system G (m × k) and a
controller represented by a linear system −K (k × m). The output from
G is fed back into the input. The minus sign indicates that we want
negative feedback (if the engine goes too fast, the controller will slow it
down).
• Controllers generally use negative feedback.
• Usually, plants are described by their Laplace transforms.
• Rational transfer functions and controllers are simplest to deal with.
• Proportional-integral-differentiator controllers PID are widely used.

Proof. If P and Q have a common zero λ, then
