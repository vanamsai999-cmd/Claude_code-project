  What these slides are trying to teach you
  These slides explain how matrices stretch vectors, how eigenvectors simplify a matrix,
  and how this lets us solve differential equations such as X ′ (t) = AX(t).

             Norms −→ diagonalisation −→ etA −→ solutions and bounds.

  The numbering follows the numbers printed on the lecture slides: slides 41–59 are PDF
  pages 43–61 in the supplied beamer deck. This explanation follows that deck and the
  corresponding written notes by Gordon Blower. Worked solutions are explanatory model
  answers, rather than official mark schemes.

  Method | How to use this explanation
  Read the meaning of each result before learning its formula. For a proof question, practise
  the displayed argument without looking. For a calculation, follow the numbered method
  and check the final answer. Attempt the purple questions before reading the green model
  answers.

  Notation
  I is the identity matrix; v is a column vector; λ is an eigenvalue; ∆ is diagonal; S has
  eigenvectors as its columns. Vector length is Euclidean, and matrix norm is the induced
  operator norm. The number ℜλ is the real part of λ. The matrix exponential is etA , also
  written exp(tA).

1       Slides 41–42: estimating the norm of a matrix
    Meaning: the largest possible stretching factor

    For v = (v1 , . . . , vn )⊤ , its Euclidean length is
                                                  q
                                          ∥v∥ =       |v1 |2 + · · · + |vn |2 .

    The matrix norm in the lecture notes is

                                             ∥A∥ = sup ∥Av∥.
                                                         ∥v∥≤1


    This asks: among all input vectors of length at most one, how large can the output
    vector become? Equivalently, ∥A∥ is the smallest constant M such that ∥Av∥ ≤ M ∥v∥
    for every v.
    If ∥A∥ = 3, applying A cannot make a vector more than three times as long, and three is
    the largest stretching factor. The definition concerns vector lengths; it is not obtained by
    taking the largest matrix entry in general.

    If ∆ = diag(λ1 , . . . , λn ), then
                                                ∥∆∥ = max |λj |.

    diagonal entry with largest modulus. For example,

2      Slide 43: the three matrix-norm rules
    Learn these three properties

                                            ∥A + B∥ ≤ ∥A∥ + ∥B∥,
                                               ∥αA∥ = |α|∥A∥,
                                              ∥AB∥ ≤ ∥A∥∥B∥.
    The last property is called submultiplicativity. Applying B and then A cannot stretch a
    vector more than the product of their largest stretching factors.




                                                              4
MATH6342 | Slides 41–59 explained                                                      Sai Vanam


    Method | The short proof of submultiplicativity
    For every vector v,
                               ∥ABv∥ ≤ ∥A∥∥Bv∥ ≤ ∥A∥∥B∥∥v∥.
    Take the supremum over ∥v∥ ≤ 1 to obtain ∥AB∥ ≤ ∥A∥∥B∥. Repeating the argument
    gives
                                      ∥Ak ∥ ≤ ∥A∥k .
    This later allows a matrix exponential series to be compared with an ordinary scalar
    exponential series.

    How to justify the other two properties
    For the sum, use ∥(A + B)v∥ ≤ ∥Av∥ + ∥Bv∥ ≤ (∥A∥ + ∥B∥)∥v∥ and take the supremum.
    For scalar multiplication, ∥(αA)v∥ = |α|∥Av∥, so the same factor comes outside the
    supremum.

    Try it first | Exam-style norm estimate

    Suppose ∥A∥ ≤ 2 and ∥B∥ ≤ 3. Give a justified bound for ∥A2 + AB∥.

    Model answer


                                 ∥A2 + AB∥ ≤ ∥A2 ∥ + ∥AB∥
                                           ≤ ∥A∥2 + ∥A∥∥B∥
                                           ≤ 4 + 6 = 10.

    The first line uses the triangle inequality; the second uses submultiplicativity. The result
    is an upper bound, not necessarily the exact norm.

4      Slides 45–46: matrix polynomials and eigenvalues
    Substitute a matrix into a polynomial carefully
    If g(z) = z 2 + 3z + 2, then
                                       g(A) = A2 + 3A + 2I.
    The constant becomes a multiple of I. Also A2 means matrix multiplication, not squaring
    each entry. By convention, A0 = I.
    If Av = λv, then Ak v = λk v by induction. Hence

                                           g(A)v = g(λ)v.

    A matrix polynomial acts on an eigenvector by multiplying it by the corresponding scalar
    polynomial value.

    Method | Slide 46: why eigenvalues solve the characteristic equation
    The eigenvalue equation is Av = λv with v ̸= 0. Rearranging gives (λI − A)v = 0. A
    nonzero solution exists exactly when λI − A is singular. Therefore

                            λ is an eigenvalue ⇐⇒ det(λI − A) = 0.

    The characteristic polynomial is χA (s) = det(sI − A). Over C it has n roots counted
    with algebraic multiplicity, although there may be fewer than n distinct roots.

    Method | Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors in an exam
    1. Form sI − A.
    2. Calculate and factor its determinant.
    3. The roots are the eigenvalues.
    4. For each eigenvalue, solve (A − λI)v = 0 and choose a nonzero solution.
    For a 2 × 2 matrix, the useful shortcut is

                                    χA (s) = s2 − (tr A)s + det A.



5      Slides 47–50: diagonalisation
    The main calculation tool
    A matrix is diagonalisable if
                                             A = S∆S −1
    for an invertible S and diagonal ∆. Choose eigenvectors vj as the columns of S, and put
    the corresponding eigenvalues into ∆ in the same order:
                           S = [v1 · · · vn ],    ∆ = diag(λ1 , . . . , λn ).
    The condition is
                  A is diagonalisable ⇐⇒ A has n independent eigenvectors.


                                                  6
MATH6342 | Slides 41–59 explained                                                   Sai Vanam



  Having n distinct eigenvalues guarantees this, but is not necessary: I is already diagonal
  even though its eigenvalue is repeated.

  What the coordinate change means
  S −1 changes into coordinates in an eigenvector basis. The diagonal matrix then acts
  separately on those coordinates. Finally, S changes back to the original coordinates. In
  eigenvector coordinates, the different directions do not mix.
  Because Avj = λj vj , we have AS = S∆. Multiplying by S −1 gives A = S∆S −1 .
  Independence of the columns is what makes that inverse exist.

  tion

  For λ = −1, solve (A + I)v = 0: the second coordinate is zero, so choose v1 = (1, 0)⊤ . For
  λ = −2, solve (A + 2I)v = 0: the coordinates satisfy x + y = 0, so choose v2 = (−1, 1)⊤ .
  Thus

  Method | Slides 48–50: evaluate a polynomial using diagonalisation
  First,
                            A2 = (S∆S −1 )(S∆S −1 ) = S∆2 S −1 .
  The middle S −1 S cancels. Repeating gives Ak = S∆k S −1 . Substituting into a polynomial
  gives
                             g(A) = S diag(g(λ1 ), . . . , g(λn ))S −1 .
  You evaluate the scalar function on the eigenvalues and then transform back. To prove
  this in an exam, show the cancellation for powers and then substitute those powers into
  the polynomial.

  Exam tips and common mistakes
  Eigenvectors are columns, not rows. Their order must match the eigenvalue order
  in ∆. Do not assume a repeated eigenvalue automatically gives enough independent
  eigenvectors.

6      Slide 51: Cayley–Hamilton
    A matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial
    The theorem states
                                              χA (A) = 0.
    For our running example, χA (s) = s2 + 3s + 2, so

                             A2 + 3A + 2I = 0,             A2 = −3A − 2I.

    This reduces high powers to combinations of A and I. Slide 51 states the theorem without
    proving it.

    Try it first | Exam-style calculation: simplify a high power

    Suppose χA (s) = s2 + 3s + 2. Use Cayley–Hamilton to express A3 as a combination of A
    and I.

    Model answer
    Cayley–Hamilton gives A2 = −3A − 2I. Multiplying by A,

                      A3 = −3A2 − 2A = −3(−3A − 2I) − 2A = 7A + 6I .

    The constant term is 6I, not an unqualified scalar added to a matrix.

8      Slide 56: calculating the exponential
    Diagonalise first, then exponentiate scalar entries
    If A = S∆S −1 , then
                                etA = S diag(etλ1 , . . . , etλn )S −1 .
    This follows by substituting Ak = S∆k S −1 into the defining series. The slide assumes
    distinct eigenvalues as a sufficient condition; the formula works whenever the matrix is
    diagonalisable.

    Method | The exam calculation routine
    1. Find eigenvalues and independent eigenvectors.
    2. Form S, ∆ and S −1 , matching their order.
    3. Exponentiate each diagonal entry.
    4. Multiply out if asked for the entries of etA .
    5. Check e0A = I. A second check is E ′ (0) = A.

10        Slides 57–59: growth bounds and long-time be-
          haviour
  Why the real part of an eigenvalue matters
  For λ = a + ib,
                             etλ = eat eibt ,        |etλ | = eat = etℜλ .
  The real part controls exponential growth or decay; the imaginary part produces oscillation.
  For example −2 + 3i produces a decaying oscillation with amplitude factor e−2t and
  angular frequency 3.

  Read the conclusion precisely
  For a diagonalisable matrix:
  • If every ℜλj < 0, choose κ < 0 to obtain exponential decay of every homogeneous
     solution.
  • If every ℜλj ≤ 0, take κ = 0 to obtain bounded homogeneous solutions.
  • If some ℜλj > 0, an initial vector in a corresponding eigenvector direction produces
     exponential growth.
  The slides assume distinct eigenvalues, which guarantee diagonalisation. Do not remove
  that assumption when quoting their boundary-case result.

  Exam tips and common mistakes
  Bounded homogeneous solutions do not automatically imply BIBO stability. An un-
  damped oscillator has bounded free motion but can resonate under bounded forcing.
  These slides analyse X ′ = AX; forced-system stability requires an additional argument
  later in the module.

  Method | The exam checklist for slides 41–59
  1. Distinguish an exact norm from an upper bound.
  2. Use the identity matrix for constant terms in matrix polynomials.
  3. Match the order of eigenvector columns and diagonal eigenvalues.
  4. Check independent eigenvectors before diagonalising.
  5. Use power patterns if asked for an exponential from its definition.
  6. Check E(0) = I and, where helpful, E ′ (0) = A.
  7. Impose the initial condition by X = etA X0 or eigenvector coefficients.
  8. Keep the hypotheses when concluding boundedness from real parts.
