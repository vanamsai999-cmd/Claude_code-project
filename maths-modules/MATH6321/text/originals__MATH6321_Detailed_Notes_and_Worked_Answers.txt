Mathematical tools



Essential arithmetic and notation | explanation
  The arithmetic running through the module
  a ≡ b (mod n) means n | (a − b). Reduce intermediate values frequently. A residue a has
  an inverse modulo n exactly when gcd(a, n) = 1. The units form the group (Z/nZ)× . For a
  prime p, its order is p − 1; if g generates it, powers g k repeat with period p − 1.
  Keep two moduli separate: group elements in (Z/pZ)× are reduced modulo p; their
  exponents are reduced modulo p − 1 (or the order of the chosen subgroup). RSA’s private
  exponent is an inverse modulo φ(N ), not modulo N .
  For letters use A = 0, B = 1, . . . , Z = 25, except when a displayed permutation explicitly
  labels symbols 1, . . . , 26. In F2 , 1 + 1 = 0, so addition and subtraction both equal XOR.

  Method: Extended Euclid, modular equations and fast powers
  To invert a (mod n), apply Euclid to get gcd 1, then back-substitute to obtain ua + vn = 1.
  The inverse is u (mod n).
  To solve ak ≡ b (mod n), let d = gcd(a, n). If d ∤ b, there is no solution. Otherwise divide
  the whole congruence and modulus by d, invert a/d (mod n/d), and recover d possible
  residues modulo n.
  To calculate ae (mod n), write e in binary. Compute a, a2 , a4 , a8 , . . . by repeated squaring,
  reducing each time, then multiply only the needed powers.

  Read a congruence as a statement about remainders
 The equation a ≡ b (mod n) says that the two numbers land at the same position on a
 clock with n positions. It does not say that a = b as ordinary integers. For example 31 ≡ 5
 (mod 26), because subtracting one complete cycle of 26 leaves 5. This is why a letter shift
 can wrap from Z to A without breaking the cipher.
 You may add, subtract and multiply congruences, provided the modulus is unchanged.
 Division needs more care: it means multiplication by an inverse, and some residues have no
 inverse. The obstruction is a shared factor with the modulus. If 6x ≡ 1 (mod 26), the left
 side is even modulo an even modulus and can never differ from 1 by a multiple of 26. Thus
 no inverse of 6 exists modulo 26.
 There is no reason to keep large intermediate numbers. After a multiplication, replace the
 result by its least nonnegative residue; all later modular calculations remain valid. State the
 modulus beside a calculation whenever the problem uses more than one.

Essential arithmetic and notation | worked practice
  Worked example: Find the RSA inverse 11 inverse modulo 64

                         64 = 5 · 11 + 9,   11 = 9 + 2,    9 = 4 · 2 + 1.
  Back-substitute:

                      1 = 9 − 4(11 − 9) = 5 · 9 − 4 · 11 = 5 · 64 − 29 · 11.

  Thus 11−1 ≡ −29 ≡ 35 (mod 64). Check: 11 · 35 = 385 = 6 · 64 + 1.

  Exam tip: Do not divide a congruence by a nonunit
  From 6k ≡ 10 (mod 22), you cannot multiply by 6−1 , which does not exist. Divide by gcd 2
  to get 3k ≡ 5 (mod 11), giving k ≡ 9 (mod 11), hence k = 9, 20 (mod 22).

  Exam-style question A.1
  Compute 7−1 (mod 26) and solve 4x ≡ 6 (mod 14).

  Model answer A.1
  26 = 3 · 7 + 5, 7 = 5 + 2, 5 = 2 · 2 + 1. Therefore 1 = 3 · 26 − 11 · 7, giving 7−1 ≡ 15. For
  the second equation divide by gcd 2: 2x ≡ 3 (mod 7), so x ≡ 4 · 3 ≡ 5 (mod 7). The two
  residues modulo 14 are 5, 12. Both substitute correctly.

  Exam-style question Practice
  Why is 3/7 (mod 26) meaningful, whereas 3/6 (mod 26) is not a uniquely defined modular
  division?

  Model answer Practice
  The notation means multiply by a modular inverse. Since 7−1 = 15 (mod 26), 3/7 ≡ 45 ≡ 19.
  But gcd(6, 26) = 2, so 6 has no inverse; moreover 6x ≡ 3 (mod 26) has no solution because 2
  does not divide 3. An ordinary decimal approximation to 3/6 is irrelevant to this congruence.

Mathematical tools



Extended Euclid: see every back-substitution
  What the algorithm is trying to produce
  To invert a (mod n), find integers u, v with ua + vn = 1. Reducing this integer identity
  modulo n leaves ua ≡ 1, making u the inverse. Euclid finds the gcd; back-substitution
  converts the remainder calculations into the required identity.
  The inverse exists exactly when the final nonzero remainder is 1. This is the reason an
  unsuccessful inverse calculation should stop at a gcd greater than 1, rather than produce an
  ordinary fraction.

  Worked example: Find 17 inverse modulo 43
  Divide repeatedly:
                           43 = 2 · 17 + 9,   17 = 9 + 8,   9 = 8 + 1.
  Now reverse the chain:
                            1=9−8
                             = 9 − (17 − 9) = 2 · 9 − 17
                             = 2(43 − 2 · 17) − 17 = 2 · 43 − 5 · 17.

  Thus 17−1 ≡ −5 ≡ 38 (mod 43). Check 17 · 38 = 646 = 15 · 43 + 1. The negative coefficient
  is entirely acceptable; converting it to a standard residue is the final step.

  Method: Solve a nonunit linear congruence
  For 6x ≡ 8 (mod 14), the gcd is 2 and divides 8. Divide the coefficient, right side and
  modulus to get 3x ≡ 4 (mod 7). Since 3−1 = 5 (mod 7), x ≡ 20 ≡ 6 (mod 7). Lifting to
  residues modulo 14 gives x = 6, 13. Both work: 36 ≡ 8 and 78 ≡ 8 (mod 14).

  Exam-style question Practice
  Find 9−1 (mod 26) and explain how you checked it.

  Model answer Practice
  Since 3 · 9 = 27 ≡ 1 (mod 26), the inverse is 3. This short observation is a valid computation;
  extended Euclid is a systematic method when the inverse is not immediately visible. The
  multiplication check is still worth writing.

Mathematical tools



Repeated squaring, Euler and exponent cycles
  Calculate powers without writing enormous integers
  Repeated squaring uses a2r = (ar )2 , reducing modulo the chosen modulus at every step. A
  binary expansion of the exponent then tells you which squared powers to multiply. This
  requires a number of squarings proportional to the number of binary digits of the exponent,
  rather than one multiplication for every unit of the exponent.
  Euler’s theorem says aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) when gcd(a, n) = 1. For a prime p, φ(p) = p − 1,
  giving Fermat’s little theorem. The coprimality hypothesis matters: 24 ≡ 0 (mod 8), not 1,
  although φ(8) = 4.

  Worked example: Compute 7 to the power 13 modulo 23
  Since 13 = 8 + 4 + 1, calculate

                            72 ≡ 3,   74 ≡ 9,   78 ≡ 12   (mod 23).

  Therefore 713 ≡ 12 · 9 · 7 = 756 ≡ 20 (mod 23). Each squared remainder represents exactly
  the same residue as the corresponding enormous power.
  We can also simplify exponents when their cycle is known. For nonzero a (mod 23), a22 = 1,
  so a57 = a13 , because 57 = 2 · 22 + 13. This exponent reduction uses modulo 22; the final
  group element still uses modulo 23.

  Exam tip: A negative exponent means an inverse
  If a is a unit, a−r = (a−1 )r . For an element of known order q, it also equals aq−r after
  reducing the exponent modulo q. Without invertibility the negative power is not defined in
  the unit-group calculation.

  Exam-style question Practice
  Compute 310 (mod 17) by repeated squaring.

  Model answer Practice
 32 = 9, 34 ≡ 81 ≡ 13, 38 ≡ 169 ≡ 16 (mod 17). Since 10 = 8 + 2, 310 ≡ 16 · 9 = 144 ≡ 8
 (mod 17).

Chapter 1 | Classical cryptography



Caesar and monoalphabetic substitution | explanation
  Encryption, decryption and the size of the key space
  A plaintext m is encrypted as c = ek (m), with dk (ek (m)) = m. In classical symmetric
  ciphers the same secret key determines encryption and decryption. Algorithms can be public;
  secrecy rests on the key.
  Caesar: c ≡ m + k (mod 26), m ≡ c − k (mod 26). There are 26 keys, so exhaustive search
  is easy.
  Monoalphabetic substitution: a permutation σ ∈ S26 is the key, with c = σ(m) and
  m = σ −1 (c). There are 26! possible keys. This defeats a naive exhaustive search but preserves
  frequencies, repeated letters, word patterns and repeated words. Large key space alone does
  not establish security.
  In cycle notation (a b c) sends a → b → c → a. To decrypt, move backwards in the cycle.
  Symbols absent from the cycles are fixed.

  Method: Caesar or substitution questions
  For Caesar, convert letters to numbers, add/subtract the key, reduce mod 26, and con-
  vert back. To crack it, test the 26 shifts and check a full sentence, or use a credible
  plaintext/ciphertext letter pair to determine k = c − m.
  For substitution, write an explicit ciphertext-to-plaintext table. Start with one-letter words,
  repeated patterns, frequent trigrams (such as THE) and likely common words. Every
  proposed assignment must be consistent across the whole text and injective. Verify by
  re-encryption; a few plausible words are insufficient evidence.

  Why permutations are the right language
  A legitimate substitution must allow the receiver to reverse every replacement. If two distinct
  plaintext letters both encrypted to the same symbol, the receiver could not tell which was
  intended. Requiring a permutation rules out that ambiguity: every letter has exactly one
  image and exactly one preimage.
  For a Caesar key, the whole alphabet moves together. If A goes to H, B must go to I, and the
  remaining assignments follow automatically. A general substitution is much more flexible: A
  might go to H while B goes to Q. Therefore a numerical shift that works for one pair need
  not work for the other pairs.
  The distinction matters in cryptanalysis. A Caesar attack estimates one number. A substi-
  tution attack builds a table of compatible assignments, using repeated words, frequencies
  and letter patterns. For instance a word of pattern XYXX must decrypt to a word whose
  first, third and fourth letters agree. Guessing a four-letter word with four distinct letters
  would immediately violate the cipher’s structure. A candidate table must remain consistent
  across every occurrence, not just in the word that suggested it.

Caesar and monoalphabetic substitution | worked practice

  Worked example: Actual 2025 Q1(a)–(b)
  Caesar key 7 sends CRYPTOGRAPHY to JYFWAVNYHWOF: for instance C : 2 7→ 9 : J, Y : 24 7→
  31 ≡ 5 : F .
  The substitution key in Q1(b) uses 1, . . . , 26, so A = 1. To decrypt AJQIDN, take predecessors
  in the displayed cycles:

                         A(1) ← C(3), J(10) ← I(9), Q(17) ← P (16),

                          I(9) ← H(8), D(4) ← E(5), N (14) ← R(18).
  The plaintext is CIPHER.

  Exam tip: Frequency analysis is evidence, not certainty
  The most frequent letter need not represent E, especially in short texts. Combine frequency,
  word shapes and global consistency. The coursework explicitly rewards the explanation of
  cryptanalysis and awards little for plaintext alone.

  Exam-style question 1.1
  Encrypt MATHS with Caesar key 8, then decrypt KZGXB with key 8. Explain one weakness of
  substitution.

  Model answer 1.1
  M, A, T, H, S correspond to 12, 0, 19, 7, 18. Adding 8 gives 20, 8, 1, 15, 0, so UIBPA. Subtract-
  ing 8 from KZGXB gives CRYPT. Substitution preserves the number and pattern of occurrences
  of each letter: the ciphertext frequency distribution is a relabelled plaintext distribution.
  Enough natural-language ciphertext therefore permits statistical attacks despite 26! keys.

  Exam-style question Practice
  A substitution maps A → Q and B → R. Does that prove it is Caesar key 16? What would
  A → Q, B → S imply?

  Model answer Practice
  The first two assignments are consistent with a shift of 16, but do not determine the other
  24 assignments of a general permutation. A Caesar claim requires every letter to follow that
  same shift. The second pair cannot be a Caesar cipher because the shifts are 16 and 17
  respectively, but it can still belong to a valid general substitution. In either case distinguish
  consistency with a rule from proof that the whole rule holds.

Chapter 1 | Classical cryptography



Vigenere, Kasiski and the one-time pad | explanation
  A repeating key versus a genuinely one-use key
  For a Vigenere key k = (k1 , . . . , kn ), repeat the shifts periodically:

          ci ≡ mi + k1+((i−1) mod n)    (mod 26),       mi ≡ ci − k1+((i−1) mod n)   (mod 26).

  A keyword is converted with A = 0. A plaintext letter may encrypt to several ciphertext
  letters, hiding a single global frequency pattern; there are 26n keys of fixed length n.
  Repetition still creates a weakness.
  Kasiski’s test finds repeated ciphertext trigrams and their spacings. Repetition caused by
  the same plaintext under the same key alignment tends to have spacing divisible by the key
  length. Factor several spacings and look for a recurring divisor. Chance repetitions occur,
  so confirm using IoC and actual decryption.
  A one-time pad has an independent uniformly random key as long as the message, kept
  secret and never reused. Encryption is the same modular addition, or XOR for bits. Every
  candidate plaintext determines exactly one equally likely key; ciphertext alone therefore
  provides no extra information about the plaintext. This does not mean a nonuniform prior
  over plaintexts becomes uniform.

  Method: Execute a repeating-key encryption
  Write the plaintext, repeated keyword, numerical plaintext values, numerical key values and
  the resulting sum in aligned rows. Remove spaces if the cipher convention ignores them; do
  not accidentally advance the key for punctuation. For decryption subtract rather than add.

  The key repeats; the plaintext does not have to
  A Vigenere keyword of length n produces a schedule of Caesar shifts. Think of writing
  the keyword repeatedly beneath the plaintext. The first key letter controls positions
  1, 1 + n, 1 + 2n, . . ., the second controls another set of positions, and so on. A single overall
  frequency table mixes these differently shifted populations and is therefore less directly
  informative than for a fixed substitution.
  The same organisation also explains the attack. If the length is known, separating the
  positions by their remainder modulo n unmixed those populations: each resulting column
  again has one fixed shift. We have not solved the cipher yet, but we have replaced one
  complicated-looking problem by several Caesar problems.
  An OTP goes further by removing the short repeating schedule. Its key entries are indepen-
  dent random choices, not the repeated letters of a word. A message-length piece of ordinary
  English is not automatically a valid pad: it has strong statistical structure. Likewise, copying
  a random five-letter key 100 times does not create 500 independent random key letters. Ask
  how the key was generated and used, rather than looking only at its written length.

Vigenere, Kasiski and the one-time pad | worked practice
  Worked example: Vigenere and pad reuse
  Using keyword KEY for ATTACK, the numerical rows are

                          m = (0, 19, 19, 0, 2, 10),        k = (10, 4, 24, 10, 4, 24).

  Their sum mod 26 is (10, 23, 17, 10, 6, 8), giving KXRKGI.
  For bit pads, if c = m ⊕ k and c′ = m′ ⊕ k, then

                                             c ⊕ c′ = m ⊕ m′ .

  The pad cancels. Known plaintext for one message reveals k = c ⊕ m, and therefore decrypts
  the other. The analogous letter equation is ci − c′i ≡ mi − m′i (mod 26).

  Exam tip: What a comparison answer should say
  Vigenere improves on fixed substitution by varying the letter map with position and obscuring
  overall frequencies. It is still vulnerable to periodicity attacks. OTP eliminates that repeated-
  key structure when its strict randomness/one-use conditions hold, but needs a long securely
  distributed key. An LFSR-generated pad is deterministic and is not a true OTP.

  Exam-style question 1.2
  Decrypt KXRKGI with keyword KEY. Why is using the same keyword as long as the message
  insufficient for perfect secrecy?

  Model answer 1.2
  Subtract repeated shifts (10, 4, 24, 10, 4, 24) from (10, 23, 17, 10, 6, 8), giving (0, 19, 19, 0, 2, 10):
  ATTACK. Length alone does not ensure that the key is uniform, independent, secret or used
  only once. A human-chosen sentence-length keyword has structure and therefore fails the
  uniform-random requirement.

  Exam-style question Practice
  The plaintext word THE occurs twice, with starting positions 4 and 19, under a five-letter
  Vigenere key. Must the ciphertext blocks agree?

  Model answer Practice
  The difference in starting positions is 19 − 4 = 15, a multiple of 5. Both occurrences therefore
  use the same three consecutive key positions, so they encrypt identically. If the distance
  were 14, the alignments would differ and equality would no longer be guaranteed. Conversely,
  repeated ciphertext blocks do not prove repeated plaintext: accidental coincidences can
  occur.

Classical cryptanalysis



Kasiski and finite-sample evidence
  Why a repeated block can reveal a period
  A repeated plaintext block encrypts identically whenever it is aligned with the same positions
  in a repeating Vigenere key. If the key length is n, a spacing divisible by n repeats that
  alignment. Kasiski therefore collects spacings between repeated ciphertext trigrams or longer
  blocks and searches for recurring divisors.
  Not every repeated ciphertext block is caused by this mechanism. Different plaintexts under
  different shifts can coincide by chance. Conversely a text might not repeat a useful block
  at all. A greatest common divisor of all observed spacings can be misleading if even one
  spacing is accidental; compare several pieces of evidence and retain plausible candidates
  rather than treating one statistic as a proof.

  Worked example: Interpret spacings 30, 45 and 75
  Their gcd is 15, so a length dividing 15 is plausible. Candidate lengths include 3,5 and15,
  depending on any supplied bounds. If a hint limits the length to 3–6, the plausible candidates
  become 3 and 5. Separate the ciphertext into columns for both and compare IoCs, then
  attempt actual decryption.
  Suppose another repetition has spacing 14. The gcd of all four numbers is 1. This need
  not mean the key length is 1: the new repetition might be accidental. Look at how often
  particular factors recur and how the column tests behave.

  Method: Combine weak tests into a strong conclusion
  Use Kasiski for possible lengths, column IoC to assess a repeating Caesar structure, shifted
  Mutual IoC to estimate relative keys, then coherent full decryption and re-encryption. Each
  stage answers a different question. A length multiple may split one true Caesar column into
  several smaller Caesar columns and still score well, so test the smallest consistent period too.

  Exam-style question Practice
  If the actual key is CATCAT, what is its fundamental period?

  Model answer Practice
  Although six letters are written, the shift pattern repeats after three. The fundamental
  period is 3, and keys CAT and CATCAT define the same repeating encryption schedule. A
  statistical test may flag both lengths.

Classical security



Why a true one-time pad hides the message
  The key count explains perfect secrecy
  Take a message of t letters and a key chosen uniformly from all 26t possible keys, independently
  of the message. For any fixed message m and ciphertext c, exactly one key works: ki = ci −mi
  (mod 26). Therefore
                                    Pr(C = c | M = m) = 26−t ,
  which is the same for every possible message. Observing c does not favour one message
  over another beyond whatever prior probabilities were already present. This is the precise
  meaning of the ciphertext revealing no information about the message.
  This argument concerns confidentiality under the key assumptions. It does not stop an
  attacker changing a ciphertext. In the bit version, replacing c by c ⊕ ∆ makes the receiver
  obtain m ⊕ ∆. Perfect secrecy alone is therefore not an integrity guarantee.

  Worked example: Known plaintext with a reused bit pad
  Suppose m = 1010 and c = 0110. The pad is m ⊕ c = 1100. If a second ciphertext c′ = 1001
  used the same pad, its message is c′ ⊕ 1100 = 0101. The entire second message is recovered
  without guessing it.
  Even without known plaintext, the relation c ⊕ c′ = m ⊕ m′ leaks structure. This does not
  automatically identify both messages uniquely, but it removes the random mask and supplies
  exploitable constraints.

  Exam-style question Practice
  Does choosing one random letter and repeating it for a 100-letter pad satisfy the OTP
  condition?

  Model answer Practice
  No. There are only 26 such keys, not 26100 . The entries are perfectly dependent, and the
  encryption is just a Caesar cipher on the whole message. Randomly generating a short key
  does not eliminate the weakness caused by its repetition.

Chapter 1 | Statistical cryptanalysis



Index of Coincidence and Mutual IoC | explanation
  Two similar statistics with different denominators
  For a string s of length t ≥ 2, let Na (s) count letter a. Then

                                                   a Na (s)(Na (s) − 1)
                                                P
                                     IoC(s) =                             .
                                                         t(t − 1)

  It is the probability that two distinct positions, chosen uniformly, carry the same symbol. It is
  unchanged by a fixed substitution, since the counts are merely permuted. Long English-like strings
  typically give about 0.0656; independently uniform letters give expected value 1/26 ≈ 0.0385.
  For two strings s, r of lengths t1 , t2 > 0,

                                                          a Na (s)Na (r)
                                                       P
                                     MutIoC(s, r) =                        .
                                                              t1 t2

  One position is selected from each string. When s = r, selection of the same position is allowed,
  unlike IoC. English texts encrypted with the same shift tend to yield MutIoC near 0.0656. Different
  shifts often give smaller values, but this is statistical guidance, not a deterministic test.

  Worked example: Compute both statistics carefully
  For s = ABABA, counts are A : 3, B : 2. Thus

                                                    3·2+2·1  2
                                        IoC(s) =            = .
                                                      5·4    5
  For r = AABC, counts are A : 2, B : 1, C : 1, giving

                                                      3·2+2·1  2
                                     MutIoC(s, r) =           = .
                                                        5·4    5
  The equality is coincidental. For comparison MutIoC(s, s) = (9 + 4)/25 = 13/25, which differs from
  IoC(s).

  Count matching pairs rather than memorising a formula
  Suppose A occurs NA times in a string. There are NA choices for the first selected A and NA − 1
  for a second A at a different position. Their product explains the term NA (NA − 1). Add the same
  count for every letter. The total number of ordered pairs of distinct positions is t(t − 1), explaining
  the denominator of IoC.
  For Mutual IoC, the first position is in one string and the second is in another. There are NA (s)NA (r)
  matching A-pairs, and |s||r| total pairs. There is no subtraction of one, even when the strings happen
  to be equal.
  In a Vigenere attack, a large score is only a clue. Short columns can give noisy scores, and a repetitive
  passage can be far from average English. Several mutually consistent relations are stronger evidence
  than a single isolated maximum. After relative shifts are known, the remaining common shift must
  still be fixed using language plausibility and full decryption. A numerical score never replaces the
  final check that one key explains every letter.

Chapter 1 | Statistical cryptanalysis



Index of Coincidence and Mutual IoC | worked practice
  Method: Recover a Vigenere key from a MutIoC table
  For key length n, split the ciphertext into columns si = ci ci+n ci+2n · · ·. Column i has shift ki . If the
  table shifts sj forward by β, its effective key becomes kj + β. Alignment with si means

                                          βij ≡ ki − kj    (mod 26).

  Pick plausible high/English-like MutIoC entries, turn them into congruences, put k1 = t, and express
  the remaining keys in terms of t. Check the unused rows for consistency, then try the 26 values
  of t using keyword plausibility and decryption. Relative shifts alone leave a common Caesar shift
  undetermined.
  For unknown key length, compare average column IoC across candidate lengths. Multiples of the
  true length may also look good: prefer the smallest consistent length with coherent decryption,
  supported by Kasiski evidence.

  Worked example: Actual 2025 Q1(d): read the sign correctly

  The prominent entries occur at

                        β12 = 7, β13 = 4, β14 = 18, β23 = 23, β24 = 11, β34 = 14.

Chapter 2 | Mechanical cryptography



Enigma components and counting | explanation
  A changing substitution with a reversible path
  A keypress sends a signal through the plugboard, the rotors, the reflector, then back through
  the inverse rotor path and the plugboard. The plugboard swaps disjoint pairs. Rotors
  permute letters; stepping changes the effective permutation from one position to another.
  The reflector pairs all letters and sends the signal backwards. With identical initial settings,
  the machine decrypts by the same process.
  For a fixed rotor state, if the forward permutation is R, reflector U , and plugboard P = P −1 ,
  the overall permutation is P R−1 U RP (rightmost map acts first). Since U 2 = I, the overall
  map is an involution. If U has no fixed points, no letter encrypts to itself. These structural
  restrictions helped attacks, despite the huge key space.
  The module uses a simplified stepping/counting model. Answer using the assumptions stated
  in the question rather than adding historical ring/notch choices not requested.

  Method: Count independent choices without overcounting
  For L letters and exactly m disjoint plugboard pairs,
                                                 !
                              L!          L
                                       =    (2m − 1)(2m − 3) · · · 1.
                        (L − 2m)! 2 m!
                                   m     2m

  Choose the paired letters, then pair them: divide by 2 for the order within each pair and
  by m! for the order of the pairs. If the number of pairs is optional, sum over permitted m,
  including m = 0 if allowed.
  Choose r ordered distinct rotors from R: R!/(R − r)!. If each has L starting positions,
  multiply by Lr . A fully variable paired reflector on even L has L!/(2L/2 (L/2)!) wirings; a
  choice of h fixed reflectors contributes only h. Multiply independent factors.

  Separate the device from the counting assumptions
  An Enigma key specifies machine settings, whereas a message position specifies how far the
  rotors have advanced since those settings were established. Therefore two identical letters at
  different positions need not have the same ciphertext. The effective permutation changes
  even though the initial secret key has not changed.
  The reflector is more than an extra random substitution. It makes the electrical path return
  through the inverse rotor mappings. If the forward path sends a letter to one contact,
  the reflector pairs that contact with another and the reverse path returns to a new letter.
  Applying the complete fixed-state map twice cancels the path and reflector pairing, so it is
  its own inverse. Sender and receiver can therefore use the same initial settings for encryption
  and decryption.
  For counting, write down exactly which choices are independent. A choice of three already
  manufactured rotors does not include a choice of their internal wiring. Starting positions are
  additional choices only when the model treats the fixed wiring separately. The simplified
  model in the notes is the one to use unless the question explicitly specifies another. A
  defensible product of labelled factors is better than an unexplained huge integer.

Chapter 3 | LFSRs



LFSR generation, XOR and pictures | explanation
  A small secret state produces a long deterministic stream
  An LFSR of length n has initial fill (a0 , . . . , an−1 ) ∈ Fn2 and taps (b0 , . . . , bn−1 ), with update

                                ai+n = b0 ai ⊕ b1 ai+1 ⊕ · · · ⊕ bn−1 ai+n−1 .

  The state at time i is (ai , . . . , ai+n−1 ); shift left and append the new bit. The output
  convention used here and in the notes is the stream a0 a1 a2 · · ·, including the initial fill.
  Encrypt bits by ci = mi ⊕ ai ; decrypt by the identical XOR.
  Pictures are flattened in the specified order (the paper uses left to right, top to bottom,
  0 white/1 black), XORed, then reshaped. A long period does not make a bare LFSR
  cryptographically secure: its linear structure can reveal states from enough known output.

  Method: A safe LFSR calculation table
  Write the initial bits with indices. Compute one new bit per column using the original absolute
  indices, never by guessing a visual pattern. For ai+5 = ai ⊕ ai+2 , calculate a5 = a0 ⊕ a2 , then
  a6 = a1 ⊕ a3 , and so on. Keep a row of indices, stream, ciphertext and plaintext. Count the
  required output length before stopping.

  Follow the indices, not an imagined random pattern
 The register is a moving window through an infinite sequence. At time zero it contains
 (a0 , . . . , an−1 ). One update discards the first stored bit, shifts the remaining bits, and
 appends an . The output convention used by the notes lists the sequence starting at a0 , so
 those initial stored bits count towards the requested output length.
 A tap vector is a list of coefficients in F2 , not a list of ordinary counts. Two tapped ones
 contribute 1 + 1 = 0, while three tapped ones contribute 1. Reducing the sum modulo 2
 after adding the tapped bits is equivalent to repeated XOR.
 When encrypting a picture, turn its pixels into a single long sequence before applying XOR.
 A four-by-five picture has 20 positions, not four independent messages of length five. The
 first row uses stream bits 0–4, the next uses 5–9, and so on. A receiver who restarts the
 initial fill for every row is using a different key stream, so even correct XOR arithmetic will
 produce the wrong picture. Label the index ranges explicitly to prevent that error.

  Exam tip: The zero fill always stays zero

Encoding reference



ASCII, binary strings and the cipher are separate
  An encoding is not itself encryption
  ASCII assigns numbers to characters. For example uppercase A has decimal code 65, B has
  66, and space has 32. Writing these codes in binary merely changes their representation;
  anyone knowing the encoding can reverse it. A stream cipher subsequently combines those
  bits with a secret stream using XOR.

  Worked example: Encode a short message before masking

                               Character    Decimal      8-bit form
                               Space                32   00100000
                               A                    65   01000001
                               B                    66   01000010
                               Z                    90   01011010
                               a                    97   01100001
                               0                    48   00110000

  For message AB, concatenate 01000001 01000010. If the first eight stream bits are 10101010,
  the first ciphertext byte is 01000001 ⊕ 10101010 = 11101011. Decryption XORs that byte
  with the same stream and recovers the original A byte.

  Exam tip: Do not confuse alphabet values and ASCII values
  The classical-cipher convention A=0 uses arithmetic modulo 26 on letters. ASCII uses
  character codes such as A=65 and includes spaces, digits and punctuation. A binary LFSR
  example works modulo 2 on encoded bits. State the representation before applying an
  algorithm; switching between these systems without conversion changes the problem.

  Exam-style question Practice
  How many stream bits are needed to XOR-mask five characters encoded as eight bits each?

  Model answer Practice
  5 · 8 = 40 bits. Leading zeros remain part of each byte. Dropping them would alter byte
  boundaries and lose the original encoding. An initial LFSR fill contributes to those first 40
  output bits under the notes’ convention.

Chapter 3 | LFSR periods



LFSR periods and irreducibility | explanation
  The theorems and their hypotheses
  The characteristic polynomial is

                         cb (x) = xn + bn−1 xn−1 + · · · + b1 x + b0 ∈ F2 [x].

  With the notes’ row-state convention, the companion matrix has ones below the diagonal
  and taps in its final column, and ai+1 = ai Bb .
  If b0 = 1, Bb is invertible: the period is the first return to the initial state and divides
  ord(Bb ). Every length-n LFSR is eventually periodic; the eventual cycle has length at most
  2n − 1. If b0 = 0, a transient can occur; leading untapped bits can be discarded to analyse
  the smaller recurrence.
  Main theorem: if cb is irreducible (and has nonzero constant term), every nonzero fill has
  the same period, the smallest positive divisor j | 2n − 1 for which cb | xj + 1. An irreducible
  polynomial is primitive when this order is 2n − 1. Irreducible does not automatically mean
  maximal period.

  Method: Period questions without generating a huge stream
  1. Write cb from the update rule and check its constant term.
  2. Establish irreducibility if the theorem requires it. For degree 3, excluding roots 0 and 1 is
     enough. For degree 4, also exclude division by the only irreducible quadratic x2 + x + 1.
  3. List divisors of 2n − 1 in increasing order.
  4. Reduce xj modulo cb ; test for remainder 1. The first successful j is the nonzero-fill period.
  5. Treat the zero fill separately. If reducible, use state iteration or another valid matrix-order
     argument, not the irreducible theorem.

  A state cycle is different from a repeated individual bit
  A binary output necessarily contains many repeated zeros and ones. That does not identify
  its period. We must look for a return of the whole register state, or prove a recurrence
  repeats from that point. If b0 = 1, the update can be reversed, so a repeated state forces a
  repeated initial state; there is no transient leading into a different cycle.
  There are 2n possible states. The zero state is fixed and, under an invertible update, nonzero
  states cannot enter it. This leaves at most 2n − 1 states on a nonzero cycle. But the state
  space can split into several shorter cycles, so this upper bound is not automatically attained.
  Irreducibility makes the nonzero-fill period independent of the particular fill, through the
  field F2 [x]/(cb ). Primitivity is a stronger assertion: multiplication by the class of x must
  pass through every nonzero field element. Distinguishing these two assertions is essential.
  An irreducible quartic can have period 5 even though the maximum possible nonzero cycle
  length is 15. State which theorem applies before using its conclusion.

  The possible periods divide 15: 1, 3, 5, 15. Degree rules out dividing x + 1 or x3 + 1. The

                             x5 + 1 = (x + 1)(x4 + x3 + x2 + x + 1)
  shows that f ∤ x5 + 1. Thus every nonzero fill has period 15; the zero fill has period 1.

  Exam tip: No roots is insufficient for a quartic
  x4 + x2 + 1 = (x2 + x + 1)2 has no roots in F2 but is reducible. For an irreducible quartic,
  periods can be 5 or 15; quote the theorem and exclude the smaller divisors.

LFSR foundations



Polynomial arithmetic over F2
  Coefficients wrap modulo 2
 In F2 [x], coefficients are 0 or 1, and addition/subtraction are the same operation. Thus
 (x2 + x + 1) + (x2 + 1) = x: the matching x2 and constant terms cancel. Polynomial
 multiplication still distributes, but repeated coefficients are reduced modulo 2.
 Reduction modulo a polynomial is a different second operation. For f = x3 + x + 1, the
 relation f = 0 in the quotient gives x3 = x + 1. Use it to replace every power of degree at
 least 3. The remainder has degree below 3, which makes equality in the quotient easy to
 check.

  Worked example: Build the order test one power at a time
  Working modulo f = x3 + x + 1,

                                       j   xj (mod f )
                                       0   1
                                       1   x
                                       2   x2
                                       3   x+1
                                       4   x2 + x
                                       5   x2 + x + 1
                                       6   x2 + 1
                                       7   1

  For example x6 = x(x2 + x + 1) = x3 + x2 + x ≡ (x + 1) + x2 + x = x2 + 1. The final step is
  x(x2 + 1) = x3 + x ≡ 1. No earlier listed positive power equals 1, so the order is 7.

  Exam tip: An integer remainder calculation will not substitute for this
  The statement f | xj + 1 concerns polynomials, not the numerical value obtained by setting
  x = 2. Work with coefficients modulo 2 and a polynomial remainder. Evaluation at 0 and 1
  only tests possible linear factors.

  Exam-style question Practice
  Reduce x5 modulo x3 + x2 + 1.

  Model answer Practice
  Here x3 = x2 +1, so x4 = x3 +x = x2 +x+1. Thus x5 = x3 +x2 +x = (x2 +1)+x2 +x = x+1.
  Every cancellation takes place modulo 2.

LFSR cryptanalysis



Recover taps from known output
  Linearity can make a long stream predictable
  If the recurrence length is known but the taps are unknown, each observed new bit gives a
  linear equation in the tap coefficients. Enough independent equations can determine those
  coefficients. Known plaintext reveals output bits because ai = mi ⊕ ci , so a cipher that
  exposes enough plaintext/ciphertext pairs may expose the stream’s generating rule.
  The number of equations is not the only issue: they must be independent. A run of zeros
  can supply only zero equations, leaving taps undetermined. Explain the actual system rather
  than claiming any arbitrary set of observed bits always determines the recurrence.

  Exam-style question H.1: a known-plaintext stream attack
  A length-three LFSR has unknown taps (b0 , b1 , b2 ) and observed output 100101. Recover its
  taps. Explain why even a maximal-period LFSR is not a true one-time pad.

  Model answer H.1
  Use ai+3 = b0 ai + b1 ai+1 + b2 ai+2 in F2 . From a0 a1 a2 = 100 and a3 = 1, get b0 = 1. From
  a1 a2 a3 = 001, a4 = 0 gives b2 = 0. From a2 a3 a4 = 010, a5 = 1 gives b1 = 1. Thus the
  recurrence is ai+3 = ai + ai+1 , characteristic polynomial x3 + x + 1, with period 7 for nonzero
  fills.
  Known plaintext and ciphertext reveal stream bits by XOR. Enough stream bits provide
  linear equations for taps and expose the fill. A maximal period describes repetition, not
  computational unpredictability. An OTP instead requires independent uniformly random
  key bits, with no short deterministic recurrence.

  Method: Check a recovered recurrence
  After solving for taps, regenerate every observed output bit, including some not needed for
  solving the equations if available. Then predict the next bit. A successful check demonstrates
  consistency with the observations; the known recurrence length is part of the assumption.

  Exam-style question Practice
  With the recovered recurrence ai+3 = ai + ai+1 and observed 100101, predict a6 .

  Model answer Practice
  Use the bits at indices 3 and 4: a6 = a3 + a4 = 1 + 0 = 1. Do not combine the last two
  observed bits unless those are the positions specified by the taps. All arithmetic is in F2 .

LFSR periods



Reducible examples and theorem boundaries
  Why a failed hypothesis changes the problem
  The irreducible-characteristic-polynomial theorem is powerful because the quotient is a field
  and every nonzero element can be cancelled. With a reducible polynomial the quotient can
  have zero divisors, so that cancellation reasoning no longer applies. Different fills can have
  different periods, and those periods need not divide 2n − 1.
  A direct state table remains valid when the irreducibility theorem is unavailable. If b0 = 1,
  stop at the first return to the initial state. To prove minimality, check that the preceding
  states were distinct. Merely seeing a visually repetitive pattern in some initial output bits is
  insufficient.

  Exam-style question H.2: reducible LFSRs
  An LFSR has recurrence ai+4 = ai + ai+2 . Find the period for fill 0101 and explain why the
  irreducible-polynomial theorem is unavailable.

  Model answer H.2
  Its polynomial is x4 + x2 + 1 = (x2 + x + 1)2 , so it is reducible. Starting 0101, the next
  bits are a4 = 0 + 0 = 0, a5 = 1 + 1 = 0, a6 = 0 + 0 = 0, a7 = 1 + 0 = 1. Continue:
  a8 = a4 + a6 = 0, a9 = a5 + a7 = 1. The first states are

                             0101, 1010, 0100, 1000, 0001, 0010, 0101.

  The initial state first returns at time 6, so its period is 6. It need not divide 15 because
  irreducibility is absent. This explicit calculation also guards against trusting an incorrect
  visual-period claim without checking the recurrence.

  For ai+4 = ai + ai+2 , fill 0101 has period 6. Some statements in the supplied notes’ reducible

  calculation to trust; it checks every appended bit. This is a local correction, not a change to
  the irreducible theorem.

Public-key foundations



Cyclic groups, generators and discrete logarithms
  An exponent is naturally a residue class
  The order of an element g is the least positive q such that g q = 1. Its generated subgroup
  is {1, g, . . . , g q−1 }. If g generates the whole group, it is a generator; in (Z/pZ)× it is also
  called a primitive root.
  If g k = h, then g k+tq = h for every integer t. The full set of discrete logs is therefore k + qZ,
  rather than one special integer. A standard answer chooses a representative in 0, . . . , q − 1,
  together with the modulus.

  Worked example: Compare 2 and 3 modulo 7
  Powers of 2 give 2, 4, 1, then repeat. Its order is 3, so it does not generate 3, 5, 6. The
  discrete log of 3 to base 2 does not exist in this subgroup.
  Powers of 3 give 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1. Its order is 6, so it is a generator. We read log3 5 = 5 (mod 6)
  from the fifth power. Both calculations use the same element modulus 7 but different
  exponent periods.

  Method: Test a proposed generator efficiently

  If the group has known order q, factor q. An element has order q exactly when g q/r = ̸ 1 for
  each distinct prime divisor r | q. Any proper divisor of q misses at least one of these tests.
  For order 6, check powers 3 and 2. For 3 modulo 7 these are 6 and 2, both different from 1.

  Exam-style question Practice
  What are log3 4 and log3 1 modulo 7?

  Model answer Practice
 The table gives 34 ≡ 4, so the first log is 4 (mod 6). The identity is 30 = 1, so its log is 0
 (mod 6), equivalently any multiple of 6. Do not report these exponents modulo 7.

Chapter 4 | Public-key cryptography



Diffie–Hellman key exchange | explanation
  Agreeing a secret without sending it
  Choose a public finite cyclic group G = ⟨g⟩ of order q. In the notes, a standard choice is
  G = (Z/pZ)× , q = p − 1. Alice privately chooses a, Bob privately chooses b. They exchange
  A = g a , B = g b and compute
                                     K = B a = Ab = g ab .
  An observer sees G, g, A, B. Computing a discrete log would recover a or b, hence the shared
  key. The discrete log problem asks for k with g k = h; logs are determined modulo q. DH
  itself establishes a shared value rather than encrypting a message.
  DH requires a suitable group and parameter choices. A small-order base provides only a
  small number of possible secrets. Basic unauthenticated DH is also vulnerable to an active
  intermediary who performs separate exchanges with the two parties; computational hardness
  alone does not authenticate identities.

  Method: A DH computation or explanation
  State the public group/base and private exponents, calculate both public powers by repeated
  squaring, then compute the shared value in both directions. Show that both equal g ab . For
  an insecure-key question, inspect the order of g, trivial exponents, and whether the chosen
  group makes logs easy.

  Public powers hide exponents, not the group operation
  An observer knows how to multiply and invert elements of the public group. Those operations
  are supposed to be easy. The intended difficulty is recovering an exponent from a power, or
  computing the shared power from the two public powers without the secret exponents.
  Alice can raise Bob’s public value to a because she knows a. Bob can raise Alice’s public
  value to b. Both calculations reach g ab because integer multiplication of exponents commutes.
  The attacker cannot obtain g ab merely by multiplying the public values: g a g b = g a+b , which
  has the wrong exponent.
  The discrete-log problem is sufficient to break the exchange: learning a lets the attacker
  compute B a . However, this does not prove that every way to compute the shared value
  must solve a discrete log. Keep a distinction between a useful attack and a mathematical
  equivalence of problems.
  Finally, the order of the chosen base controls the true search space. If g has order 4, then all
  powers depend only on exponents modulo 4, however many decimal digits the parties write
  in their private exponents.

RSA encryption and insecure parameter choices | explana-
tion
  The trapdoor is knowing the prime factors
 Choose distinct secret primes p, q, set N = pq and φ(N ) = (p − 1)(q − 1). Choose e coprime
 to φ(N ), then calculate d ≡ e−1 (mod φ(N )). Public key: (N, e). Private exponent: d; the
 factors must also remain secret.
 For the course’s message space m ∈ (Z/N Z)× , encrypt c ≡ me (mod N ), decrypt m ≡ cd
 (mod N ). Since ed = 1 + tφ(N ), Fermat–Euler gives med = m(mφ(N ) )t ≡ m. This explains
 correctness without being an examinable proof requirement.
 Factoring N reveals φ(N ) and d. Factoring is the course’s underlying hard problem; it is
 not a proved equivalence between every RSA attack and factorisation. These are textbook
 examples; deterministic unpadded RSA has additional weaknesses.

  Method: RSA question checklist
  1. Compute N and φ(N ).
  2. Check gcd(e, φ(N )) = 1. If not, no private exponent exists by the stated construction.
  3. Use extended Euclid to find d; verify ed ≡ 1.
  4. Encrypt modulo N using repeated squaring.
  5. Demonstrate decryption by an explicit power calculation, not only by quoting correctness.

  RSA uses two very different kinds of modular arithmetic
  The modulus N specifies where messages and ciphertexts live. The totient φ(N ) describes
  the exponent cycle on the unit group. Consequently the equation defining the private
  exponent is ed ≡ 1 (mod φ(N )), while the actual message operations are computed modulo
  N.
  The public exponent is chosen to make exponentiation reversible on that group. If e shares
  a factor with φ(N ), the standard inverse construction fails; choosing a large-looking e does
  not repair the missing coprimality. Conversely, any representative d + tφ(N ) produces the
  same power map on units, so the private exponent is naturally an equivalence class modulo
  the totient.
  Factoring N exposes p, q, hence the totient and the inverse exponent. This explains why the
  factors are valuable trapdoor information. It does not mean that RSA hides the algorithms
  or that no other attack could exist. In small exam examples, trial division might be easy;
  you are demonstrating how the construction works, not asserting that these toy numbers
  provide real security. Always show a decryption calculation if the question asks you to
  demonstrate recovery.

RSA foundations



Chinese remainders and why RSA decryption works
  Work modulo each secret prime, then recombine
  For distinct primes p, q, knowing a residue modulo N = pq is equivalent to knowing its
  residues modulo both primes. If two integers agree modulo p and modulo q, their difference
  is divisible by both coprime primes, hence by pq. This is the uniqueness part of the Chinese
  Remainder Theorem used here.
  RSA correctness on units follows immediately from Euler’s theorem. One can also check
  each prime separately: when m is not divisible by p, Fermat’s theorem reduces the exponent
  modulo p − 1. If m is divisible by p, then med and m are both zero modulo p. Repeat
  modulo q, then recombine. This extends the usual positive-exponent decryption identity to
  all message residues, although the notes formulate the scheme on units.

  Worked example: Decrypt the 2025 ciphertext 64 by CRT
  The key has p = 5, q = 17, d = 35. Modulo 5, 64 ≡ 4, so 6435 ≡ 4 because 35 is odd.
  Modulo 17, 64 ≡ 13. Compute 132 ≡ 16 = −1, 134 ≡ 1; since 35 ≡ 3 (mod 4), the result is
  133 ≡ (−1)13 ≡ 4.
  Thus the plaintext satisfies m ≡ 4 (mod 5) and m ≡ 4 (mod 17), hence m ≡ 4 (mod 85).
  In this example the residues match; that is a convenient special case, not a general CRT
  rule.

  Exam-style question Practice

  Model answer Practice

 (mod 35). Check both remainders.

RSA security



Textbook arithmetic can create weaknesses
  A hard underlying problem is not the whole security argument
  Even if factoring a chosen large modulus is difficult, a specific message or key choice may
  make a particular ciphertext easy to recover. Look for structure in the actual data: tiny
  messages, a public exponent of one, repeated prime factors between users, or primes with a
  very small gap.
  For example, if m = 4, e = 3, N = 1001, then the unpadded ciphertext is the ordinary integer
  43 = 64, below N . Taking the integer cube root recovers 4 immediately. This attack does
  not factor 1001. It shows why an attack on one badly prepared plaintext is not the same
  claim as a general solution to all RSA instances.

RSA and El-Gamal signatures | explanation
  A signature is checked with the signer’s public key

  For the course’s RSA auxiliary value m0 , Alice signs σ ≡ md0 (mod N ). Anyone with her

  For the course’s El-Gamal signature in (Z/pZ)× , let q = p − 1, private key k, public h = g k .
  Choose a fresh nonce s with gcd(s, q) = 1. Then

                     m1 = g s   (mod p),   m2 ≡ (m0 − km1 )s−1      (mod q).

  Who needs which secret?
  Confidentiality and verification ask different questions. Encryption asks whether the intended
  recipient can recover a message while outsiders cannot. A signature asks whether an observer
  can check an action attributed to the owner of a particular private key. The receiver of a
  signature should not need the signer’s secret key; otherwise the receiver could produce the
  same evidence.
  For the lecture’s RSA rule, raising an auxiliary value to d is the private action, and raising
  the result to public e checks it. There is no contradiction in everyone being able to check:
  verification reverses a one-way-looking private operation on a specified value, without
  revealing the exponent used to perform it.
  The auxiliary-value scheme is simplified. If the value is unrelated to the document, successful
  verification establishes the arithmetic claim about that value, not that the document’s
  text has been protected against alteration. Likewise, reusing an old valid pair does not
  automatically prove a fresh interaction. For numerical questions follow the supplied rule
  exactly, but for a conceptual weakness question be precise about what the verification
  equation actually establishes. That is stronger reasoning than calling every verifying pair a
  secure document signature.

  El-Gamal is a group-valued masking operation

  the mask; the second contains the message multiplied by that mask. Dropping the first
  component would usually make legitimate decryption impossible. Sending the nonce itself
  would make decryption easy for everyone, since the public h would reveal hs .
  Multiplication is the group operation here, so remove the mask by multiplying by its inverse.
  In the usual prime-modulus examples this means a modular inverse, not ordinary fraction
  arithmetic. You can compute ck1 first and invert its result, or compute c−k

Trial division and Fermat factorisation | explanation
  Difference of squares turns a search into factors
                                       √
  Trial division tests primes up to N ; every composite has such a prime factor. It is easy
  but costly for large generic inputs.
  For odd semiprime N = pq, write N = A2 − B 2 = (A − B)(A + B). The useful pair is
  A = (p + q)/2, B = |p − q|/2. Close factors give small B, explaining Fermat’s advantage on
  such inputs.
  The notes search√ B = 1, 2, . . . for square N + B 2 . An equivalent common implementation
  searches A = ⌈ N ⌉, . . . for square A − N . If a question prescribes a version, follow it. A
                                           2

  nontrivial congruence of squares also suffices:

                 A2 ≡ B 2   (mod N )    =⇒     gcd(A − B, N ), gcd(A + B, N ).

  The gcd must be strictly between 1 and N . A congruence with A ≡ ±B (mod N ) gives

  Method: Fermat calculation
  For an odd input, calculate N + B 2 for consecutive small B, or use the specified A-search.
  Record square/non-square results. Once a square is found, compute both factors and multiply
  them back to check. Deal with an even input immediately by extracting 2; check a perfect
  square separately.

  Why the successful difference of squares is useful
 If N = A2 −B 2 as ordinary integers, then (A−B)(A+B) = N . We have found a factorisation
 by algebra rather than by testing whether every small integer divides N . The factors are
 useful only if both differ from 1 and N ; every odd N also has the trivial representation
 ((N + 1)/2)2 − ((N − 1)/2)2 .
 For a semiprime with odd factors, solving A − B = p, A + B = q gives A = (p + q)/2,
 B = (q − p)/2. This identifies exactly why the gap between the factors matters. A small
 gap means√ a small successful B for the notes’ search. For the A-search, the successful A is
 close to N when the factors are close.
 The modular generalisation is more flexible: it asks only that the squares have the same
 remainder, not that their difference equals N exactly. The product (A − B)(A + B) is then
 divisible by N . Different prime factors may divide different brackets, and gcd computations
 can isolate them. However the brackets may also carry only trivial information, which is
 why both gcds must be checked rather than assuming every square congruence succeeds.

Chapter 5 | Factorisation



Pollard rho for factorisation | explanation
  Find a collision modulo an unknown factor
  Iterate a polynomial such as f (x) = x2 + 1 (mod N ). The residues modulo a hidden factor
  may collide before the residues modulo N do. A gcd of a difference can detect this without
  knowing the factor.
  The notes permit checking gcd(xi ± xj , N ). A faster low-memory implementation (Floyd’s
  cycle approach) runs one sequence at one step per row and another at two steps:

                                  x ← f (x),      y ← f (f (y)).

  Compute d = gcd(|x − y|, N ) (and optionally the plus variant used in the notes). If
  1 < d < N , factorisation succeeds. If d = 1, continue. If d = N , it is a trivial collision:
  restart with another seed/polynomial or backtrack, rather than declaring N a factorisation.

  Method: Show a readable rho table
  Columns should be iteration, x, intermediate f (y), new y = f (f (y)), difference, gcd. Reduce
  modulo N after each squaring. State your seed and polynomial. Stop at a nontrivial gcd
  and divide N by it.

  The hidden collision is detected without knowing the factor
  Suppose N = pq and two iterates satisfy x ≡ y (mod p) but not modulo q. Then p | x − y,
  while q ∤ x − y, so gcd(x − y, N ) = p. We did not need to calculate residues modulo the
  unknown p explicitly: the gcd detects their agreement.
  The polynomial iteration is used as a cheap way to explore residue values. Once a finite
  sequence repeats, it enters a cycle. Floyd’s method compares a walker taking one iteration
  at a time with another taking two. This reduces the storage requirement: a table of every
  earlier iterate is not needed. It does not mean the two walkers take identical steps or that
  you can square only once for both.
  A gcd of N means that the two compared values coincide modulo the whole public modulus;
  it is not a useful discovery of a proper factor. Restarting with a new seed or a different
  iteration polynomial is a normal part of the algorithm. A gcd of 1 simply means that
  this comparison has not separated a factor yet. These outcomes describe progress in a
  probabilistic search, rather than a theorem that one chosen seed must succeed quickly.

  Parity is a bookkeeping device for squares
  An integer is a square exactly when every prime in its factorisation has an even exponent.
  Dixon stores each exponent only modulo 2 while searching for a combination: 0 means even,

  means the selected product has even exponents.

  modulo a large composite N . They solve different tasks. The mod-N arithmetic records
  square residues. The mod-2 arithmetic chooses which relations to multiply. Finally ordinary
  integer exponents reconstruct the square root of the selected product.

  not necessarily usable: if it contains a prime outside the base, discard it for this calculation
  or enlarge the base consistently. Enlarging it adds a new coordinate, so a previously guessed
  parity cancellation might no longer cancel.

  necessarily a useful factorisation. The resulting square congruence can still have equal roots
  up to sign modulo N . Try another dependency when the gcds are trivial.

Discrete logs and Baby-Step Giant-Step | explanation
  Trade a long search for two shorter lists

  For h = g k in a cyclic group of order q, brute force lists 1, g, g 2 , . . ., taking up to order q
                                                     √
  operations. BSGS writes k = i + jm, where m = ⌈ q⌉, 0 ≤ i, j < m. Then

                                             g i = h(g −m )j .

 Build baby steps g i and giant steps h(g −m )j , and look for equality. A collision gives k = i+jm
                         √
 (mod q). It uses order q time and storage with efficient lookup. All candidates must satisfy
 g k = h.

  Method: BSGS table layout
  1. Identify q, the order of the group generated by g, not the modulus itself.
                    √
  2. Choose m = ⌈ q⌉, list g 0 , . . . , g m−1 .
  3. Calculate z = g −m using modular inversion.
  4. List h, hz, hz 2 , . . ., checking for a match after each row.
  5. For a match at baby index i, giant index j, report k = i + jm, reduce modulo q, and
     verify.

  The collision is designed, not accidental algebra
  Every exponent k in the range 0 ≤ k < q has a quotient-and-remainder representation
                                                          √
  k = i + jm, where 0 ≤ i < m and the chosen m = ⌈ q⌉ keeps j < m. This guarantees that
  the two lists contain a match when h belongs to the generated group.
  The baby list explores consecutive small exponents. Each giant step multiplies by g −m ,
  moving backwards by an entire block of m exponents. Matching the lists locates both the
  block index j and the within-block index i. Their combined exponent is then i + jm.
  It is convenient to store the baby values in a lookup table indexed by group element. Generate
  the next baby by multiplying the previous one by g, and the next giant by multiplying the
  previous one by g −m . Recomputing a large power from scratch in every row is unnecessary.
  In a handwritten solution the small tables make the method visible, whereas simply writing
  a discrete-log answer does not show you used BSGS.
  If g is not a generator of the whole ambient group, use its order and make sure h lies in its
  subgroup. Otherwise a log may not exist at all.

Chapter 6 | Discrete logarithms



Pollard rho for discrete logarithms | explanation
  Track how every state depends on the unknown log

  Work with h = g k ∈ (Z/pZ)× and q = p − 1. Store each state as x = g a hb , recording (x, a, b).
  The supplied three-region rule updates:

             Region of x          New element modulo p        New exponents modulo q
             1 ≤ x < p/3          gx                          (a + 1, b)
             p/3 < x < 2p/3       x2                          (2a, 2b)
             2p/3 < x ≤ p − 1     hx                          (a, b + 1)

  For a collision xi = xj ,

                ai + bi k ≡ aj + bj k   (mod q),    (bi − bj )k ≡ aj − ai   (mod q).

  Solve this congruence carefully. If its coefficient is a unit, get one residue; otherwise divide by
  the gcd if permitted and test the resulting candidates. An identity 0 = 0 gives no information
  and needs another collision.

  Method: Rho bookkeeping
  Choose the branch from the current representative x. Update the element and exponents
  together. Reduce element values modulo p and exponents modulo q. After a collision,
  derive the linear congruence from the two recorded exponent pairs. Test each candidate by
  exponentiation. The supplied interval boundaries are nonintegral for primes other than 3; a
  tiny exceptional modulus needs an explicit boundary convention.

  Preserve an invariant at every step

  The table keeps x = g a hb . This is an invariant: after every update, the stored element must
  still equal the expression defined by its stored exponents. Multiplication by g increments a;
  multiplication by h increments b; squaring doubles both. If the element and exponent columns
  are updated using different branches, the final collision equation becomes meaningless.
  At a collision, the two representations of the same element imply equal powers of g, after
  replacing h by g k . Equality of powers gives a congruence in their exponents modulo the
  order of g, not an ordinary integer equality. The usual order p − 1 is often composite, so the
  linear coefficient need not be invertible.
  If the congruence gives several candidates, this is not necessarily failure. Substitute each
  candidate into g k = h, and retain the successful one. A collision that gives only 0 ≡ 0
  contains no information, so another run or collision is needed. More generally, reducing a
  search from hundreds of candidates to two or three can already make the final direct testing
  easy. This combines a structured collision method with a small, justified brute-force finish.

Index calculus in detail



Modular linear algebra with a composite modulus
  Nonzero is not the same as invertible
  Gaussian elimination over a field can divide by any nonzero pivot. The ring Z/qZ is not a
  field when q is composite. For example 2 is nonzero modulo 10 but has no inverse there. Row
  additions and row swaps are safe; multiplying or dividing a row by a nonunit can change the
  solution set.
  One option is to solve individual linear congruences and retain all candidate classes. Another
  is to solve separately modulo coprime prime-power factors of q, then combine the results
  by CRT. State which method you use and verify the reconstructed solution in all original
  relations.

  Worked example: A small relation system modulo 10
  Suppose factor-base logs u, v satisfy

                         2u ≡ 6,      u + v ≡ 1,      3v ≡ 9   (mod 10).

  v ≡ 63 ≡ 3. The middle equation then forces u ≡ 1 − 3 ≡ 8. Thus (u, v) = (8, 3), and all
  three original equations check.

  forced u = 3 and missed the actual solution. This is a mathematical error, not merely a

Chapter 7 | Knapsack



Superincreasing knapsack encryption | explanation
  Hide an easy subset-sum problem
  A positive sequence (a1 , . . . , an ) is superincreasing when each term exceeds the sum of all
  earlier terms. To solve xi ai = s, xi ∈ {0, 1}, inspect terms largest first. Include a term iff
                         P

  it does not exceed the remaining target, subtract, and continue. Superincreasing structure
  makes this greedy choice forced.
  For encryption choose M > i ai , and w coprime to M . Publish the ordered sequence
                                     P

  bi ≡ wai (mod M ), keeping the trapdoor information secret. Encrypt a bit vector by the
  ordinary integer sum c = i xi bi . Decrypt
                              P


                              c′ ≡ w−1 c (mod M ),      0 ≤ c′ < M,

  then solve c′ = i xi ai greedily. The modulus bound prevents wraparound in the private
                  P

  sum. The order of published bi matters even when those numbers are not increasing.

  Method: Knapsack table
  Make aligned rows for ai , bi , and xi . Compute c as the sum of selected bi , without an
  extra final reduction unless a different scheme explicitly asks for it. Find w−1 (mod M ),
  compute c′ , greedily recover bits from the largest private weight down, then report them in
  the original order.

  Why the private greedy step is reliable
  For a superincreasing sequence, a weight larger than the current target cannot occur in the
  subset because all weights are positive. If the current target is at least the largest available
  weight, that weight must be used: the sum of all smaller weights is strictly less than it and
  cannot reach the target without it. These two observations force the greedy include-or-skip
  decision at each stage.
  Encryption hides this easy structure by multiplying each weight by w and reducing modulo M .
  The resulting public sequence can look out of order, but the position-to-bit correspondence
  remains the original one. Sorting the public weights would change which plaintext bit each
  weight represents.
  Multiplication by w−1 (mod M ) removes the disguise from the ciphertext sum. The bound
  M > ai then upgrades a modular statement to an ordinary equality: the genuine private
        P

  subset sum already lies in [0, M ). Without that bound, several private sums could have the
  same residue, making the greedy target ambiguous.
  After the greedy pass, check that the remaining target is zero. A nonzero remainder signals
  inconsistent data, a wrong inverse, arithmetic error, or a sequence for which the guarantee
  does not apply.

  target 6 rather than 26, and the original no-wraparound reasoning fails. Coprimality supplies
  an inverse of the multiplier, but does not by itself preserve the integer subset-sum target.

The simple noisy scheme | explanation
  Private small numbers undo the public modular disguise
  The course chooses a prime p and coprime private integers s, t with
                            q             q                      q
                              p/4 < s <       p/2,       1<t<     p/2.

  Public key
          q (p, v), where v ≡ stq (mod p). For a small nonnegative message/noise value
                                −1

  0 ≤ r < p/4, choose 1 < a < p/2 and encrypt

                                    e ≡ av + r        (mod p).

  Decrypt in two distinct moduli:

             b ≡ te   (mod p),   0 ≤ b < p,           r ≡ t−1 b (mod s),   0 ≤ r < s.

  Why it works: te ≡ as + tr (mod p); the size bounds ensure 0 < as + tr < p, so the least
  nonnegative representative b equals as + tr as an integer. Reducing modulo s removes as,
  and coprimality allows division by t. Since the allowed r < s, the residue is the original
  message.

  Method: Always label both inverse computations
  First compute t−1 (mod p) for the public key. For decryption first reduce te modulo p,
  then compute t−1 (mod s) for the second step. These inverses generally differ. Check
  the parameter bounds before claiming the prescribed scheme applies, and explain the
  no-wraparound step if asked why decryption succeeds.

  The two reductions serve different purposes
 Multiplying e by t modulo p first changes the public mask av into the small integer multiple
 as. The reduction modulo p would normally lose information, but the parameter bounds
 make the actual integer as + tr lie below p. Therefore the standard representative is exactly
 that integer, not merely congruent to it.
 Only after that reconstruction do we reduce modulo s. This removes as, leaving tr. The
 condition gcd(s, t) = 1 then allows multiplication by t−1 (mod s). The recovered residue
 identifies the message because the permitted range of r fits inside [0, s).
 Every condition has a role: invertibility modulo p permits forming the public key; coprimality
 modulo s permits the final division; smallness permits the integer lift; and a bounded message
 range prevents different messages sharing the same final residue. It is not enough to say
 “the numbers are small” without relating that size to these two wraparound steps.
 For a numerical example, record both inverses explicitly even if they happen to have the
 same written value. They solve different congruences and generally will be different.

Gauss reduction and breaking the noisy scheme | explana-
tion
  Replace a bad lattice basis by a short one

  For two independent vectors, order them so ∥u∥ ≤ ∥v∥, then set

                                                 u·v
                                                         
                              µ = nearestInteger      ,         v ← v − µu.
                                                 ∥u∥2

  Repeat with reordering until µ = 0. These integer elementary operations preserve the lattice. A
  reduced basis has ∥u∥ ≤ ∥v∥ and |u · v| ≤ ∥u∥2 /2. Its shorter vector is a shortest nonzero lattice
  vector in dimension two. Nearest-integer ties can be handled consistently; the non-strict reduced
  inequality is the safe stopping test.
  For the simple noisy public key (p, v), construct

                       L = Z(v, 1) + Z(p, 0) = {(S, T ) ∈ Z2 : S ≡ vT     (mod p)}.

  The private pair satisfies s ≡ vt (mod p), so (s, t) ∈ L, and it is short relative to the long public
  basis. Gauss reduction can recover that pair or another suitable short pair. Check coprimality, size
  and the public congruence before using a candidate for decryption.

  Method: A Gauss-reduction exam answer
  1. Give basis (v, 1), (p, 0) using only public data, and show why the private pair belongs.
  2. Use squared norms to decide ordering; avoid unnecessary square roots.
  3. Show the dot product, quotient, rounded integer and new vector at every step.
  4. Reorder after each subtraction. Stop when the rounded quotient is zero.
  5. Change both signs if necessary, test the short pair, and demonstrate decryption.

  Projection chooses the best integer subtraction

  The real number (u · v)/∥u∥2 is the coefficient of the orthogonal projection of v onto the line through
  u. Subtracting that real multiple would remove the entire component parallel to u, but usually leave
  the lattice. Rounding to the nearest integer gives the best allowed integer multiple instead.
  After the subtraction the second vector may become the shorter one, so compare squared lengths
  again and swap if necessary. The algorithm is a repeated improvement of the basis, not a single
  subtraction from the original longer vector. At a reduced pair, the shorter vector has no integer
  multiple that makes the other vector shorter by this projection rule.
  The public noisy-scheme basis is long and nearly parallel; its short integer combinations can therefore
  be much shorter than either displayed vector. This illustrates why the size of a supplied basis does
  not measure the size of the shortest lattice vector. Reduction uncovers the hidden short geometry.
  In the attack, finding a short vector is followed by an interpretation step: orient its sign, check that
  both coordinates meet the key conditions, and test T v ≡ S (mod p). Merely producing a short pair
  without checking its arithmetic role is not a complete decryption attack.

Background: non-examinable



Enigma attacks through conjugation and loops
  A known plaintext creates constraints on machine settings
  Write the position-dependent encryption permutation as σi = P −1 Qi P , where P is the
  unknown plugboard and Qi comes from the rotor/reflector path. A ciphertext/plaintext
  pair at position i gives one equation involving these maps. A loop of such pairs can combine
  several equations into a fixed-point condition.
  For example, a sequence of known relations might force a product Qa Qb Qc to fix the letter
  P (E). The attacker does not know that letter, but can test whether the product has any
  fixed point at all for a proposed rotor configuration. If it has none, that configuration is
  impossible, regardless of the plugboard.

  Worked example: Why conjugation preserves the relevant cycle structure
  If Q sends a → b → c → a, then P −1 QP sends P −1 (a) → P −1 (b) → P −1 (c) → P −1 (a). The
  names change, but the cycle length remains 3. In particular a fixed point remains a fixed
  point under relabelling. This explains why conjugation can remove the need to guess every
  plugboard assignment in a structural test.

  Exam tip: Crib alignment and statistical assumptions
  Because the fixed-state Enigma map has no self-encrypted letter, reject a proposed crib
  placement if a plaintext letter agrees with the ciphertext letter at that position. Further loop
  tests can narrow rotor guesses. Expectations based on independent random permutations
  are heuristics; do not turn an expected number of fixed points into an exact probability
  without additional analysis.

  Exam-style question Background check
  Why can a conjugate of a fixed-point-free permutation not suddenly acquire a fixed point?

  Model answer Background check
  If P −1 QP (x) = x, multiplying the relation by P gives Q(P (x)) = P (x), a fixed point of Q.
  This contradicts the assumption. The supplied exam guidance excludes this attack section,
  so the calculation is for conceptual completeness.

Background: non-examinable



NTRU begins with cyclic polynomial arithmetic
  Replace one integer by a vector of coefficients

  In R = Z[x]/(xN − 1), every class has a representative of degree less than N . The relation
  xN = 1 wraps high powers around. Coefficients can then be reduced modulo p or q to obtain
  the quotient rings called Rp , Rq in these lecture notes.
  The names of the two moduli vary between presentations. Here p is the large coefficient
  modulus, following the supplied notes. Do not mix their notation with another source while
  carrying out the calculation.

  Worked example: A short cyclic multiplication
  Take N = 3. Then

                        (1 + x)(1 − x + x2 ) = 1 + x3 ≡ 2      (mod x3 − 1).

  The coefficient vectors are (1, 1, 0) and (1, −1, 1), and their cyclic convolution is (2, 0, 0). If
  coefficients are then reduced modulo 5, the result is still (2, 0, 0); modulo 2 it is the zero
  vector. Exponent wrapping and coefficient reduction are separate operations.

  The lecture’s encryption mechanism
  Short private polynomials s, t define v = st−1 ∈ Rp . Encrypt a small polynomial message as
  e = qav + r ∈ Rp . The recipient forms

                                       te = qas + tr    in Rp .

  If the integer coefficients are sufficiently small to recover without modular wraparound,
  reduce them modulo q and multiply by t−1 ∈ Rq to obtain r. Thus the two-stage logic
  resembles the simple noisy scheme, but coefficient vectors replace single integers.

  Exam tip: A necessary correction to a printed assumption
 The displayed operations need inverses of t. A balanced polynomial s with equally many
 +1 and -1 coefficients has s(1) = 0, so cannot be invertible modulo xN − 1: evaluation at
 1 would contradict su = 1. The notes’ blanket requirement that this s also be invertible
 is inconsistent with its balanced form. This excluded background section uses the actual
 inverses required by the operations.

1      Question 1: Caesar cipher
    The ciphertext and the rule
    QB EIA I JZQOPB KWTL LIG QV IXZQT, IVL BPM KTWKSA EMZM ABZQSQVO BPQZBMMV.
    A Caesar key k shifts every letter forwards by the same amount:

                                            c≡m+k           (mod 26).

    To reverse encryption, subtract the key:

                                            m≡c−k           (mod 26).

    There are only 26 possible keys, so testing all of them is feasible. Here we can first obtain a promising
    key from the word structure, then check it against the entire sentence.

    Method: Step 1: use the one-letter word to generate a candidate
    The ciphertext contains the single-letter word I. In a normal English sentence, a single-letter word is
    usually A or I.
    If this ciphertext I is plaintext A, then its numerical values satisfy

                                             8≡0+k       (mod 26),

    so k = 8 is a candidate.
    If it were plaintext I instead, then 8 ≡ 8 + k, giving k = 0. But key 0 leaves the string unchanged,
    beginning QB EIA; it does not explain a normal English sentence. The candidate k = 8 is therefore
    worth testing. This is a hypothesis based on language, which the next steps confirm.

    Method: Step 2: test the opening words numerically
    For QB, subtract 8 from each letter value:

                                             Q : 16 − 8 = 8 −→ I,

                                   B : 1 − 8 = −7 ≡ 19       (mod 26) −→ T.
    Thus QB becomes IT. For EIA:

                             E : 4 − 8 = −4 ≡ 22 −→ W,           I : 8 − 8 = 0 −→ A,

                                          A : 0 − 8 = −8 ≡ 18 −→ S.
    So EIA becomes WAS. The opening QB EIA I therefore decrypts as IT WAS A, a coherent start under
    the same key.

    Method: Step 3: show one longer word in full

                                  Ciphertext         J       Z   Q    O      P    B
                                 Cipher value        9      25   16   14    15    1
                                  Subtract 8         1      17    8    6     7   −7
                               Reduce modulo 26      1      17    8    6     7   19

    Thus JZQOPB decrypts to BRIGHT. Every letter uses the same subtraction, including the wraparound
    in the last column.

    What changes from Question 1?
    A general substitution does not use one numerical shift. Instead, a permutation σ replaces each
    plaintext letter by one ciphertext letter:

                                         c = σ(m),          m = σ −1 (c).

    The same substitution is used at every occurrence. For instance, if ciphertext L means plaintext
    E, then every L must mean E. Different ciphertext letters must not be assigned the same plaintext
    letter, because the key is a bijection.
    The cipher preserves repeated-letter patterns, word lengths, repeated words and the frequency counts
    up to a relabelling of the alphabet. These are the clues to exploit. A full list of all 26! keys is
    unnecessary.

    Method: Step 1: count frequencies, then use word patterns
    There are 336 letters. The most frequent ciphertext symbols are:

                            Symbol      L    T     S   K       A    Q        I    J    P
                             Count     45   35    30   25     22    22      22   20   19

    The count for L is 45/336 ≈ 13.39%, making plaintext E a plausible hypothesis. The repeated word
    TKL could then be THE. This gives the candidate decryption assignments

                                        T 7→ T,    K 7→ H,         L 7→ E.

    These arrows are ciphertext to plaintext. A likely assignment must be tested in other words.
    Frequency alone does not prove it: the most frequent plaintext letter can vary between passages.

    Method: Step 2: combine several small clues
    Here is one consistent route through the guesses; it is not the only possible order of discovery.
    1. The single-letter word A plausibly represents I, so set A 7→ I.
    2. The repeated SIR plausibly represents AND, giving S 7→ A, I 7→ N, R 7→ D.
    3. The opening A SG now reads I A?. Taking SG=AM gives G 7→ M .
    4. The word SFF becomes A??; the repeated final letter and context suggest ALL, giving F 7→ L.
    Together these clues begin the sentence “I AM ... WITH ALL ...” rather than relying on a single
    guessed word.

  Why a huge key space did not protect this passage
  A substitution key can be any of 26! permutations, but this cipher does not destroy the language’s
  structure. Repeated plaintext letters remain repeated ciphertext letters; THE is always encrypted
  the same way; and the frequency distribution survives with new labels. This allows the key to be
  built incrementally from constraints, instead of checking all permutations.
  Each new assignment reduces uncertainty in many other words at once. If a guess causes one
  ciphertext letter to mean two different plaintext letters, or makes the passage inconsistent, reject it
  and try another. The successful key passes all those global checks.

  Other pairs give β12 = 15 and β23 = 9. Their congruences imply

                          k1 − k3 = (k1 − k2 ) + (k2 − k3 ) ≡ 15 + 9 ≡ 24              (mod 26).

  Thus 24 is the consistent choice for β13 . Choosing every local maximum blindly would give incom-
  patible equations. This is why the notes emphasise flexibility and checking the full system.

  Method: Step 6: solve the relative-shift equations
  Use the mutually consistent relations

                   k1 − k2 ≡ 15,      k2 − k3 ≡ 9,       k1 − k4 ≡ 7,      k1 − k5 ≡ 23     (mod 26).

  Put k1 = t. Rearranging one equation at a time gives

                                                       k2 ≡ t − 15,

                                                 k3 ≡ k2 − 9 ≡ t − 24,
                                             k4 ≡ t − 7,          k5 ≡ t − 23.
  Therefore the 26 possible keys have the form

                        (k1 , k2 , k3 , k4 , k5 ) ≡ (t, t − 15, t − 24, t − 7, t − 23)   (mod 26).

  This is a major reduction from 265 = 11881376 arbitrary length-five keys to only 26 common-shift
  candidates.

                    (8, 19, 22, 0, 18) + (15, 0, 17, 8, 18) ≡ (23, 19, 13, 8, 10)      (mod 26),

  Method: E. How the numerical checks can be reproduced
  The calculations used simple counting and modular arithmetic, not an unexplained automatic cipher
  solver. The following procedures describe the computation completely.
  1. Normalise: keep uppercase A–Z letters; remove whitespace and punctuation for counting. The
     lengths must be 58, 336 and 174 for Questions 1, 2 and 3.
  2. Caesar check: for each plaintext letter value mi , calculate (mi + 8) mod 26. Compare the
     resulting 58 letters to the supplied ciphertext.
  3. Substitution check: index the encryption alphabet SHERLOCKABDFGIJMNPQTUVWXYZ by plaintext
     letter value. Compare its 336 output letters to the supplied ciphertext. Confirm that all 26 entries
     in the alphabet are distinct.
  4. IoC: for trial length n ∈ {3, 4, 5, 6}, place ciphertext position i in column 1 + ((i − 1) mod n).
     Count letters and use the displayed IoC formula.
  5. Mutual IoC: for each of the ten pairs of five-column strings and every β = 0, . . . , 25, use the
     shifted-count formula in Appendix B. Keep plausible alternatives and solve a consistent system of
     key-difference congruences.
  6. Vigenere check: use repeated key values (15, 0, 17, 8, 18), add them to the recovered plaintext
     modulo 26, and compare all 174 letters to the supplied ciphertext.
  All three full-ciphertext comparisons pass. These checks validate the arithmetic, while the explana-
  tions in the main sections show why the keys were reasonable candidates in the first place.
