Question 1
Using Alice’s public key (N, e) = (299, 5), Bob encrypts m = 10 by computing

                       c ≡ me ≡ 105 ≡ 100 000 ≡ 134         (mod 299),

since 100 000 = 334 · 299 + 134. Thus Bob sends the ciphertext c = 134.
To find Alice’s private exponent, factorise

                                       299 = 13 · 23.

Both factors are prime, so

                       φ(299) = (13 − 1)(23 − 1) = 12 · 22 = 264.

The private exponent d satisfies 5d ≡ 1 (mod 264). Euclid’s algorithm gives

                             264 = 52 · 5 + 4,       5 = 1 · 4 + 1.

Back-substitution yields

                           1 = 5 − (264 − 52 · 5) = 53 · 5 − 264.

Hence Alice may take d = 53.
To decrypt, Alice computes 13453 modulo 299. Repeated squaring gives

                             j        1 2    4 8 16 32
                             j
                        134 mod 299 134 16 256 55 35 29

Since 53 = 32 + 16 + 4 + 1, it follows that

                             13453 ≡ 29 · 35 · 256 · 134
                                   ≡ 118 · 256 · 134
                                   ≡ 9 · 134 ≡ 10      (mod 299).

Therefore Alice recovers the original message m = 10.

Question 3
Fix a member of staff and write their public key as (Ni , ei ). Let

                                     ti = ⌊log10 (Ni )⌋.

Since every message is a positive integer with at most 30 digits,

                                 1 ≤ m ≤ 1030 − 1 < 1030 .

The public exponent is positive and satisfies ei ≤ ti /30. Therefore

                                 mei < 1030ei ≤ 10ti ≤ Ni .

Thus mei is already strictly smaller than the modulus. Reduction modulo Ni does not
change it, so the ciphertext, represented in {0, 1, . . . , Ni − 1}, is the ordinary integer

                                          c = mei .

An attacker who obtains the ciphertext and public exponent can therefore recover the
original message by taking the exact integer root
                                             √
                                         m = ei c.

This can be computed efficiently, for example by binary search on 1 ≤ m < 1030 , comparing
candidate powers with c. The map x 7→ xei is strictly increasing on the positive integers,
so the positive integer root is unique.
The same argument applies to every message encrypted under every staff member’s public
key. No factorisation of Ni or knowledge of the private exponent is required. The database
could therefore be recovered because the exponent bound ensures that encryption never
wraps around the modulus. The security of the prime factors does not prevent this direct
integer-root attack.
