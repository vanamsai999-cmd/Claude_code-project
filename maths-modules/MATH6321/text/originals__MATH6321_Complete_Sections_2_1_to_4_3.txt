Mathematical tools



Extended Euclid: see every back-substitution
  What the algorithm is trying to produce
  To invert a (mod n), find integers u, v with ua + vn = 1. Reducing this integer identity
  modulo n leaves ua ≡ 1, making u the inverse. Euclid finds the gcd; back-substitution
  converts the remainder calculations into the required identity.
  The inverse exists exactly when the final nonzero remainder is 1. This is the reason an
  unsuccessful inverse calculation should stop at a gcd greater than 1, rather than produce an
  ordinary fraction.

  Worked example: Find 17 inverse modulo 43
  Divide repeatedly:
                            43 = 2 · 17 + 9,   17 = 9 + 8,   9 = 8 + 1.
  Now reverse the chain:
                             1=9−8
                              = 9 − (17 − 9) = 2 · 9 − 17
                              = 2(43 − 2 · 17) − 17 = 2 · 43 − 5 · 17.

  Thus 17−1 ≡ −5 ≡ 38 (mod 43). Check 17 · 38 = 646 = 15 · 43 + 1. The negative coefficient
  is entirely acceptable; converting it to a standard residue is the final step.

  Method: Solve a nonunit linear congruence
  For 6x ≡ 8 (mod 14), the gcd is 2 and divides 8. Divide the coefficient, right side and
  modulus to get 3x ≡ 4 (mod 7). Since 3−1 = 5 (mod 7), x ≡ 20 ≡ 6 (mod 7). Lifting to
  residues modulo 14 gives x = 6, 13. Both work: 36 ≡ 8 and 78 ≡ 8 (mod 14).

  Exam-style question Practice
  Find 9−1 (mod 26) and explain how you checked it.

  Model answer Practice
  Since 3 · 9 = 27 ≡ 1 (mod 26), the inverse is 3. This short observation is a valid computation;
  extended Euclid is a systematic method when the inverse is not immediately visible. The
  multiplication check is still worth writing.

Mathematical tools



Repeated squaring, Euler and exponent cycles
  Calculate powers without writing enormous integers
  Repeated squaring uses a2r = (ar )2 , reducing modulo the chosen modulus at every step. A
  binary expansion of the exponent then tells you which squared powers to multiply. This
  requires a number of squarings proportional to the number of binary digits of the exponent,
  rather than one multiplication for every unit of the exponent.
  Euler’s theorem says aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) when gcd(a, n) = 1. For a prime p, φ(p) = p − 1,
  giving Fermat’s little theorem. The coprimality hypothesis matters: 24 ≡ 0 (mod 8), not 1,
  although φ(8) = 4.

  Worked example: Compute 7 to the power 13 modulo 23
  Since 13 = 8 + 4 + 1, calculate

                            72 ≡ 3,   74 ≡ 9,   78 ≡ 12   (mod 23).

  Therefore 713 ≡ 12 · 9 · 7 = 756 ≡ 20 (mod 23). Each squared remainder represents exactly
  the same residue as the corresponding enormous power.
  We can also simplify exponents when their cycle is known. For nonzero a (mod 23), a22 = 1,
  so a57 = a13 , because 57 = 2 · 22 + 13. This exponent reduction uses modulo 22; the final
  group element still uses modulo 23.

  Exam tip: A negative exponent means an inverse
  If a is a unit, a−r = (a−1 )r . For an element of known order q, it also equals aq−r after
  reducing the exponent modulo q. Without invertibility the negative power is not defined in
  the unit-group calculation.

  Exam-style question Practice
  Compute 310 (mod 17) by repeated squaring.

  Model answer Practice
 32 = 9, 34 ≡ 81 ≡ 13, 38 ≡ 169 ≡ 16 (mod 17). Since 10 = 8 + 2, 310 ≡ 16 · 9 = 144 ≡ 8
 (mod 17).

Chapter 2 | Mechanical cryptography



Enigma components and counting | explanation
  A changing substitution with a reversible path
  A keypress sends a signal through the plugboard, the rotors, the reflector, then back through
  the inverse rotor path and the plugboard. The plugboard swaps disjoint pairs. Rotors
  permute letters; stepping changes the effective permutation from one position to another.
  The reflector pairs all letters and sends the signal backwards. With identical initial settings,
  the machine decrypts by the same process.
  For a fixed rotor state, if the forward permutation is R, reflector U , and plugboard P = P −1 ,
  the overall permutation is P R−1 U RP (rightmost map acts first). Since U 2 = I, the overall
  map is an involution. If U has no fixed points, no letter encrypts to itself. These structural
  restrictions helped attacks, despite the huge key space.
  The module uses a simplified stepping/counting model. Answer using the assumptions stated
  in the question rather than adding historical ring/notch choices not requested.

  Method: Count independent choices without overcounting
  For L letters and exactly m disjoint plugboard pairs,
                                                 !
                              L!          L
                                   m
                                       =    (2m − 1)(2m − 3) · · · 1.
                        (L − 2m)! 2 m!   2m

  Choose the paired letters, then pair them: divide by 2 for the order within each pair and
  by m! for the order of the pairs. If the number of pairs is optional, sum over permitted m,
  including m = 0 if allowed.
  Choose r ordered distinct rotors from R: R!/(R − r)!. If each has L starting positions,
  multiply by Lr . A fully variable paired reflector on even L has L!/(2L/2 (L/2)!) wirings; a
  choice of h fixed reflectors contributes only h. Multiply independent factors.

  Separate the device from the counting assumptions
  An Enigma key specifies machine settings, whereas a message position specifies how far the
  rotors have advanced since those settings were established. Therefore two identical letters at
  different positions need not have the same ciphertext. The effective permutation changes
  even though the initial secret key has not changed.
  The reflector is more than an extra random substitution. It makes the electrical path return
  through the inverse rotor mappings. If the forward path sends a letter to one contact,
  the reflector pairs that contact with another and the reverse path returns to a new letter.
  Applying the complete fixed-state map twice cancels the path and reflector pairing, so it is
  its own inverse. Sender and receiver can therefore use the same initial settings for encryption
  and decryption.
  For counting, write down exactly which choices are independent. A choice of three already
  manufactured rotors does not include a choice of their internal wiring. Starting positions are
  additional choices only when the model treats the fixed wiring separately. The simplified
  model in the notes is the one to use unless the question explicitly specifies another. A
  defensible product of labelled factors is better than an unexplained huge integer.

Section 2.1: the full counting model and daily keys
  History and the operational model
  The notes introduce Arthur Scherbius’s 1918 machine as a device for commercial, diplomatic
  and military communication, then focus on its wartime use. The user selects a secret setting,
  types the plaintext and records the illuminated letters. Resetting an identical machine to
  the same starting setting lets the receiver type the ciphertext to recover plaintext.
  In the simplified stepping model, the right rotor advances every keypress, the middle advances
  after 26 right-rotor steps, and the left advances after 26 middle-rotor steps. The rotor wirings
  stay fixed; rotation changes the induced substitution.
  The attack section assumes the fixed wirings are known. The notes credit Marian Rejewski
  and other Polish cryptographers, using intelligence from the French, for foundational work
  recovering those wirings through group theory. A known-plaintext attack also needs a correct
  crib; structural weaknesses do not automatically reveal every message.
  Daily codebooks supplied new settings. An attack therefore had to be fast enough to exploit
  that day’s messages. The key is the initial configuration; the position-dependent permutation
  σi then gives ci = σi (mi ) and mi = σi−1 (ci ).

Conjugation explains a structural restriction
  The rotor path is a relabelling around the reflector
  Let P be the plugboard permutation, Ri the combined forward rotor map at position i, and
  S the reflector. The full fixed-state map is

                                         σi = P −1 Ri−1 SRi P.

  Putting Hi = Ri P , this becomes σi = Hi−1 SHi , a conjugate of S. Conjugation means:
  relabel the input, apply the original map, then undo the relabelling.

  Worked example: Cycle lengths survive a relabelling
  If g cycles a1 → a2 → · · · → ar → a1 , then h−1 gh cycles h−1 (a1 ) → · · · → h−1 (ar ) → h−1 (a1 ).
  The letters change, but the length remains r.
  In Example 2.9, g = (1 4)(2 3 5), h = (1 2 3 4 5). Relabelling by h−1 gives (5 3)(1 2 4),
  equivalently (1 2 4)(3 5). The cycle type remains one 2-cycle and one 3-cycle.

  Method: Why Enigma cannot encrypt a letter to itself
  The reflector is a product of 13 disjoint 2-cycles and has no fixed point. A conjugate has the
  same cycle type, so neither does σi . Also S 2 = I gives σi2 = Hi−1 S 2 Hi = I, explaining why
  the same fixed-state map reverses itself.

  Exam-style question Check your understanding
  If h−1 gh(x) = x, what does this imply about g?

  Model answer Check your understanding
  Multiply by h: g(h(x)) = h(x). Thus h(x) is a fixed point of g. A fixed-point-free permutation
  therefore cannot acquire a fixed point by conjugation.

Theorem 2.8: why conjugation preserves cycle type
  Conjugation relabels the same cycles
  Suppose g has a cycle (a1 a2 · · · ar ) and let u = h−1 gh. Then

                              u(h−1 (aj )) = h−1 (g(aj )) = h−1 (aj+1 ),

  with the index returning to 1 at the end. Hence the corresponding cycle of u is

                                 (h−1 (a1 ) h−1 (a2 ) · · · h−1 (ar )).

  Every cycle is merely relabelled, so all cycle lengths are unchanged. In particular the number
  of fixed points and the property of being an involution are unchanged.

  Worked example: Example 2.9, without multiplying three permutations blindly
  For g = (14)(235) and h = (12345), we have

                 h−1 (1) = 5, h−1 (4) = 3, h−1 (2) = 1, h−1 (3) = 2, h−1 (5) = 4.

  Relabel each cycle of g:
                                 h−1 gh = (53)(124) = (124)(35).
  Its cycle lengths are still 2 and 3. For Enigma, the reflector consists of thirteen 2-cycles, so
  every fixed-position encryption permutation has thirteen 2-cycles and no fixed point.

  Exam-style question Conjugation practice
  If g = (123)(45), what cycle lengths can h−1 gh have? Can it fix a symbol in this five-symbol
  alphabet?

  Model answer Conjugation practice
  Its cycle lengths are exactly 3 and 2. All five symbols occur in these cycles, so it has no
  fixed point. The labels may change but the lengths cannot.

A crib is a possible known-plaintext fragment
  Try positions, then reject impossible alignments
  A crib is a word or phrase thought to occur in the plaintext. Slide it along the ciphertext.
  At each placement, compare the aligned letters. If any letter would have encrypted to itself,
  reject that placement immediately, since Enigma’s fixed-state map has no fixed points.
  An alignment with no equal letters is merely not yet ruled out. It is not automatically the
  correct plaintext or rotor setting. Subsequent constraints are still needed.

  Worked example: The CRYPTOGRAPHY alignment idea
  The notes compare CRYPTOGRAPHY against a ciphertext fragment beginning XMRPTU.... At
  the first placement, the fourth plaintext letter P aligns with ciphertext P, and the fifth T
  aligns with T. Either coincidence rejects that placement.
  Shifting the crib changes every pairwise alignment. Repeat the same test at the new position
  rather than transferring the previous rejection to it. You are eliminating possible placements,
  not proving that the crib word cannot occur anywhere.

  Method: Separate the plugboard from guessed rotor settings

  Write σi = P −1 Qi P , where Qi = Ri−1 SRi . The fixed rotor/reflector wirings are assumed
  known in this simplified attack; a guess at their initial configuration determines each Qi .
  The plugboard P is still unknown and accounts for many possible full keys. The aim is to
  test some rotor guesses without exhaustively trying every plugboard.

  Exam-style question Check your understanding
  Can crib CAT align with ciphertext block CAR under Enigma? Can it align with DOG?

  Model answer Check your understanding
  CAR is impossible at that alignment because C would map to C and A to A. DOG has no
  equal aligned letters, so this simple test does not reject it. That does not establish it as the
  true alignment; other machine constraints must still be checked.

Known-plaintext loops remove the plugboard
  Follow one letter around a loop of observed pairs
  In Example 2.12, the relevant pairs imply

                           σ14 (E) = A,      σ9 (A) = M,         σ7 (M ) = E.

  Thus following E through positions 14, 9 and 7 returns E:

                                          σ7 σ9 σ14 (E) = E.

  Products act rightmost first; the order is determined by the path E to A to M to E, not by
  sorting the position numbers.

  Method: Expand the conjugates and cancel adjacent maps
  Using σi = P −1 Qi P ,

                            σ7 σ9 σ14 = (P −1 Q7 P )(P −1 Q9 P )(P −1 Q14 P )
                                      = P −1 Q7 Q9 Q14 P.

  The adjacent P P −1 ’s cancel. Since this product fixes E, applying P to both sides gives

                                     Q7 Q9 Q14 (P (E)) = P (E).

  The plugboard value P (E) is unknown, but it must be a fixed point of a product determined
  by the guessed rotor configuration. If that product has no fixed point, the configuration
  cannot be correct for any plugboard.

  Worked example: Why this test saves work
  Imagine a rotor guess yields a product with no fixed letters at all. Testing millions of
  plugboards under that guess is unnecessary: whichever letter P (E) might be, it cannot
  satisfy the loop equation. The structural contradiction rejects all those full keys together.

  Exam-style question Check your understanding
  Why can we not simply set P (E) = E to test the loop?

  Model answer Check your understanding
  The plugboard might swap E with another letter, so P (E) need not be E. The correct test
  asks whether some permissible fixed letter exists; a second loop can further restrict that
  same unknown letter. Assuming E is unpaired would discard possible valid settings without
  justification.

Expected fixed points: explain the probability calculation
  A random permutation has expected one fixed point
  Let Xi be 1 if a uniform random permutation fixes letter i, otherwise 0. There are (n − 1)!
  permutations fixing a specified letter out of n! total, so Pr(Xi = 1) = 1/n. The total number
                         P
  of fixed points is X = i Xi , giving
                                          X
                                 E[X] =        E[Xi ] = n(1/n) = 1.
                                           i

  Linearity of expectation does not require the indicators for different letters to be independent.
  This supplies the missing reason each expected contribution can be added.

  Exam tip: An expectation is not a guaranteed value
  Some permutations have no fixed points; the identity has n. Saying the expectation is one
  does not mean every permutation fixes exactly one letter or that the probability of at least
  one fixed point equals one. The notes’ qualitative motivation is that a second independent
  constraint can substantially strengthen filtering.

  Method: Two independent random permutations
  For a fixed letter, the probability both permutations fix it is (1/n)2 , under independence
  between the permutations. Summing over the n letters gives expected common fixed points

                                          n(1/n2 ) = 1/n.

  For 26 letters this is 1/26. The independence is between the two permutations for a specified
  letter, not between all fixed-point events inside one permutation.

  Exam-style question Check your understanding

  tations on 26 letters?

  Model answer Check your understanding
  For each specified letter the probability all three fix it is 1/263 . Summing over 26 letters
  gives 1/262 . This is a model calculation; actual Enigma loop products are constrained and
  need not be independent uniform permutations.

A second loop and the overall filtering strategy
  The same unknown plugboard letter must satisfy both loops
  The further pairs in Example 2.14 connect E to M at position 7, M to W at 8, W to R by
  reversing position 15, and R to E at 4. The relevant maps are involutions, so traversing an
  observed pair backwards uses the same map. Combining the path gives the source condition

                                  Q4 Q−1  −1
                                      15 Q8 Q7 (P (E)) = P (E).

  Together with the first loop, P (E) must lie in the intersection of two fixed-point sets. Having
  a fixed point for each product separately is not enough if those fixed points are different.

  Method: The simplified attack in the notes
  1. Obtain a plausible crib placement using the no-self-encryption test.
  2. Guess rotor order, starting positions and reflector: 1054560 · 2 = 2109120 possibilities in
     this model.
  3. Compute the position maps Qi and loop products for that guess.
  4. Reject guesses with incompatible fixed-point constraints; repeat with more loops.
  5. For surviving rotor guesses, solve the remaining plugboard assignments and check the
     full text.
  The notes’ rough random model suggests repeated pairs of loops can reduce surviving
  possibilities by factors around 26. Such estimates explain the sieve idea; expectations of
  common fixed points alone do not prove an exact surviving fraction. Dependencies between
  actual loops also matter.

  The historical lesson within the supplied notes
  The notes attribute key mathematical groundwork on rotor wiring to Rejewski and describe
  the loop-based work and mechanised search associated with Turing’s Bombe. The takeaway
  is that structural mathematics can replace an infeasible full-key enumeration with a much
  smaller constrained search.

  Exam-style question Check your understanding
  Products have fixed sets {A, D} and {D, G}. What can P (E) be? What if the sets are
  disjoint?

  Model answer Check your understanding
  The common candidate is D, so the two constraints force P (E) = D. If their fixed-point
  sets are disjoint, the rotor guess is impossible. Both loops constrain the same letter, not two
  independent plugboard choices.

Chapter 3 | LFSRs



LFSR generation, XOR and pictures | explanation
  A small secret state produces a long deterministic stream
  An LFSR of length n has initial fill (a0 , . . . , an−1 ) ∈ Fn2 and taps (b0 , . . . , bn−1 ), with update

                                ai+n = b0 ai ⊕ b1 ai+1 ⊕ · · · ⊕ bn−1 ai+n−1 .

  The state at time i is (ai , . . . , ai+n−1 ); shift left and append the new bit. The output
  convention used here and in the notes is the stream a0 a1 a2 · · ·, including the initial fill.
  Encrypt bits by ci = mi ⊕ ai ; decrypt by the identical XOR.
  Pictures are flattened in the specified order (the paper uses left to right, top to bottom,
  0 white/1 black), XORed, then reshaped. A long period does not make a bare LFSR
  cryptographically secure: its linear structure can reveal states from enough known output.

  Method: A safe LFSR calculation table
  Write the initial bits with indices. Compute one new bit per column using the original absolute
  indices, never by guessing a visual pattern. For ai+5 = ai ⊕ ai+2 , calculate a5 = a0 ⊕ a2 , then
  a6 = a1 ⊕ a3 , and so on. Keep a row of indices, stream, ciphertext and plaintext. Count the
  required output length before stopping.

  Follow the indices, not an imagined random pattern
 The register is a moving window through an infinite sequence. At time zero it contains
 (a0 , . . . , an−1 ). One update discards the first stored bit, shifts the remaining bits, and
 appends an . The output convention used by the notes lists the sequence starting at a0 , so
 those initial stored bits count towards the requested output length.
 A tap vector is a list of coefficients in F2 , not a list of ordinary counts. Two tapped ones
 contribute 1 + 1 = 0, while three tapped ones contribute 1. Reducing the sum modulo 2
 after adding the tapped bits is equivalent to repeated XOR.
 When encrypting a picture, turn its pixels into a single long sequence before applying XOR.
 A four-by-five picture has 20 positions, not four independent messages of length five. The
 first row uses stream bits 0–4, the next uses 5–9, and so on. A receiver who restarts the
 initial fill for every row is using a different key stream, so even correct XOR arithmetic will

Encoding reference



ASCII, binary strings and the cipher are separate
  An encoding is not itself encryption
  ASCII assigns numbers to characters. For example uppercase A has decimal code 65, B has
  66, and space has 32. Writing these codes in binary merely changes their representation;
  anyone knowing the encoding can reverse it. A stream cipher subsequently combines those
  bits with a secret stream using XOR.

  Worked example: Encode a short message before masking

                               Character    Decimal      8-bit form
                               Space                32   00100000
                               A                    65   01000001
                               B                    66   01000010
                               Z                    90   01011010
                               a                    97   01100001
                               0                    48   00110000

  For message AB, concatenate 01000001 01000010. If the first eight stream bits are 10101010,
  the first ciphertext byte is 01000001 ⊕ 10101010 = 11101011. Decryption XORs that byte
  with the same stream and recovers the original A byte.

  Exam tip: Do not confuse alphabet values and ASCII values
  The classical-cipher convention A=0 uses arithmetic modulo 26 on letters. ASCII uses
  character codes such as A=65 and includes spaces, digits and punctuation. A binary LFSR
  example works modulo 2 on encoded bits. State the representation before applying an
  algorithm; switching between these systems without conversion changes the problem.

A period is a repeated whole state
  Read the definition with its indices
  The state at time i is ai = (ai , . . . , ai+n−1 ). The notes define the period as the smallest
  positive j such that
                                   ai+j = ai for some i ≥ 0.
  Once a state repeats, all later states follow the same deterministic updates. A repeated bit
  is not enough: zeros and ones recur constantly without the entire register having returned.
  The phrase “for some i” allows a transient before an eventual cycle. For general taps, the
  initial state need not return. Example: recurrence ai+2 = ai+1 , fill 10, gives states 10,00,00,...
  . Its eventual period is 1, even though the initial 10 never returns.

  Worked example: The source short-cycle register has period 3
  For length five, recurrence ai+5 = ai ⊕ ai+1 , fill 01101, the states are

                               01101 → 11011 → 10110 → 01101.

  The three displayed states are distinct, and the initial state returns after 3 updates. Thus
  the cycle length is exactly 3, not merely at most 3. Its output is repeating 011.

  Method: How to measure a period by direct iteration
  Record each complete state and its time. On the first repeat, subtract the earlier occurrence
  time from the later one. Distinct states before the repeat establish minimality of the cycle
  length. For an example whose initial state returns, this is its first return time. More advanced
  tests start on page 29 and are not needed for this direct method.

  Exam-style question Check your understanding
  Can the presence of 11 twice in the output prove period 2?

  Model answer Check your understanding
  No. The update depends on the whole register state. Two occurrences of a short bit block
  need not sit inside identical full states or be followed by identical future bits. Check the full
  state or prove a genuinely repeating output pattern under the recurrence.

The finite-state proof of the LFSR period bound
  There are only finitely many possible states
  Each of the n stored positions is 0 or 1, so there are 2n possible states. A deterministic
  sequence of states cannot remain distinct forever. Once a state repeats, its entire future
  repeats because the update rule depends only on that current state.
  The special zero state improves the general finite-state bound from 2n to 2n − 1. The proof
  separates cases so that it also covers a nonzero fill that later reaches zero.

  Method: Proposition 3.9, explained one step at a time
  1. If the initial state is zero, it stays zero, so the period is 1.
  2. If any later state is zero, every state after it is zero. The eventual period is again 1.
  3. Otherwise, every state is one of the 2n −1 nonzero vectors. Consider states a0 , a1 , . . . , a2n −1 :
     there are 2n states on this list but only 2n − 1 allowed values.
  4. By the pigeonhole principle, ar = as for some 0 ≤ r < s ≤ 2n − 1. The gap s − r is at
     most 2n − 1, and the least repeating gap is no larger.
  Thus Per(a0 ) ≤ 2n − 1 . Using r, s for time indices avoids confusing them with the register
  length n.

  Exam tip: What has not been proved here
  The argument does not say every fill has maximal period, or that every nonzero fill returns
  to itself for arbitrary taps. It proves an eventual period bound. A separate analysis is needed
  to calculate the actual period for a specified fill and recurrence. The source’s next section
  develops further tools.

  Exam-style question Check your understanding
  A length-four register is claimed to have eventual period 16. Is this compatible with
  Proposition 3.9?

  Model answer Check your understanding
  No. The bound is 24 − 1 = 15. The zero state is absorbing, so it cannot form one part of a
  cycle visiting all 16 states. A claim of period 16 signals a counting or modelling error.

Chapter 3 | LFSR periods



LFSR periods and irreducibility | explanation
  The theorems and their hypotheses
  The characteristic polynomial is

                         cb (x) = xn + bn−1 xn−1 + · · · + b1 x + b0 ∈ F2 [x].

  With the notes’ row-state convention, the companion matrix has ones below the diagonal
  and taps in its final column, and ai+1 = ai Bb .
  If b0 = 1, Bb is invertible: the period is the first return to the initial state and divides
  ord(Bb ). Every length-n LFSR is eventually periodic; the eventual cycle has length at most
  2n − 1. If b0 = 0, a transient can occur; leading untapped bits can be discarded to analyse
  the smaller recurrence.
  Main theorem: if cb is irreducible (and has nonzero constant term), every nonzero fill has
  the same period, the smallest positive divisor j | 2n − 1 for which cb | xj + 1. An irreducible
  polynomial is primitive when this order is 2n − 1. Irreducible does not automatically mean
  maximal period.

  Method: Period questions without generating a huge stream
  1. Write cb from the update rule and check its constant term.
  2. Establish irreducibility if the theorem requires it. For degree 3, excluding roots 0 and 1 is
     enough. For degree 4, also exclude division by the only irreducible quadratic x2 + x + 1.
  3. List divisors of 2n − 1 in increasing order.
  4. Reduce xj modulo cb ; test for remainder 1. The first successful j is the nonzero-fill period.
  5. Treat the zero fill separately. If reducible, use state iteration or another valid matrix-order
     argument, not the irreducible theorem.

  A state cycle is different from a repeated individual bit
  A binary output necessarily contains many repeated zeros and ones. That does not identify
  its period. We must look for a return of the whole register state, or prove a recurrence
  repeats from that point. If b0 = 1, the update can be reversed, so a repeated state forces a
  repeated initial state; there is no transient leading into a different cycle.
  There are 2n possible states. The zero state is fixed and, under an invertible update, nonzero
  states cannot enter it. This leaves at most 2n − 1 states on a nonzero cycle. But the state
  space can split into several shorter cycles, so this upper bound is not automatically attained.
  Irreducibility makes the nonzero-fill period independent of the particular fill, through the
  field F2 [x]/(cb ). Primitivity is a stronger assertion: multiplication by the class of x must
  pass through every nonzero field element. Distinguishing these two assertions is essential.
  An irreducible quartic can have period 5 even though the maximum possible nonzero cycle
  length is 15. State which theorem applies before using its conclusion.

LFSR foundations



Polynomial arithmetic over F2
  Coefficients wrap modulo 2
 In F2 [x], coefficients are 0 or 1, and addition/subtraction are the same operation. Thus
 (x2 + x + 1) + (x2 + 1) = x: the matching x2 and constant terms cancel. Polynomial
 multiplication still distributes, but repeated coefficients are reduced modulo 2.
 Reduction modulo a polynomial is a different second operation. For f = x3 + x + 1, the
 relation f = 0 in the quotient gives x3 = x + 1. Use it to replace every power of degree at
 least 3. The remainder has degree below 3, which makes equality in the quotient easy to
 check.

  Worked example: Build the order test one power at a time
  Working modulo f = x3 + x + 1,

                                       j   xj (mod f )
                                       0   1
                                       1   x
                                       2   x2
                                       3   x+1
                                       4   x2 + x
                                       5   x2 + x + 1
                                       6   x2 + 1
                                       7   1

  For example x6 = x(x2 + x + 1) = x3 + x2 + x ≡ (x + 1) + x2 + x = x2 + 1. The final step is
  x(x2 + 1) = x3 + x ≡ 1. No earlier listed positive power equals 1, so the order is 7.

  Exam tip: An integer remainder calculation will not substitute for this
  The statement f | xj + 1 concerns polynomials, not the numerical value obtained by setting
  x = 2. Work with coefficients modulo 2 and a polynomial remainder. Evaluation at 0 and 1
  only tests possible linear factors.

  Exam-style question Practice
  Reduce x5 modulo x3 + x2 + 1.

  Model answer Practice
  Here x3 = x2 +1, so x4 = x3 +x = x2 +x+1. Thus x5 = x3 +x2 +x = (x2 +1)+x2 +x = x+1.
  Every cancellation takes place modulo 2.

LFSR cryptanalysis



Recover taps from known output
  Linearity can make a long stream predictable
  If the recurrence length is known but the taps are unknown, each observed new bit gives a
  linear equation in the tap coefficients. Enough independent equations can determine those
  coefficients. Known plaintext reveals output bits because ai = mi ⊕ ci , so a cipher that
  exposes enough plaintext/ciphertext pairs may expose the stream’s generating rule.
  The number of equations is not the only issue: they must be independent. A run of zeros
  can supply only zero equations, leaving taps undetermined. Explain the actual system rather
  than claiming any arbitrary set of observed bits always determines the recurrence.

  Exam-style question H.1: a known-plaintext stream attack
  A length-three LFSR has unknown taps (b0 , b1 , b2 ) and observed output 100101. Recover its
  taps. Explain why even a maximal-period LFSR is not a true one-time pad.

  Model answer H.1
  Use ai+3 = b0 ai + b1 ai+1 + b2 ai+2 in F2 . From a0 a1 a2 = 100 and a3 = 1, get b0 = 1. From
  a1 a2 a3 = 001, a4 = 0 gives b2 = 0. From a2 a3 a4 = 010, a5 = 1 gives b1 = 1. Thus the
  recurrence is ai+3 = ai + ai+1 , characteristic polynomial x3 + x + 1, with period 7 for nonzero
  fills.
  Known plaintext and ciphertext reveal stream bits by XOR. Enough stream bits provide
  linear equations for taps and expose the fill. A maximal period describes repetition, not
  computational unpredictability. An OTP instead requires independent uniformly random
  key bits, with no short deterministic recurrence.

  Method: Check a recovered recurrence
  After solving for taps, regenerate every observed output bit, including some not needed for
  solving the equations if available. Then predict the next bit. A successful check demonstrates
  consistency with the observations; the known recurrence length is part of the assumption.

  With the recovered recurrence ai+3 = ai + ai+1 and observed 100101, predict a6 .

  Model answer Practice
  Use the bits at indices 3 and 4: a6 = a3 + a4 = 1 + 0 = 1. Do not combine the last two
  observed bits unless those are the positions specified by the taps. All arithmetic is in F2 .

Reducible examples and theorem boundaries
  Why a failed hypothesis changes the problem
  The irreducible-characteristic-polynomial theorem is powerful because the quotient is a field
  and every nonzero element can be cancelled. With a reducible polynomial the quotient can
  have zero divisors, so that cancellation reasoning no longer applies. Different fills can have
  different periods, and those periods need not divide 2n − 1.
  A direct state table remains valid when the irreducibility theorem is unavailable. If b0 = 1,
  stop at the first return to the initial state. To prove minimality, check that the preceding
  states were distinct. Merely seeing a visually repetitive pattern in some initial output bits is
  insufficient.

  Exam-style question H.2: reducible LFSRs

                             0101, 1010, 0100, 1000, 0001, 0010, 0101.

Why the main theorem works
  Replace a matrix problem by multiplication in a field
  Let f = cb be irreducible, with nonzero constant term. The quotient

                                          K = F2 [x]/⟨f ⟩

  is a field with 2n elements. Every element has a unique representative of degree less than n.
  There are n binary coefficients, hence 2n choices.
  Let α = [x]. Multiplication by α has the usual column-vector companion matrix, which is
  Bb as displayed in the notes. For the row-state update, the corresponding column update is
  BbT . A matrix and its transpose have the same characteristic and minimal polynomials; this
  preserves the relevant polynomial-order calculation. Keeping the row/column convention
  explicit avoids a misleading coordinate identification.

  Irreducibility forces one common nonzero period
  Equivalently, regard the state space as a module where x acts by the LFSR update. Cayley–
  Hamilton gives f (Bb ) = 0. Since f is irreducible of degree n, this equips the n-dimensional
  binary state space with the structure of a one-dimensional vector space over K.
  The update acts as multiplication by α. For a nonzero state represented in this field structure
  by v, returning after j steps means
                                             αj v = v.
  Since v is invertible, this is equivalent to αj = 1. Thus all nonzero fills have the same period,
  the multiplicative order of α.
  The group K × has 2n − 1 elements, so Lagrange gives j | 2n − 1. Finally,

                           αj = 1 ⇐⇒ [xj + 1] = 0 ⇐⇒ f | xj + 1.

  This is Theorem 3.16. For computations, use its five-step procedure rather than rebuilding
  the field argument.

Public-key foundations



Cyclic groups, generators and discrete logarithms
  An exponent is naturally a residue class
  The order of an element g is the least positive q such that g q = 1. Its generated subgroup
  is {1, g, . . . , g q−1 }. If g generates the whole group, it is a generator; in (Z/pZ)× it is also
  called a primitive root.
  If g k = h, then g k+tq = h for every integer t. The full set of discrete logs is therefore k + qZ,
  rather than one special integer. A standard answer chooses a representative in 0, . . . , q − 1,
  together with the modulus.

  Worked example: Compare 2 and 3 modulo 7
  Powers of 2 give 2, 4, 1, then repeat. Its order is 3, so it does not generate 3, 5, 6. The
  discrete log of 3 to base 2 does not exist in this subgroup.
  Powers of 3 give 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1. Its order is 6, so it is a generator. We read log3 5 = 5 (mod 6)
  from the fifth power. Both calculations use the same element modulus 7 but different
  exponent periods.

  Method: Test a proposed generator efficiently

  If the group has known order q, factor q. An element has order q exactly when g q/r = ̸ 1 for
  each distinct prime divisor r | q. Any proper divisor of q misses at least one of these tests.
  For order 6, check powers 3 and 2. For 3 modulo 7 these are 6 and 2, both different from 1.

  Exam-style question Practice
  What are log3 4 and log3 1 modulo 7?

  Model answer Practice
 The table gives 34 ≡ 4, so the first log is 4 (mod 6). The identity is 30 = 1, so its log is 0
 (mod 6), equivalently any multiple of 6. Do not report these exponents modulo 7.

Chapter 4 | Public-key cryptography



Diffie–Hellman key exchange | explanation
  Agreeing a secret without sending it
  Choose a public finite cyclic group G = ⟨g⟩ of order q. In the notes, a standard choice is
  G = (Z/pZ)× , q = p − 1. Alice privately chooses a, Bob privately chooses b. They exchange
  A = g a , B = g b and compute
                                     K = B a = Ab = g ab .
  An observer sees G, g, A, B. Computing a discrete log would recover a or b, hence the shared
  key. The discrete log problem asks for k with g k = h; logs are determined modulo q. DH
  itself establishes a shared value rather than encrypting a message.
  DH requires a suitable group and parameter choices. A small-order base provides only a
  small number of possible secrets. Basic unauthenticated DH is also vulnerable to an active
  intermediary who performs separate exchanges with the two parties; computational hardness
  alone does not authenticate identities.

  Method: A DH computation or explanation
  State the public group/base and private exponents, calculate both public powers by repeated
  squaring, then compute the shared value in both directions. Show that both equal g ab . For
  an insecure-key question, inspect the order of g, trivial exponents, and whether the chosen
  group makes logs easy.

  Public powers hide exponents, not the group operation
  An observer knows how to multiply and invert elements of the public group. Those operations
  are supposed to be easy. The intended difficulty is recovering an exponent from a power, or
  computing the shared power from the two public powers without the secret exponents.
  Alice can raise Bob’s public value to a because she knows a. Bob can raise Alice’s public
  value to b. Both calculations reach g ab because integer multiplication of exponents commutes.
  The attacker cannot obtain g ab merely by multiplying the public values: g a g b = g a+b , which
  has the wrong exponent.
  The discrete-log problem is sufficient to break the exchange: learning a lets the attacker
  compute B a . However, this does not prove that every way to compute the shared value
  must solve a discrete log. Keep a distinction between a useful attack and a mathematical
  equivalence of problems.
  Finally, the order of the chosen base controls the true search space. If g has order 4, then all
  powers depend only on exponents modulo 4, however many decimal digits the parties write
  in their private exponents.

Chapter 4: motivation, history and the hardness discussion
  What changes from symmetric to asymmetric cryptography?
  The earlier schemes require shared secret information before communication. Increased
  computing power makes both using and attacking ciphers easier, while digital financial,
  health and personal data increase the need for protection. The notes mention DES, 3DES,
  AES and Blowfish as examples of symmetric ciphers; they are background names here, not
  algorithms developed in these sections.
  Public-key cryptography separates public operations from secret information. Diffie–Hellman
  establishes a shared key; RSA and ElGamal let others encrypt to a recipient. The algorithms
  are public: the intended obstacle is a hard mathematical problem, rather than concealing
  the method.
  The notes date public Diffie–Hellman to 1976 and RSA to Rivest, Shamir and Adleman’s
  1977 announcement, with Clifford Cocks’s earlier GCHQ work in 1973. These historical
  points explain the shift in viewpoint, rather than adding calculations to memorise.

  The primitive-root remark and the scatter plot
  Theorem 4.2 says the nonzero residues modulo a prime form a cyclic group; Definition 4.3
  names its generators primitive roots. The notes mention the Artin-conjecture heuristic that
  2 is a primitive root for roughly 37.4% of primes. This is background motivation, not a test
  for any particular prime.
  The source plot of 6j mod 17627 for j = 1, . . . , 100 illustrates the lack of ordinary increasing
  size in modular powers. Real-number logarithm intuition therefore does not directly give
  an efficient modular logarithm method. The plot illustrates behaviour; it is not a proof of
  computational hardness.
  The notes discuss number-theoretic attacks and illustrative digit lengths for discrete logs and
  factorisation. Those sizes are course context, not a current parameter recommendation. For
  questions here, identify the group, public data, secret exponent and mathematical problem;
  keep the one-way implications precise.

Chapter 4 | RSA



RSA encryption and insecure parameter choices | explana-
tion
  The trapdoor is knowing the prime factors
 Choose distinct secret primes p, q, set N = pq and φ(N ) = (p − 1)(q − 1). Choose e coprime
 to φ(N ), then calculate d ≡ e−1 (mod φ(N )). Public key: (N, e). Private exponent: d; the
 factors must also remain secret.
 For the course’s message space m ∈ (Z/N Z)× , encrypt c ≡ me (mod N ), decrypt m ≡ cd
 (mod N ). Since ed = 1 + tφ(N ), Fermat–Euler gives med = m(mφ(N ) )t ≡ m. This explains
 correctness without being an examinable proof requirement.
 Factoring N reveals φ(N ) and d. Factoring is the course’s underlying hard problem; it is
 not a proved equivalence between every RSA attack and factorisation. These are textbook
 examples; deterministic unpadded RSA has additional weaknesses.

  Method: RSA question checklist
  1. Compute N and φ(N ).
  2. Check gcd(e, φ(N )) = 1. If not, no private exponent exists by the stated construction.
  3. Use extended Euclid to find d; verify ed ≡ 1.
  4. Encrypt modulo N using repeated squaring.
  5. Demonstrate decryption by an explicit power calculation, not only by quoting correctness.

  RSA uses two very different kinds of modular arithmetic
  The modulus N specifies where messages and ciphertexts live. The totient φ(N ) describes
  the exponent cycle on the unit group. Consequently the equation defining the private
  exponent is ed ≡ 1 (mod φ(N )), while the actual message operations are computed modulo
  N.
  The public exponent is chosen to make exponentiation reversible on that group. If e shares
  a factor with φ(N ), the standard inverse construction fails; choosing a large-looking e does
  not repair the missing coprimality. Conversely, any representative d + tφ(N ) produces the
  same power map on units, so the private exponent is naturally an equivalence class modulo
  the totient.
  Factoring N exposes p, q, hence the totient and the inverse exponent. This explains why the
  factors are valuable trapdoor information. It does not mean that RSA hides the algorithms
  or that no other attack could exist. In small exam examples, trial division might be easy;
  you are demonstrating how the construction works, not asserting that these toy numbers
  provide real security. Always show a decryption calculation if the question asks you to
  demonstrate recovery.

  Units, the totient and Fermat–Euler
  A unit modulo N is a residue a with gcd(a, N ) = 1. Precisely these residues have multiplica-

  deterministic map, fresh s can give different ciphertexts for the same message. If s is reused,
  c2 (c′2 )−1 = m(m′ )−1 : the common mask cancels.
  A discrete log of public h recovers k, hence breaks the scheme. The underlying group must
  make the relevant exponent problems difficult; cyclicity alone is not enough.

  Method: El-Gamal decryption

  Compute ck1 , find its inverse in the group, and multiply it by c2 . In (Z/pZ)× , the group
  operations are multiplication and modular inversion. Do not subtract the mask: that would
  be an additive-group protocol.

  El-Gamal is a group-valued masking operation
  The factor hs is a temporary mask. The sender can calculate it using the public h and the
  chosen secret nonce s. The recipient can reconstruct exactly the same mask as ck1 , using the
  transmitted c1 = g s and private k. These agree because both are g ks .
  The ciphertext therefore has two components for a reason. The first lets the recipient rebuild
  the mask; the second contains the message multiplied by that mask. Dropping the first
  component would usually make legitimate decryption impossible. Sending the nonce itself
  would make decryption easy for everyone, since the public h would reveal hs .
  Multiplication is the group operation here, so remove the mask by multiplying by its inverse.
  In the usual prime-modulus examples this means a modular inverse, not ordinary fraction
  arithmetic. You can compute ck1 first and invert its result, or compute c−k
                                                                           1 directly by a valid
  equivalent exponent. The two approaches are mathematically identical.
  The nonce is not the long-term private key. It is a fresh per-encryption choice; disclosing it
  can expose that ciphertext even when the long-term key is still unknown. Reuse can expose
  relationships between messages without giving the key outright.

ElGamal in other cyclic groups
  Definitions 4.15–4.17: the operation controls the notation

  In a cyclic group G = ⟨g⟩ of order q, a discrete log solves g k = h. All solutions are exactly
  k + qZ: g k+tq = g k , and equality of two powers implies their exponent difference is a multiple
  of the order.
  In an additive group, g k in multiplicative-style notation means kg. In Z/10Z, g = 7 and

The end of Section 4.3: representations and elliptic curves
  Why abstractly identical groups can differ computationally
  Every cyclic group of order q is isomorphic to Z/qZ. But this does not make discrete logs
  easy in every representation. If you could efficiently convert an arbitrary element h = g k
  into its additive exponent coordinate k, that conversion would itself solve the discrete-log
  problem.
  In the additive representation Z/N Z, the input h is already an ordinary residue and recovering
  k from kg = h is just modular division. In a multiplicative representation, the exponent is
  not supplied as a coordinate.

  The final paragraph: elliptic-curve groups
  The notes also mention E(Fp ), the group of points of an elliptic curve over a finite field. Its
  operation is a geometrically defined point addition, and the analogue of exponentiation is
  scalar multiplication kP .
  The discrete-log question becomes: given P and Q = kP , recover k. Cryptographic
  constructions usually choose a suitable cyclic subgroup generated by P . This connects
  to Remark 4.23’s idea of obtaining a cyclic group by choosing a generator and using its
  generated subgroup.
  The notes list ECDH, ECDSA and Bitcoin as applications. Elliptic-curve formulas and digital
  signatures are not developed in Sections 2.1–4.3; understanding their mention here requires
  only recognising another group representation. Section 4.4 begins after this paragraph and
  is outside the requested scope.

2     Mechanical Cryptography: An industrial revolution in
      security

The ciphers discussed so far are all examples of historical ciphers, dating from Ancient Rome
through to the early 1900’s. This was by no means a complete list...there were many other ciphers
in use during this time. However, all suffered from similar issues:


    1. Encryption/decryption was often a lengthy process, since it had to be done by hand.

    2. Because of this, ciphers had to be simple enough in design so that a human could understand
       and use them.

    3. This meant that most ciphers had underlying weaknesses due to underlying language patterns.
       Statistical attacks rendered these ciphers insecure for large message lengths.


Around the turn of the 20th Century, the Second Industrial Revolution began...bringing with it
an age of mechanical discovery. We had now started to harvest electricity and use it to build
machines that could perform complicated tasks way more efficiently than humans. Around this
time, cryptographers wondered whether machines could be used for encryption/decryption, in such
a way that would solve all of the above issues.

In this section we’ll study one of the most famous examples of such a machine, the Enigma machine,
and learn about one of the most famous feats in classical cryptography, Turing’s attack on the
Enigma cipher.


2.1     The Enigma cipher

The Enigma machine is perhaps one of the most famous cipher machines in history. Designed
by German engineer Arthur Scherbius in 1918, the machine was designed to provide widespread
security for commercial, diplomatic and military communications. However, the most well-known
use of the Enigma machine was by the Nazi’s during World War II.

Let’s take a look at the machine in detail. In this course we’ll study a slightly simplified version,
but the real machine wasn’t too different from this.




                                                 16
To use the machine, you would:


    First set up the machine according to a secret choice of machine settings (this is your key).

    Encrypt by typing the plaintext message using the keyboard and recording the corresponding
     ciphertext letters that light up on the display.

    Decrypt by setting up the machine using the identical key settings, typing the ciphertext
     message using the keyboard and recording the corresponding plaintext letters that light up

This is much easier than the previous ciphers we have seen...you didn’t even need to know how the

How does the machine work though? Let’s open the hood and have a look. The inside of the
machine consisted of many electrical wires. Each time a button is pressed on the keyboard it
completes a circuit, hence lighting up the corresponding ciphertext letter. The ciphery goodness
happens in the middle.

2.2    Attacking Enigma

Given the complicated nature of the Enigma cipher, we might believe that it’s completely secure.
Surprisingly, it isn’t...although it isn’t completely insecure! (The common misconception is that
Enigma is “completely broken”... but this isn’t true, there are still encrypted Enigma messages
that we have failed to decipher!).

In this section we’ll see roughly how a known plaintext attack works. The full story would
easily take a full semester of lectures, since this was the product of many years of research by many
different groups of mathematicians/cryptographers.

We will make the assumption that we are dealing with the standard German usage of the Enigma
machine...so that there are exactly 10 plugs in the plugboard, a choice of 3 from 5 rotors of fixed
wiring (that we know) and a choice of two reflectors of fixed wiring (that we know).
Remark 2.6. We take it for granted that we know the fixed rotor/reflector wirings used by the
Germans, but these had to be figured out the hard way. Polish mathematician Marian Rejewski
was able to do this using intel from the French...not by using statistical tools but by using results
in group theory. This was the dawn of the usefulness of Pure Mathematics in Cryptography.




Later he was joined by other Polish cryptographers to launch a full scale attack on Enigma. Re-
jewski’s achievement of understanding the internal wirings used by the Germans is often described
as one of the major feats of 20th Century Cryptography.

                                                   20
First, let’s model the Enigma machine mathematically. Before the ith key press the machine will

Recall that when a keyboard button is pressed, the signal first travels through the plugboard. Let
P ∈ S26 be the fixed permutation corresponding to the plugs inserted.

S ∈ S26 .

permutations we see at this stage are the inverses of the permutations going forward.

Corollary 2.10. The Enigma cipher cannot encrypt a letter to itself.

Proof. Note that S swaps all of the letters A-Z in pairs, and so the cycle type of S ∈ S26 is
(2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2). It then follows that σi ∈ S26 also has this cycle type, since it’s
visibly a conjugate of S.

No such permutation can have a fixed point, and so the substitution key given by σi cannot send
a letter to itself.

to locate the position of known plaintext.

possibilities for the initial rotor settings and the choice of reflector). By today’s standards, this
brute force would be possible in seconds...but this was highly non-trivial back in the 1940’s. One
of Turing’s crowning achievements was to design and build a spectacular machine, the Turing
Bombe, that was able to perform the attack described above.

3     Linear Feedback Shift Registers

We saw earlier that the One Time Pad is a provably secure cipher. However, it suffered from some
disadvantages in that we needed to generate a long key uniformly at random. This is not only hard
to do, but might be quite time consuming.

We wonder whether there is a way to compromise a little and generate a pseudorandom key, i.e.
a key that is random enough for you to use as a One Time Pad and get away with it. We also
wonder whether we can do this without too much effort...maybe requiring only a small input to
generate our long random output.

Linear Feedback Shift Registers (LFSR’s) give a simple way to do both of these. In this course,
we’ll only consider LFSR’s over F2 , the finite field of order 2, for simplicity (there are much more
general notions of LFSR over other fields/rings, e.g. Fp for other primes p and Z/N Z for other
integers N ≥ 2).


3.1     The basics

Definition 3.1. Let a, b ∈ F2 . Then the XOR of a and b, denoted a ⊕ b is given by the following
table:

                                                 a\b    0   1
                                                  0     0   1
                                                  1     1   0


The term XOR stands for Exclusive OR, since this is almost the logical operation OR (but it
excludes BOTH). Note that this is simply addition mod 2, but we adopt the notation since it’s a
more natural notation from the point of view of Computer Science.

Definition 3.2. A Linear Feedback Shift Register of length n ≥ 1 with taps b = (b0 , b1 , ..., bn−1 ) ∈
Fn2 is a device that stores a state ai ∈ Fn2 at each (integer) time step i ≥ 0 and updates over time
as follows:
                    ai = (ai , ai+1 , ..., ai+n−1 ) ↦−→ ai+1 = (ai+1 , ai+2 , ..., ai+n ),
where ai+n = b0 ai ⊕ b1 ai+1 ⊕ ... ⊕ bn−1 ai+n−1 .

The starting string a0 = (a0 , a1 , ..., an−1 ) ∈ Fn2 is called the initial fill.

In other words, at each time step the current state shifts to the left, removing the leftmost entry
and updating the rightmost entry with a new bit (depending recursively on the old state).

We’ll often draw diagrams to illustrate LFSR’s as follows:

Example 3.3. The LFSR of length n = 6 with taps b = (b0 , b1 , b2 , b3 , b4 , b5 ) = (1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0) has
update rule:
                                ai+6 = ai ⊕ ai+2 ⊕ ai+3 .

                                           a0        0        1        1        0        1        0
                                           a1        1        1        0        1        0        1
                                           a2        1        0        1        0        1        0
                                           a3        0        1        0        1        0        0
                                            ..       ..       ..       ..       ..       ..       ..

                                                 s = a0 a1 a2 ...an ...

which can be used as a One Time Pad key (if we convert our plaintext message into bits using a
standard method, such as ASCII (see Appendix 8.3)).

Example 3.4. The sequence of bits we get from the LFSR in the previous example is:

We could also use the bit string to encrypt pictures made up of black and white pixels (in the same
way that we saw in the One Time Pad chapter).

It’s now clear why LFSR’s are useful in Cryptography, we can generate long “random-looking”
One Time Pad keys by using small initial fills (which must be kept secret). This has the obvious
advantage that the key is easier to communicate with someone...they would only need to know the
initial fill and the update rule used to be able to generate s.

The only downside is that the key isn’t really random...it was generated using a recursion, so is
deterministic. LFSR’s can only generate pseudorandom strings, and some LFSR’s are better
than others in this regard!

So now we ask the natural question, is it possible to look at an LFSR and predict its quality?
How “pseudorandom” can its output strings be, based on the taps and the initial fill?

Clearly this is a vague question...how are we quantifying pseudorandomness? A good way to
measure this is by how often the string s repeats.

Definition 3.7. Consider an LFSR with initial fill a0 . Then the period of a0 is the smallest
j ≥ 1 such that ai+j = ai for some i ≥ 0 (i.e. the smallest number of time steps needed to observe
a repeat between two states of the LFSR).

We denote this by Per(a0 ) = j.

Example 3.8. In the previous example we considered two LFSR’s of length 5 and the same initial
fill a0 = (0, 1, 1, 0, 1).

The second clearly satisfies Per(a0 ) ≤ 3, since ai+3 = ai for every i ≥ 0. Since we see visibly that
ai+1 ̸= ai and ai+2 ̸= ai for all i ≥ 0, we have that Per(a0 ) = 3.

For the first LFSR we note that the computation above didn’t reveal any repeats, so we must compute
further:
                         s = 011011101010000100101100111110001101...

A quick count reveals that a31 = a0 . This implies that ai+31 = ai for every i ≥ 0 (Why?), and so
Per(a0 ) ≤ 31. We must have equality since if ai+j = ai for some 1 ≤ j ≤ 30 and i ≥ 0, then by
periodicity we would have this equality for some 0 ≤ i ≤ 30 (and visibly there are no repeats in the
states a0 , a1 , ..., a30 ).

Thus Per(a0 ) = 31. (Later, we’ll see why for this LFSR the period is always 31 for any non-zero
initial fill).

Ok, so now that we have a way of quantifying the pseudorandomness of an LFSR, two questions
remain:


    How do we compute Per(a0 )? We’ll see a partial answer to this question soon!

    Does Per(a0 ) actually exist? Is it always the case that the output string of an LFSR always
     repeats from some point onwards?

Proposition 3.9. An LFSR of length n satisfies Per(a0 ) ≤ 2n − 1 for every initial fill a0 .

Proof. Suppose that a0 = (0, 0, ..., 0). Then Per(a0 ) = 1 ≤ 2n − 1 (see exercises).

Now suppose that a0 ̸= (0, 0, ..., 0). If at some time step we have ai = (0, 0, ..., 0) then the above
tells us that Per(a0 ) = 1 ≤ 2n − 1. So we may assume that ai ̸= (0, 0, ..., 0) for any i ≥ 0.

There are 2n −1 elements of Fn2 \{(0, 0, ..., 0)}. Now consider the states a0 , a1 , ..., a2n −1 of the LFSR.
There are 2n such states, and so by the Pigeonhole principle we must have that an = am for some
0 ≤ m < n ≤ 2n − 1. It follows that Per(a0 ) ≤ n − m ≤ 2n − 1.

Proposition 3.13. Consider an LFSR with taps b and initial fill a0 . Suppose b0 = 1.

  1. We have that Per(a0 ) | ord(Bb ) (i.e. the smallest k ∈ N such that Bbk = I).

  2. We have that Per(a0 ) is the smallest j such that a0 = aj (i.e. to find the period it’s enough
     to just look for the first repeat of the initial fill a0 ).

Proof.    1. Suppose that k = ord(Bb ). Then Bbk = I and so since:

                         ai+k = ai+k−1 Bb = ai+k−2 Bb2 = ... = ai Bbk = ai I = ai ,

     we have that ai+k = ai for any i ≥ 0. It follows that Per(a0 ) ≤ k = ord(Bb ).
     To get divisibility, suppose that Per(a0 ) = j ≤ k. Choose i ≥ 0 such that ai+j = ai . Writing
     k = qj + r for some q ≥ 1 and 0 ≤ r < j we see that:

                                             ai = ai+j = ... = ai+qj .

     But we also know that ai = ai+k , so that ai+qj = ai+k . If r ̸= 0 then this implies that
     Per(a0 ) ≤ r < j, which is a contradiction. Thus r = 0, so that j|k.

  2. Suppose that i ≥ 0 and j ≥ 1 are such that ai+j = ai . Then a0 Bbi+j = a0 Bbi . Note that
     det(Bb ) = b0 = 1, and so Bb ∈ GLn (F2 ) (i.e. is invertible mod 2). Cancelling Bbi from both
     sides of the equation gives a0 Bbj = a0 , i.e. aj = a0 . It follows that any repetition in the
     sequence a0 , a1 , a2 , ... descends to a repetition with the starting term a0 , and so the period
     is the smallest j such that aj = a0 .

4     Public Key Cryptography: Asymmetric security in the
      digital era

We saw earlier that the invention of computational machines provided a whole host of new capa-
bilities in Cryptography. Such machines could be used to both implement and break ciphers in
ways that we could only dream of before.

As time went on the performance of these machines rapidly increased. They also became more
readily available on a commercial scale (almost everyone has access to a computer now!). As such,
the Digital Era (that we currently live in) has brought with it new challenges:


     In a data-driven society it has become more important than ever to find ways of keeping
      personal data secure (e.g. financial data, healthcare data). This is very much a different
      viewpoint than the “cloak and dagger” style Cryptography that we’ve seen previously...in
      the modern world anyone’s personal data can be a target!

     Cryptography has had to keep up with technological advances! The security of a scheme
      now depends how how good the current capabilities are (e.g. can I simply invest a billion
      pounds into building a good enough supercomputer to break someone’s security).


Over the last 50-60 years there have been many new and wonderful ciphers developed to cope
with the rise in demand (e.g. DES, 3DES, AES, Blowfish). These new schemes are much more
complicated to understand by humans, but are designed to be easily carried out by computers (e.g.
iterating intricate sequences of non-linear operations many times).

Alongside these developments, a revolution happened in Cryptography in the 1970’s:


     It was discovered that we could use ideas from Pure Mathematics to provide our security.

     We could also use ideas from Pure Mathematics to communicate without needing a
      shared key.


The main idea is to shift focus. Up to this point our ciphers have been symmetric, i.e. knowing
the encryption key is equivalent to knowing the decryption key. Kerckhoffs’ principle told us that
the security depended entirely on keeping the key private.

The idea of Public key cryptography is to make the situation asymmetric, using both public
and private information to share keys or communicate.


4.1    Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange

So far, all ciphers we have seen have required you to communicate the key with your friend. If this
is intercepted in any way then the security is broken!



                                                33
Question 4.1. Is there a way for two people to communicate a shared key without either having
to send it to the other?

The above sounds impossible at first glance...but we are about to see that it’s very much possible
using Modular Arithmetic.

Recall that for each prime p ≥ 1 we have the group of units mod p:

                                   (Z/pZ)× = {1, 2, ..., p − 1}.

We’ll need the following fact, which we will state without proof.

Theorem 4.2. (Z/pZ)× is cyclic, i.e. there exists g ∈ (Z/pZ)× such that every h can be written
as h = g n for some n ∈ Z.

Definition 4.3. An element g ∈ (Z/pZ)× satisfying the above property is called a primitive root.

Example 4.4. Show that 2 ∈ (Z/7Z)× is not a primitive root. Show that 3 ∈ (Z/7Z)× is a
primitive root.

Solution. The powers of 2 ∈ (Z/7Z)× are:
               1           2              3          4             5         6
              2 = 2,      2 = 4,      2 = 1,         2 = 2,    2 = 4,       2 = 1, ...
                                                                        n
and so 2 ∈ (Z/7Z)× is not a primitive root (e.g. we cannot write 3 = 2 ).

The powers of 3 ∈ (Z/7Z)× are:
               1           2              3          4             5         6
              3 = 3,      3 = 2,      3 = 6,         3 = 4,    3 = 5,       3 = 1, ...

and so 3 ∈ (Z/7Z)× is a primitive root.                                                         ■

In general, a primitive element is easy to find. For example, heuristics say that 2 ∈ (Z/pZ)× is a
primitive root for roughly 37.4% of primes p (although it is still an open problem to prove that
this is true, it is a case of the Artin Primitive Root Conjecture).

We’re now ready to see the Diffie-Hellman method of generating shared keys.

Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange


  1. Alice and Bob (publically) agree on a prime number p ≥ 1 and a primitive root g ∈ (Z/pZ)× .

  2. Alice chooses a private key kA ∈ Z and sends cA = g kA to Bob.

  3. Bob chooses a private key kB ∈ Z and sends cB = g kB to Alice.

  4. Alice computes c = cB kA and Bob computes c = cA kB . This is their shared key.


The above protocol was invented by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976. Before seeing
an example, we show that it does indeed work.

                                                34
Proposition 4.5. The above protocol generates the same key for Alice and Bob.

Proof. This is a simple calculation:

                         cB kA = (g kB )kA = g kB kA = g kA kB = (g kA )kB = ckAB .



Example 4.6. Alice and Bob decide to use Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange with p = 89 and g = 3 ∈
(Z/89Z)× . Alice decides to use private key kA = 10 and Bob decides to use private key kB = 25.
What messages do they send to each other and what is their shared key?

Solution. Alice sends the following to Bob:

                            cA ≡ g kA ≡ 310 ≡ (−8)2 · 9 ≡ 576 ≡ 42 mod 89.


Bob sends the following to Alice:

             cB ≡ g kB ≡ 325 ≡ (−8)6 · 3 ≡ (−25)3 · 3 ≡ 625 · 14 ≡ 2 · 14 ≡ 28 mod 89.


The shared key is given by:

 c ≡ ckAB ≡ 4225 ≡ 625 · 725 ≡ 204 · (−13)8 · 6 · 7 ≡ 442 · (−9)4 · 6 · 7 ≡ 67 · 64 · 6 · 7 ≡ 49 mod 89.


We check this as follows:

  c ≡ ckBA ≡ 2810 ≡ 410 · 710 ≡ (−11)2 · (−2)2 · 16 · 49 ≡ 32 · 4 · 16 · 49 ≡ 39 · (−17) ≡ 49 mod 89.

Now that we understand how Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange works, we discuss what makes this
secure.

Eve the evesdropper is interested in getting their hands on the shared key, so that they can read
future messages. Eve knows the prime p ≥ 1 and the primitive root g ∈ (Z/pZ)× , and has
intercepted the quantities cA = g kA and cB = g kB . In order to calculate the shared key c, Eve has
to solve the following problem.

Diffie-Hellman Problem: Given g x ∈ (Z/pZ)× and g y ∈ (Z/pZ)× , compute g xy ∈ (Z/pZ)× .

Generically, it turns out that this problem is very difficult to solve if p is large, unless you know
either of the quantities x or y. In the case above both of these pieces of information were kept
private, and so Eve has to solve a hard problem to get access to the shared key!

This kind of philosophy revolutionised Cryptography in the 1970’s, and is still a driving theme in
today’s cryptographic protocols. Classically, security rested entirely on the problem of communi-
cating a shared key secretly. Now, security could be being based on attackers having to solve hard
problems to get their hands on the information. In other words, the fact that Maths is hard is
a good thing!

How might you go about solving the Diffie-Hellman Problem if you don’t know x or y? You might
try and recover x from g x (or similarly with y). If we were working in R you know exactly what
you would do...take logs!
Definition 4.7. Let p ≥ 1 be prime and g ∈ (Z/pZ)× be a primitive root. A discrete log of h
with respect to g is x ∈ Z such that h = g x . We write x = dlogg (h).
Example 4.8. Given that 2 ∈ (Z/11Z)× is a primitive root, compute dlog2 (3) and dlog2 (10)

Because the powers of g repeat every p − 1 by Fermat’s Little Theorem, the quantity dlogg (h) is

Discrete Log Problem: Given a prime p ≥ 1, a primitive root g ∈ (Z/pZ)× and h ∈ (Z/pZ)× ,
compute dlogg (h).

So, if we can solve the Discrete Log Problem efficiently, then we can solve the Diffie-Hellman
Problem efficiently. However, this is also a very difficult problem to solve if p is large. This is
because the powers of g ∈ (Z/pZ)× behave quite randomly, as opposed to powers of a real number
(where we have a notion of size).

                                                    36
4.2    RSA

Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange provides a way of generating shared keys without needing to commu-
nicate them with each other. The idea was to each generate a private key and to only communicate
intermediate keys in public. Gaining access to the shared key required attackers to solve a tricky
mathematical problem (the Diffie-Hellman Problem or the Discrete Log Problem).

This was the world’s first method of asymmetric key exchange. Public knowledge of the
intermediate keys did not allow attackers to access either the private keys or the shared keys.

Soon after this, people wondered whether there could be an asymmetric cipher, i.e. one where
the public know how to encrypt messages intended for you, but such that decryption is only
feasible for the user.

The first such algorithm was announced to the public by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard
Adleman in 1977 (although later it was revealed that Clifford Cocks at GCHQ developed the same
idea internally in 1973). Their algorithm became known as RSA.

Before we see how RSA works, we need to further our understanding of Modular Arithmetic.

Definition 4.9. Let N ≥ 1. The group of units modulo N is

                        (Z/N Z)× = {a ∈ Z/N Z : gcd(a, N ) = 1} ⊆ Z/N Z.


For example, if p is prime then (Z/pZ)× = {1, 2, ..., p − 1} is the group of units mod p from earlier.
If N = 10 then (Z/10Z)× = {1, 3, 7, 9}.

Definition 4.10. Let N ≥ 2. Then the Euler totient function is defined by ϕ(N ) = |(Z/N Z)× |.

We’ll see more about this function later. For now we will just need the fact that ϕ(pq) = (p−1)(q−1)
if p ̸= q ≥ 1 are primes. We will also define ϕ(1) = 1 where necessary.

It turns out that there is a generalisation of Fermat’s Little Theorem for powers in (Z/N Z)× .

Theorem 4.11. (Fermat-Euler Theorem) Let N ≥ 1 and a ∈ Z be such that gcd(a, N ) = 1. Then
aϕ(N ) ≡ 1 mod N .

Proof. This is very similar to the proof of Fermat’s Little Theorem.

Since gcd(a, N ) = 1, a ∈ Z/N Z is invertible, and so multiplication by a is a bijection on (Z/N Z)× .

It follows that:
                                            ∏︂                ∏︂
                                                    ax =                x.
                                     x∈(Z/N Z)×            x∈(Z/N Z)×

However, this product also equals aϕ(N )
                                            ∏︁
                                                 x∈(Z/N Z)× x, and so we have that
                                                 ∏︂              ∏︂
                                   aϕ(N )                x=                  x.
                                            x∈(Z/N Z)×        x∈(Z/N Z)×


But each x ∈ (Z/N Z)× is invertible, so cancelling from both sides gives aϕ(N ) = 1.

We’ll need to know one more thing before we can state the RSA encryption algorithm.

Lemma 4.12. Let N ≥ 1 and e ∈ Z. The map

                                    fe : (Z/N Z)× −→ (Z/N Z)×
                                                m ↦→ me

has an inverse whenever gcd(e, ϕ(N )) = 1.

Proof. First, note that if gcd(e, ϕ(N )) = 1 then there exists d ∈ Z such that ed ≡ 1 mod ϕ(N ).
Equivalently, ed = 1 + kϕ(N ) for some k ∈ Z.

We claim that the inverse of fe is fd . This follows since:
                                                                              k
               fd (fe (m)) = fd (me ) = med = m1+kϕ(N ) = m · (mϕ(N ) )k = m · 1 = m,

by the Fermat-Euler Theorem. Similarly fe (fd (m)) = m.

RSA Encryption/Decryption

  1. Alice secretly chooses two distinct primes p and q and computes N = pq. She chooses e ∈ Z
     such that gcd(e, (p − 1)(q − 1)) = 1.

  2. Alice publicly reveals her public key to be the pair (N, e).

  3. Bob encrypts the message m ∈ (Z/N Z)× by computing c = fe (m) ∈ (Z/N Z)× . He sends
     this to Alice.

  4. To decrypt, Alice uses Euclid’s algorithm to compute her private key, d ∈ Z such that
     ed ≡ 1 mod (p − 1)(q − 1). She then decrypts by computing m = fd (c).

Note that Alice now has a public key (N, e) and a private key (p, q, d). The public key allows
anyone to communicate with Alice, but the private key is kept secret for decryption purposes.

Proposition 4.13. The above protocol allows Alice to retrieve the message.

Proof. Since ϕ(N ) = (p − 1)(q − 1), and gcd(e, (p − 1)(q − 1)) = 1, the map fe has an inverse. As
in the above proof, this is given by fd with d ∈ Z satisfying ed ≡ 1 mod (p − 1)(q − 1).

A simple computation now shows that

                                       fd (c) = fd (fe (m)) = m,

so that Alice is able to recover the message.

Example 4.14. Alice uses RSA with public key (N, e) = (55, 3). Bob wishes to send the message
m = 12 ∈ (Z/N Z)× to Alice.

What is the ciphertext c ∈ (Z/55Z)× that Bob sends? Demonstrate that Alice is able to decrypt the
ciphertext.

Solution. Bob sends the ciphertext:

                               c ≡ m3 ≡ 123 ≡ 1728 ≡ 23 mod 55.

Alice knows that p = 5 and q = 11 and so computes (p − 1)(q − 1) = 40. She then finds that her
private key is d = 27, since 3 · 27 ≡ 1 mod 40 (in practice, Euclid’s algorithm would be used here).

She recovers the message by computing:

                  m ≡ cd ≡ 2327 ≡ 129 ≡ 9 · 8 · (−4)3 ≡ 17 · (−9) ≡ 12 mod 55.

So why is RSA secure? Well once again let’s put ourselves in Eve’s shoes. The only information
available is public key (N, e). If she intercepts a ciphertext c then she has to solve the following
problem.

RSA Problem: Given N = pq with p, q distinct primes, e ∈ Z such that gcd(e, ϕ(N )) = 1 and
c = me ∈ (Z/N Z)× , retrieve the message m ∈ (Z/N Z)× .

Generally, it turns out that this problem is very difficult. The only known way of recovering the
message m is to calculate the private key d (since fd is the inverse map of fe ).

However, to calculate d you need to solve the congruence ed ≡ 1 mod (p − 1)(q − 1). Eve could
easily do this using Euclid’s Algorithm if she knew what the number (p − 1)(q − 1) was. But there
seems to be no way to compute this number without knowing what p and q are (as opposed to just
knowing their product, N = pq). In other words, Eve needs to solve the following problem.

Semiprime Factorisation Problem: Given a semiprime N = pq, recover the primes p and q.

If we can solve the Semiprime Factorisation Problem efficiently, then we can solve the RSA Problem
efficiently. However, generally this is also a very difficult problem to solve if p and q are large.

The best attacks for the Semiprime Factorisation Problem use sophisticated tools from Algebraic
Number Theory. However, they only run in sub-exponential time relative to N and so are
not efficient to be practical for large primes p, q (which would be about 300 − 400 digits long in
the real world).


4.3    El-Gamal

In the last two sections we have got a feel for the idea of public key cryptography. We can now
use Mathematics to provide security by forcing attackers to solve hard mathematical problems to
recover our messages.

We saw that for the Diffie-Hellman key exchange the security relied on the fact that the Discrete
Log Problem is hard to solve. Once this method has been used, both parties use the shared
key to encrypt/decrypt under some symmetric scheme. The question now is whether there exists
an asymmetric scheme allowing communication, whose security is based on the difficulty of the
Discrete Log Problem?

                                                39
The answer is yes, the El-Gamal scheme! This scheme can be used with any finite group, and
the security relies on the generalised Discrete Log Problem for such groups. In this course, we’ll
only work with cyclic groups. Not much is lost by making this restriction.

First we need to know what discrete logs are in this setting. Let’s go back to the case of (Z/pZ)× .
A discrete log of h ∈ (Z/pZ)× only made sense once we fixed a primitive root g ∈ (Z/pZ)× , and
then it was defined to be any b ∈ Z such that a = g b . For arbitrary cyclic groups things are pretty
much identical.

Definition 4.15. Let G be a finite cyclic group. A discrete log of h ∈ G with respect to generator
g ∈ G is k ∈ Z such that h = g k . We write k = dlogg (h).

Remark 4.16. Be careful, when we write g k this means apply the group operation k times to g.
It doesn’t necessarily mean that it’s the product of g with itself k times (e.g. the group operation
might be addition).

Recall that the discrete log in (Z/pZ)× was only defined mod p − 1 (because g p−1 = 1). Something
very similar happens for cyclic groups.

Theorem 4.17. Let G be a finite cyclic group and h ∈ G. Then the set of discrete logs for h ∈ G
with respect to generator g ∈ G is a congruence class mod |G|.

Proof. If k ∈ Z is a discrete log for h with respect to g then so is k + |G|m for any m ∈ Z. This
follows from the fact that g |G| = e:


                            g k+|G|m = g k g |G|m = g k (g |G| )m = g k em = h.

Now suppose that k ′ ∈ Z is another discrete log for h with respect to g, but that k ′ is not in the
same class as k mod |G|. Without loss of generality we can assume that 1 ≤ k < k ′ ≤ |G|. Then
             ′                   ′
h = g k = g k , implying that g k −k = e. But this contradicts the fact that g is a primitive root,
since then g has order dividing k ′ − k < |G|.

Example 4.18. Let G = (Z/pZ)× . Then the discrete log of h ∈ G with respect to generator g ∈ G
is by definition the discrete log that we studied earlier.

For example, if p = 11, g = 2 ∈ (Z/11Z)× and h = 6 ∈ (Z/11Z)× then dlogg (h) = dlog2 (6) =
9 + 10Z, since 29 ≡ 2−1 ≡ 6 mod 11.

Example 4.19. Let G = Z/10Z under addition, g = 7 ∈ Z/10Z and h = 3 ∈ Z/10Z. Then
dlogg (h) = dlog7 (3) = 9 + 10Z, since 7 · 9 = 63 ≡ 3 mod 10.

We are now able to see how discrete logs can be used in public key cryptography.

El-Gamal Encryption

   1. Alice chooses a finite cyclic group G of size q and fixes a generator g ∈ G. She chooses a
      secret value 1 ≤ k ≤ q and computes h = g k . Alice’s public key is (G, q, g, h) and her private
      key is k.

   2. Bob encrypts a message m ∈ G by choosing a secret value 1 ≤ s ≤ q and computing c1 = g s
      and c2 = mhs (which uses public information). He sends c = (c1 , c2 ) to Alice.

   3. Alice decrypts by computing m = c2 c−k
                                          1 (possible since she knows k).


Before seeing an example we first prove that Alice does indeed decrypt to the correct message m.

Proof. This is a simple calculation:

                       1 = (mh )(g )   = (mg sk )(g −sk ) = m(g sk g −sk ) = me = m.


Example 4.22. Let’s work with the multiplicative group G = (Z/31Z)× . Alice chooses generator
g = 3 ∈ G and private key k = 11 ∈ Z. Then Alice’s public key is (G, q, g, h) = (G, 30, 3, 13) since:


                        h = 311 ≡ (−4)4 · 3−1 ≡ 2 · 4 · 21 ≡ −80 ≡ 13 mod 31.

                                c2 = 7 · 134 ≡ 7 · 142 ≡ 70 ≡ 8 mod 31.

Remark 4.23. A few things:

    Given an arbitrary finite group G we can easily construct cyclic subgroups by picking a

We can now discuss the security of the El-Gamal scheme. Note that Eve knows G, q, g, h, c1 and c2 ,
and so to get the message m she needs to use this to compute m = c2 c−k  1 . However, Eve does not
know the value of k and so the only clear way for her to get this is to calculate dlogg (h) = k + qZ.
But this is an instance of the Discrete Log Problem for G.

The Discrete Log Problem (for cyclic groups): Given a finite cyclic group G, a generator
g ∈ G and h ∈ G, compute dlogg (h).

So the security of El-Gamal depends entirely on how hard it is to solve the Discrete Log Problem for
the chosen group G. As we saw earlier though, groups that are abstractly the same (i.e. isomorphic)
can have Discrete Log Problems of varying difficulty.

Example 4.24. Let G = Z/N Z (under addition) and choose g ∈ G coprime with N . Then g
generates G.

Solving the Discrete Log Problem for this setup means being able to solve the congruence gx ≡
h mod N (since the group is additive). This congruence is easily solvable by multiplying both sides
by g −1 mod N (computable in polynomial time by Euclid’s algorithm).

For example, suppose N = 13 and g = 7. Then dlog7 (9) = 5 + 13Z, since 7x ≡ 9 mod 13 has
solution x ≡ 9 · 7−1 ≡ 18 ≡ 5 mod 13. Surely enough 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 = 7 · 5 = 35 ≡ 9 mod 13.

Given the above example it should be clear that you should never use G = Z/N Z with the El-
Gamal scheme. The Discrete Log Problem is extremely simple to solve since we have Euclid’s
algorithm. In stark contrast, we saw that the Discrete Log Problem for (Z/pZ)× is believed to be
difficult (even though this group is isomorphic to the additive group Z/(p − 1)Z). Multiplicative
groups (Z/pZ)× have proved to be good choices for G in practice.

Another popular choice is G = E(Fp ), the set of points on an elliptic curve mod p. The group
law here is much less easy to understand, since it’s defined geometrically. Elliptic Curve Cryp-
tography is used in many real world protocols (e.g. ECDSA, ECDH and Bitcoin).


4.4    Digital Signatures

We have seen now that public key cryptography gives us a great way to communicate securely in
an asymmetric way. But it can do much more than this!

One issue with symmetric schemes is that if Eve can find the shared key she can read all messages
between Alice and Bob. Also, Eve can then pretend to be Alice or Bob without immediate detection!
