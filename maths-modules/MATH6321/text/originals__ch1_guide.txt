1    The basics you need before Section 1.1

Cryptography is about making a message unreadable to someone who does not have the secret infor-
mation required to decode it. A plaintext is the original message, a ciphertext is the scrambled
message, and a key tells us how the scrambling works.

                         Plaintext + key −→ Encryption −→ Ciphertext

                         Ciphertext + key −→ Decryption −→ Plaintext

The lecture notes use M for the set of possible plaintexts, C for ciphertexts and K for keys. For a
particular key k, encryption is a function ek and decryption is dk . The vital condition is

                                         dk (ek (m)) = m.

This says: if you encrypt and then decrypt with the correct key, you must get back the original
message. In function notation, dk ◦ ek = idM , where idM is the identity function (it leaves every
message unchanged).


Turn letters into numbers

Throughout the chapter, letters correspond to numbers:

                              Letter    A   B   C    D   E   ···   Z
                             Number     0   1   2    3   4   ···   25

So C = 2, E = 4, T = 19, and Z = 25. Spaces and punctuation are usually ignored in the
mathematical calculations.


What does “mod 26” mean?

It means “keep the remainder after division by 26”—or simply wrap around the alphabet. For
example, 28 ≡ 2 (mod 26), because 28 = 26 + 2; and −3 ≡ 23 (mod 26), because −3 + 26 = 23.

    Worked example

    Take letter Z (=25) and shift forward by 4: 25+4 = 29. Reduce modulo 26: 29 ≡ 3 (mod 26),
    which corresponds to D. Thus Z becomes D.

    Remember
    A frequent mistake is to label A as 1. In these notes, A is 0 and Z is 25. Use this
    consistently.



2    1.1 The Caesar cipher

    Key idea

    The Caesar cipher encrypts every letter using the same forward shift. Its secret key is an
    integer k ∈ {0, 1, . . . , 25}.


                                                3
MATH6321 | Chapter 1 explained                                                     Classical Cryptography



                         c≡m+k          (mod 26)        m≡c−k           (mod 26)
Here m is the plaintext-letter number and c is the ciphertext-letter number.

    Worked example

    Encrypt CAT with key k = 3.
    C = 2: 2 + 3 = 5 ⇒ F.
    A = 0: 0 + 3 = 3 ⇒ D.
    T = 19: 19 + 3 = 22 ⇒ W.
    So CAT −→ FDW .

    Decrypt FDW. Subtract 3: F(5) → C(2), D(3) → A(0), W(22) → T(19). The original
    word is CAT.


Why does decryption work? (Exercise 1.3)

First add the key, then subtract it:

                             dk (ek (m)) ≡ (m + k) − k ≡ m       (mod 26).

Since m is already a number between 0 and 25, this recovers exactly the same letter. This is the
required proof that dk ◦ ek = id.


Why it is insecure

There are only 26 keys. An attacker can simply try k = 0, 1, . . . , 25 and look for sensible English.
This is called a brute-force attack. The lecture notes illustrate Caesar encryption with shift 3 in
Example 1.2.

    Remember
    Exam pattern: If given a Caesar key, apply the formula to each letter. If asked to prove
    correctness, show that the two shifts cancel modulo 26. If asked about security, say only 26
    possible keys.



3    1.2 Monoalphabetic substitution ciphers

Caesar always shifts by the same amount, so it is a particularly simple example of a more general
scheme: choose an arbitrary rearrangement of the 26 letters.

    Key idea

    A substitution key is a permutation σ ∈ S26 , meaning a one-to-one, onto map from the
    alphabet to itself. Each plaintext letter is always replaced by the same ciphertext letter.


                                       c = σ(m)        m = σ −1 (c) .

The notation S26 is the group of all permutations of 26 objects. The inverse permutation σ −1
reverses the substitutions.

MATH6321 | Chapter 1 explained                                                       Classical Cryptography


    Worked example

    Suppose the key swaps A ↔ B, C ↔ D, E ↔ F, and so on (as in Example 1.5 of the notes).
    Then BAD encrypts to ABC: B→A, A→B, D→C. Applying the swaps again decrypts it.


How many keys? (Example 1.6)

You can send A to any of 26 letters. Once A’s destination is used, B has 25 choices, C has 24, etc.
Hence
                                 |S26 | = 26! = 26 × 25 × · · · × 1 .
The notes give 26! = 403291461126605635584000000. This is huge—but a huge keyspace does not
automatically imply good security.


The important weakness: frequency analysis

Because the substitution never changes, a plaintext letter appearing 100 times produces one particu-
lar ciphertext letter appearing 100 times. The identities are hidden, but the counts are preserved.
In typical English, E is common (the notes give about 12.7%); T, A, O, I and N are also frequent.
In a long ciphertext, its most frequent symbol may therefore stand for E. Then look for short words,
double letters, and common sequences such as THE.

    Worked example

    Suppose the ciphertext often contains TGE. Guess that it represents THE. This predicts T→T,
    G→H and E→E for these ciphertext symbols under decryption. Test the guess throughout the
    message. If many words emerge, the guess becomes more convincing. This is the reasoning
    used in Example 1.7 of the notes (with different intermediate replacements).



Exercise 1.8 explained

Alice keeps repeating the word LIKE. In a monoalphabetic substitution, this always turns into
the same four-letter ciphertext block. Repeating words reveal the message’s structure and give
an attacker strong clues. Using less repetitive language removes some clues, but does not fix the
underlying frequency weakness.


4    1.3 The Vigenère cipher

    Key idea

    Vigenère uses a sequence of Caesar shifts, cycling through the same short key over and
    over. The key changes with the position of the letter.

Write the key as k = (k1 , . . . , kn ). For plaintext letter mi in position i, use key entry k1+((i−1) mod n) .
This 1-based indexing is an explicit way to understand the cyclic subscripts in the notes.

                                   ci ≡ mi + k1+((i−1) mod n)     (mod 26)

                                   mi ≡ ci − k1+((i−1) mod n)     (mod 26)

How to encrypt with a keyword

A keyword is converted to number shifts, so BOB means (1, 14, 1), just as in Example 1.13. Repeat
it as many times as necessary.

   Worked example

   Encrypt CATCAT using key BOB.

                           Position            1        2    3   4    5    6
                           Plaintext           C        A    T   C   A     T
                           Plaintext number    2        0   19   2    0   19
                           Repeated key        B       O     B   B   O     B
                           Key number          1       14    1   1   14    1
                           Cipher number       3       14   20   3   14   20
                           Ciphertext          D       O    U    D   O     U

   So CATCAT → DOUDOU . To decrypt, subtract the repeated key numbers instead.



Why this improves on substitution

An E encrypted at one position might become F, while an E at the next position might become G.
The same letter can have different ciphertext symbols, so simple overall frequency counting is far
less useful. However, because the key repeats, patterns remain.


Exercise 1.10: Caesar is Vigenère with key length 1

When n = 1, every position uses k1 . Therefore ci ≡ mi + k1 (mod 26) at every position, which is
exactly Caesar.


How the Kasiski test finds a key length

If a plaintext sequence repeats at positions using the same part of the repeating key, the ciphertext
sequence repeats too. Those two starting positions differ by a multiple of the key length. So:

1. Search the ciphertext for repeated three-letter blocks (trigrams) or longer patterns.

2. Measure the distances between their starting positions.

3. Factor those distances and look for a small factor that occurs repeatedly.

4. Treat that common factor as a candidate key length—not a proof.

   Worked example

   The notes’ Example 1.15 includes repeated trigrams with distances 21, 98, 84. Factor them:

                           21 = 3 · 7,    98 = 2 · 72 ,      84 = 22 · 3 · 7.

   All three are divisible by 7, so n = 7 is a strong candidate. Other coincidences in the notes
   have distances not divisible by 7: repeats can occur by chance.

    Remember
    The Kasiski test suggests a key length, not the individual shift values. We need more tools

5    1.4 The One-Time Pad (OTP)

    Key idea

    The OTP looks like Vigenère, but its key is as long as the entire message, generated
    uniformly at random and never reused. Under these conditions it has perfect secrecy.

For a length-t message, take k = (k1 , . . . , kt ). There is a separate independent shift for every
plaintext position:
                      ci ≡ mi + ki    (mod 26) ,           mi ≡ ci − ki   (mod 26) .

    Worked example

    Encrypt CAT with the one-time key (4, 1, 7):   C(2)+4=6→G; A(0)+1=1→B;
    T(19)+7=26≡0→A. So CAT→GBA. Each number is only used for this particular mes-
    sage position.


Why does the OTP reveal no information?

Fix any ciphertext c and any possible plaintext m of equal length. There is exactly one key that
produces it:
                                    ki ≡ ci − mi (mod 26).
If all keys are equally likely, each possible plaintext is compatible with the observed ciphertext with
exactly the same key probability. Thus observing ciphertext does not update which plaintext was
more likely (beyond prior information). This is perfect secrecy—the logic behind the lecture
notes’ Example 1.17 involving ciphertext AAA.


What goes wrong if you reuse the key?

Encrypt messages m and m′ using the same key k:
                             ci ≡ mi + ki ,      c′i ≡ m′i + ki   (mod 26).
Subtract:
                                     ci − c′i ≡ mi − m′i    (mod 26) .
The key cancels! This reveals a relation between the two messages, so reuse destroys the perfect
secrecy argument. The lecture notes show this visually with two black-and-white images in their
page 8 illustration.


Three practical limitations

1. Key distribution: senders and receivers need a shared key as long as the message.
2. True randomness: all possible keys must be sampled uniformly.
3. One use only: a used key must not be used again.

6    1.5 The Index of Coincidence (IoC)

Why we need it

For Vigenère with key length n, every n-th letter was encrypted with the same Caesar shift. Split
the ciphertext into partial ciphertexts:

               s1 = c1 c1+n c1+2n · · · ,   s2 = c2 c2+n c2+2n · · · ,   ...,     sn = cn c2n · · · .

Each si is therefore a Caesar-encrypted piece of ordinary text when the guessed key length is correct.
Such pieces should preserve English-like letter frequencies. The Index of Coincidence checks
whether that is plausible.

    Key idea

    IoC is the probability that two different positions sampled uniformly from one string
    contain the same letter.

For a string of length t, let NA , NB , . . . , NZ be its letter counts:

                                                    L=A NL (NL − 1)
                                                   PZ
                                        IoC(s) =                           .
                                                            t(t − 1)


Understand the formula before memorising it

For one letter, say A, there are NA ways to choose an A first and NA − 1 remaining ways to choose
another A. Add that over all 26 letters for the favourable ordered pairs. The denominator counts all
ordered pairs of distinct positions, t(t − 1). Their ratio is exactly the matching probability (Lemma
1.22).

    Worked example

    Find IoC of AABBC. There are 5 letters, with NA = 2, NB = 2, NC = 1 and all other counts
    zero:
                           2(2 − 1) + 2(2 − 1) + 1(1 − 1)    4
                     IoC =                                =     = 0.20 .
                                       5(5 − 1)             20
    Note: This is a very short and deliberately repetitive word, so don’t expect it to match the
    long-text benchmarks.

Use IoC to estimate Vigenère key length

1. Guess a key length a.

2. Split ciphertext into a partial ciphertexts.

3. Compute IoC for each.

4. If values cluster around 0.0656, the guess is promising. If they cluster near 0.0385, try another
   length.

    Worked example

    The notes’ Example 1.25 tests key lengths 4–9. For a = 7, its seven partial IoCs are

                           0.062, 0.057, 0.065, 0.059, 0.060, 0.064, 0.064.

    These are closer to the English-like value 0.0656 than the other candidate lengths’ mostly
    smaller values. This supports n = 7 , agreeing with the Kasiski test.

    Remember
    IoC is a statistical clue, not a mathematical guarantee for finite ciphertexts. Short text
    and coincidences can give misleading values.



7    1.6 The Mutual Index of Coincidence (MutIoC)

The new problem

After using Kasiski/IoC, we might know the Vigenère key length, but not its shifts. We need a way
to compare two partial ciphertexts and ask whether their shift patterns match.

    Key idea

    MutIoC is the probability that a random position from string s and a random position from
    r contain the same letter. It compares two strings, rather than two positions in one string.

For strings of lengths t1 and t2 ,

                                                       L=A NL (s)NL (r)
                                                      PZ
                                     MutIoC(s, r) =                       .
                                                            t1 t2

For a fixed letter L, there are NL (s)NL (r) ways to match L across the two strings; sum and divide
by t1 t2 possible cross-string pairs (Lemma 1.27).

    Worked example

    Calculate MutIoC(AAB, ABC).
    Counts in AAB: A=2, B=1, C=0. Counts in ABC: A=1, B=1, C=1. Therefore

                                                2·1+1·1+0·1   1
                                     MutIoC =               =   .
                                                    3·3       3

Why does comparing shifts work?

Suppose partial ciphertext si was made by shifting its plaintext by ki , while sj used kj . If we shift
all of sj forward by β, its effective shift becomes kj + β. To line it up with si , choose

                        kj + β ≡ ki      (mod 26),       so     βi,j ≡ ki − kj    (mod 26) .

Then both strings behave like English encrypted under the same Caesar key. In the notes’ Theorem
1.29, their MutIoC is therefore expected to be around 0.0656 for long English-like text. Unrelated
shifts often give smaller values, commonly near 0.0385, but not always.


The complete attack algorithm

1. Estimate the key length n using Kasiski or IoC.
2. Divide ciphertext into s1 , . . . , sn .
3. For pairs (i, j), shift sj by each β = 0, . . . , 25 and compute MutIoC(si , eβ (sj )).
4. When MutIoC is relatively high (roughly 0.0656), guess ki − kj ≡ β (mod 26).
5. Solve these modular equations for the unknown key entries.
6. If one common shift remains free, test its 26 possible values and look for meaningful English.

   Worked example

   Solve a small model system. Suppose a 3-letter key obeys

                                   k1 − k2 ≡ 4,         k2 − k3 ≡ 7         (mod 26).

   Let k1 = λ. Then k2 ≡ λ − 4, and from the second equation k3 ≡ k2 − 7 ≡ λ − 11. Therefore

                                 (k1 , k2 , k3 ) = (λ, λ − 4, λ − 11)       (mod 26) .

   All 26 choices λ = 0, . . . , 25 satisfy the two equations. Try them against the ciphertext and
   see which produces intelligible English.


The lecture notes’ full Example 1.31: DICKENS

The notes use the ciphertext first introduced in Example 1.15, and settle on length 7. Their MutIoC
comparisons give several relationships, including

                          k1 − k3 ≡ 1,        k1 − k4 ≡ 19,      k2 − k3 ≡ 6      (mod 26).

After solving the full set of congruences, the notes obtain

                     k = (λ, λ − 21, λ − 1, λ − 19, λ − 25, λ − 16, λ − 11)             (mod 26) .

Try λ = 3. Modulo 26 the numbers are

                                                (3, 8, 2, 10, 4, 13, 18),

which correspond to letters DICKENS . Decrypting reveals the opening words of David Copperfield:
“WHETHER I SHALL TURN OUT TO BE THE HERO OF MY OWN LIFE ...”. This is the
end-to-end attack in the original chapter.

Quick decision tree for exam questions

1. “Encrypt/decrypt with shift k”: Caesar addition/subtraction modulo 26.

2. “Permutation key”: apply σ; decrypt with σ −1 .

3. “Keyword/repeating key”: Vigenère; write repeated shifts above letters.

4. “Key length unknown”: repeated trigrams/Kasiski, then test with partial-string IoC.

5. “Key length known but shifts unknown”: use pairwise shifted MutIoC, modular congru-
   ences, and finally a short search.

6. “Prove perfect secrecy or explain key reuse”: use uniqueness of OTP key or subtract two
   ciphertext equations.

 10     Fully worked practice answers

 1. M(12)+5 = 17=R; A(0)+5 = 5=F; T(19)+5 = 24=Y; H(7)+5 = 12=M. RFYM . Decrypt by
    subtracting 5 and reducing modulo 26.

 2. K(10)−3 = 7=H; H(7)−3 = 4=E; O(14)−3 = 11=L; O→L; R(17)−3 = 14=O. HELLO .

 3. The first alphabet letter has 26 choices of image, the next has 25, and so on; multiply to get 26!.
    But fixed substitution preserves frequency counts, allowing statistical attacks on long texts.

 4. D=3, O=14, G=6; key C=2, A=0, T=19. Add to get 5, 14, 25, i.e. FOZ .

 5. 24 = 23 · 3, 36 = 22 · 32 , 60 = 22 · 3 · 5. The gcd is 12; factors 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12 merit checking.
    n = 12 is an initial candidate, but repeated patterns can be accidental or come from multiples
    of the underlying period. Validate with IoC.

 6. With ci = mi + ki and c′i = m′i + ki mod 26, subtraction yields ci − c′i ≡ mi − m′i (mod 26) .
    The secret key vanishes.

 7. Counts NA = 3, NB = 2, length t = 5; IoC = [3(2) + 2(1)]/[5(4)] = 8/20 = 0.4 .

 8. s = AAB has A=2,B=1; r = ABB has A=1,B=2. Hence MutIoC = (2 · 1 + 1 · 2)/(3 · 3) = 4/9 .

 9. Not compelling: the partial IoCs are clustered near random-alphabet 0.0385, not English-
    substitution 0.0656. Test other lengths.

10. Let k1 = λ. Then k2 = λ − 5, k3 = λ − 5 − 9 = λ − 14. Thus (λ, λ − 5, λ − 14) (mod 26) ,
    λ = 0, . . . , 25.
