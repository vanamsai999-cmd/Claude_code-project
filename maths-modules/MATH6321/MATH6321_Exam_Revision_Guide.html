Part 0The arithmetic toolkit used everywhere

Nearly every computational mark depends on five skills. If these are fast and reliable, every chapter becomes easier.

T1. Modular arithmetic tricks

  • a ≡ b (mod n) means n divides a − b. Reduce after every multiplication so numbers stay small.
  • Use negative representatives. Mod 89, 81 ≡ −8, so 38 = (34)2 ≡ (−8)2 = 64. The notes do this constantly.
  • Two moduli rule. Group elements live mod p (or mod N). Exponents live mod p − 1 (or mod ϕ(N)). Mixing these up is the most common mistake in Chapters 4 and 6.
  • Letters: A = 0, B = 1, …, Z = 25. (A question may use 1–26 instead. Read the convention given.)

T2. gcd and modular inverses (Extended Euclid)

a has an inverse mod n exactly when gcd(a, n) = 1. To find it, run Euclid's algorithm, then substitute backwards.

Worked example: find 5−1 mod 264 (needed for RSA in Coursework 3)

Euclid:     264 = 52·5 + 4
              5 = 1·4  + 1
Back-sub:     1 = 5 − 1·4
              1 = 5 − (264 − 52·5) = 53·5 − 1·264
So 5·53 ≡ 1 (mod 264), and 5−1 ≡ 53 (mod 264).    Check: 5·53 = 265 = 264 + 1 ✓

Worked example: find 17−1 mod 61

61 = 3·17 + 10        1 = 7 − 2·3
17 = 1·10 + 7           = 7 − 2(10 − 7) = 3·7 − 2·10
10 = 1·7  + 3           = 3(17 − 10) − 2·10 = 3·17 − 5·10
 7 = 2·3  + 1           = 3·17 − 5(61 − 3·17) = 18·17 − 5·61
So 17−1 ≡ 18 (mod 61).    Check: 17·18 = 306 = 5·61 + 1 ✓

For small moduli, trial is fine: 3−1 mod 7 is 5 because 3·5 = 15 ≡ 1.

T3. Fast powers: repeated squaring

Method: compute be mod n

  1. Write e as a sum of powers of 2 (its binary expansion).
  2. Square repeatedly: b, b2, b4, b8, … reducing mod n each time.
  3. Multiply together the powers you need, reducing as you go.

Worked example: 313 mod 89

13 = 8 + 4 + 1
31 = 3,  32 = 9,  34 = 81 ≡ −8,  38 ≡ (−8)2 = 64
313 = 38·34·3 ≡ 64·(−8)·3 = −1536 ≡ −1536 + 18·89 = 66 (mod 89)

This is the h = 66 in Coursework 3 Q2, which checks that Bob's public key is consistent.

Exam tip: shortcuts that save minutes

  • Fermat: if p is prime and p ∤ a, then ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Reduce exponents mod p − 1 first: 7100 mod 11 = 7100 mod 10 = 70 = 1.
  • Fermat–Euler: if gcd(a, N) = 1, then aϕ(N) ≡ 1 (mod N), with ϕ(pq) = (p − 1)(q − 1).
  • Inverse via powers: mod a prime p, a−k = ap−1−k. The notes write 19−11 ≡ 1919 (mod 31). That is this trick.
  • If you spot bk ≡ ±1 early, use it: mod 85, 44 = 256 ≡ 1, so 411 = 48·43 ≡ 64 instantly.

T4. Solving a linear congruence ax ≡ b (mod m)

Method

  1. Let t = gcd(a, m). If t ∤ b, there is no solution.
  2. If t = 1: x ≡ a−1b (mod m), one solution mod m.
  3. If t > 1 and t | b: divide everything by t, solve (a/t)x ≡ b/t (mod m/t) to get x0. The solutions mod m are x0, x0 + m/t, x0 + 2m/t, …, giving t solutions. Test each one in the original problem.

Worked example: −6k ≡ 8 (mod 28)

gcd(6, 28) = 2, and 2 | 8, so there are 2 solutions mod 28.
Divide by 2:  −3k ≡ 4 (mod 14)   ⇔   3k ≡ −4 ≡ 10 (mod 14)
3−1 mod 14 = 5   (3·5 = 15 ≡ 1)
k ≡ 5·10 = 50 ≡ 8 (mod 14)
Solutions mod 28:  k ≡ 8  or  k ≡ 22

This exact situation appears in Pollard rho for discrete logs (Chapter 6).

T5. Arithmetic in F2 and polynomials over F2

  • In F2 = {0, 1}: 1 + 1 = 0. Addition is XOR (⊕), and subtraction is the same as addition.
  • Polynomials over F2: (x + 1)2 = x2 + 1, and x2 + x + 1 is the only irreducible quadratic.
  • A polynomial f has a root at 0 iff its constant term is 0. It has a root at 1 iff it has an even number of terms.
  • Polynomial long division mod 2: subtract (= add) shifted copies of the divisor, as in the LFSR section.

Chapter 1 · Exam Q1Classical cryptography

The big picture. Each cipher here fixes a weakness of the one before it. Caesar has too few keys. Substitution has 26! keys but keeps letter frequencies. Vigenère hides frequencies but repeats its key. The One Time Pad never repeats its key and is perfectly secure, but it is impractical. IoC and MutIoC are the statistical tools that break Vigenère.

Language of the course

Plaintext m ∈ M, ciphertext c ∈ C, key k ∈ K. Encryption ek : M → C, decryption dk with dk ∘ ek = idM. Kerckhoffs' principle: security should depend only on keeping the key secret, not on hiding the method. All ciphers in Chapters 1–3 are symmetric: knowing the encryption key means knowing the decryption key.

1.1 Caesar cipher

Definition

Key k ∈ {0, 1, …, 25}. Encrypt: c ≡ m + k (mod 26). Decrypt: m ≡ c − k (mod 26). Each letter shifts k places forward, wrapping from Z to A.

Worked example: encrypt ATTACK AT DAWN with k = 5

A T T A C K   A T   D A W N
0 19 19 0 2 10  0 19  3 0 22 13
+5:  5 24 24 5 7 15  5 24  8 5 27≡1 18
F Y Y F H P   F Y   I F B S

Note W: 22 + 5 = 27 ≡ 1 = B (wrap-around).

Weakness: only 26 keys, so brute force all shifts and read off the one that gives English.

1.2 Monoalphabetic substitution cipher

Definition

Key: any permutation σ ∈ S26. Encrypt ci = σ(mi), decrypt mi = σ−1(ci). Number of keys: |S26| = 26! ≈ 4.03 × 1026. Caesar is the special case σ(x) = x + k.

Method: decrypt when the key is given in cycle notation

  1. Encrypting moves each letter forward one place in its cycle: in (A Q Z), A → Q, Q → Z, Z → A.
  2. Decrypting applies σ−1, so move each letter backward one place: Q → A, Z → Q, A → Z.
  3. Letters not in any cycle are fixed.

Worked example

Key σ = (A Q Z)(E M)(T H R). Decrypt HRM EQAM.

H: backward in (T H R) gives T      E: backward in (E M) gives M
R: backward in (T H R) gives H      Q: backward in (A Q Z) gives A
M: backward in (E M)   gives E      A: backward in (A Q Z) gives Z
                                    M: gives E
Plaintext: THE MAZE

Check by encrypting forward: T→H, H→R, E→M, M→E, A→Q, Z→A gives HRM EQAM ✓.

Method: frequency analysis attack

  1. Count each ciphertext letter. Substitution preserves frequencies: if E is 12.7% of the plaintext, σ(E) is about 12.7% of the ciphertext.
  2. Match the most common ciphertext letters to E, T, A, O, I, N, S, H, R (order: ETAOINSHRDLU).
  3. Use word patterns: a common three-letter word is probably THE; single letters are A or I; doubled letters are often LL, EE, SS, OO, TT.
  4. Substitute your guesses, look for partial words, and keep going. Treat guesses as evidence, not proof, especially in short texts.

1.3 Vigenère cipher

Definition

Key k = (k1, …, kn), usually a keyword (BOB → (1, 14, 1)); n is the key length. Encrypt ci ≡ mi + ki mod n (mod 26), cycling through the key. Decrypt by subtracting. It is a polyalphabetic cipher: several Caesar shifts used in turn. Caesar is the case n = 1.

Worked example: encrypt SENDMOREMONEY with keyword KEY = (10, 4, 24)

plaintext  S  E  N  D  M  O  R  E  M  O  N  E  Y
values    18  4 13  3 12 14 17  4 12 14 13  4 24
key        K  E  Y  K  E  Y  K  E  Y  K  E  Y  K
shift     10  4 24 10  4 24 10  4 24 10  4 24 10
sum mod26  2  8 11 13 16 12  1  8 10 24 17  2  8
cipher     C  I  L  N  Q  M  B  I  K  Y  R  C  I

The two E's at positions 2 and 8 both become I, because both used shift 4. The E at position 12 becomes C (shift 24). The same letter can encrypt differently, and different letters can encrypt to the same letter. So simple frequency analysis fails.

The Kasiski test (finding the key length)

If a repeated plaintext string lines up with the same part of the key, the ciphertext repeats too. The distance between repeats is then a multiple of the key length. Find repeated trigrams, factor the distances, and look for a common factor. Some repeats happen by chance (the notes' AVL, LRJ, QMO), so look at the majority.

Notes' example

Distances 35, 21, 21, 98, 84, 98, 84, 84, 21 are mostly multiples of 7 (distances 141, 16, 13 were chance). Key length n = 7 was confirmed by the IoC and the keyword turned out to be DICKENS.

1.4 One Time Pad

Definition

A Vigenère cipher whose key is as long as the message: ci ≡ mi + ki (mod 26). For bit strings, ci = mi ⊕ ki. If the key is chosen uniformly at random and used once, it is provably secure: every plaintext of the right length is equally likely (ciphertext AAA could be CAT or DOG with equal probability).

Disadvantages: (1) the key is as long as the message and must be shared securely; (2) truly uniform randomness is hard to generate; (3) it must never be reused.

Why reuse is fatal

If ci ≡ mi + ki and c′i ≡ m′i + ki, then ci − c′i ≡ mi − m′i (mod 26). The key has disappeared. With bits: c ⊕ c′ = m ⊕ m′. Knowing one plaintext gives the other immediately, and even without that, the combination leaks structure (the two overlaid pictures in the notes).

1.5 Index of Coincidence (IoC)

Definition and meaning

For a string s of length t, with Ni(s) the number of times letter i appears:

          Σi Ni(s)(Ni(s) − 1)
IoC(s) = ────────────────────
               t(t − 1)

Meaning: the probability that two letters picked at random from s (without replacement) are the same.

  • Uniformly random text: IoC ≈ 1/26 ≈ 0.0385.
  • English, or English under any substitution cipher: IoC ≈ 0.0656. Substitution only relabels letters, so the IoC does not change.

Worked example: IoC(MISSISSIPPI)

t = 11.  Counts: M 1, I 4, S 4, P 2
numerator   = 1·0 + 4·3 + 4·3 + 2·1 = 0 + 12 + 12 + 2 = 26
denominator = 11·10 = 110
IoC = 26/110 ≈ 0.236

Letters that appear once contribute 0. Always write the counts in a list first.

Method: find the Vigenère key length with the IoC

  1. For a guess n, split c into the partial ciphertexts s1 = c1c1+nc1+2n…, s2 = c2c2+n…, …, sn. Each si was encrypted with a single Caesar shift ki.
  2. Compute IoC(si) for every i.
  3. If all values are near 0.0656, n is (probably) the key length. If they are near 0.0385, try the next n.

In the notes, n = 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 give values around 0.03–0.04, while n = 7 gives 0.057–0.065. So n = 7.

Common trap

Multiples of the true key length also give high IoC values (n = 14 would too). Choose the smallest n with consistently high values, and check it against the Kasiski evidence.

1.6 Mutual Index of Coincidence (MutIoC)

Definition and meaning

               Σi Ni(s) Ni(r)
MutIoC(s, r) = ─────────────────      (s has length t1, r has length t2)
                   t1 t2

The probability that one letter picked from s and one picked from r are the same.

  • s and r encrypted with the same substitution key: MutIoC ≈ 0.0656.
  • Different keys: usually ≈ 0.0385.

Worked example: MutIoC(ABBA, BAAC)

s = ABBA: A 2, B 2          r = BAAC: A 2, B 1, C 1
numerator   = NA(s)NA(r) + NB(s)NB(r) + NC(s)NC(r) = 2·2 + 2·1 + 0·1 = 6
denominator = 4·4 = 16
MutIoC = 6/16 = 0.375

Unlike the IoC, there is no "−1": you pick one letter from each string.

How MutIoC finds the keyword

Partial ciphertext sj was shifted by kj. If you shift sj forward by β, it has total shift kj + β. This matches si's shift exactly when β ≡ ki − kj (mod 26). At that β the two strings share a key, so MutIoC(si, eβ(sj)) is near 0.0656. Elsewhere it is near 0.0385.

Method: keyword from a MutIoC table

  1. For each pair i < j, find the β in the table with MutIoC close to 0.0656. Write ki − kj ≡ β (mod 26).
  2. Set k1 = λ and express every kj in terms of λ using enough equations.
  3. Use the remaining equations as a consistency check. If one disagrees, that table entry was probably a chance high value; prefer the consistent majority.
  4. Try the 26 values of λ (or use "the keyword is an English word") and choose the one that gives a sensible keyword and plaintext.

The largest values in the MutIoC table are:

Sign convention

The notes define βi,j ≡ ki − kj, where eβ shifts forward. If a question's table uses another convention, the sign of every β flips. Write the convention down before using the table. A β value is a difference of key letters, never a key letter itself.

Medium1.5 Substitution with cycles[3]

A substitution key is σ = (A Q Z)(E M)(T H R), all other letters fixed. (a) Encrypt MEET HER AT TEA. (b) Decrypt MQTHR. (c) Why is this a bad key even though substitution has 26! keys?

Show model answer

(a) Move forward in the cycles: M→E, E→M, T→H, H→R, R→T, A→Q. MEET HER AT TEA → EMMH RMT QH HMQ.

(b) Move backward: M→E, Q→A, T→R, H→T, R→H. MQTHR → EARTH.

(c) It fixes 18 of the 26 letters, so most of the plaintext is visible. More generally any substitution keeps frequencies, so frequency analysis breaks it regardless of key-space size.

Medium1.6 Kasiski and IoC[4]

In a Vigenère ciphertext, the repeated trigrams occur at distances 24, 36, 60, 18 and 35. (a) What key length do you predict? (b) Describe how you would use the IoC to confirm it.

Show model answer

(b) Split the ciphertext into the 6 partial ciphertexts s1, …, s6 (every 6th letter). Compute each IoC(si). If all are close to 0.0656 (English under a substitution), n = 6 is confirmed. Unseen1.9 Explain

(a) Why should Alice avoid using the word LIKE many times when using a monoalphabetic substitution cipher? (b) Would the same advice matter for a Vigenère cipher?

Show model answer

(a) Substitution always sends the same plaintext word to the same ciphertext word. A frequently repeated 4-letter word with a distinctive pattern stands out in the ciphertext. Once guessed, it gives σ on four letters at once, speeding up frequency analysis.

(b) Less so, but still yes. Repeats of LIKE encrypt identically only when they line up with the same part of the key, which happens when their distance is a multiple of n. Those repeats are exactly what the Kasiski test uses to find the key length.

Chapter 2 · Exam Q2 (first half)Mechanical cryptography: the Enigma machine

The big picture. Enigma is a substitution cipher whose permutation changes after every key press. Vigenère varies a shift; Enigma varies a whole permutation σi ∈ S26. The exam asks two things: what each part does, and how many settings exist (a counting question).

2.1 The components, in the order the signal travels

  1. Keyboard. Pressing a letter sends an electrical signal into the machine.
  2. Plugboard (Stecker), P. Cables join pairs of letters and swap them. Unplugged letters pass through unchanged. It is a fixed substitution that swaps some pairs.
  3. Three rotors, R1,i, R2,i, R3,i. Each rotor is a wheel with fixed internal wiring that permutes A–Z. The signal passes through all three. The rotors rotate: after each key press the right rotor steps once; after 26 steps of the right rotor the middle rotor steps; after 26 steps of the middle rotor the left one steps (like an odometer). So the overall permutation changes with every letter.
  4. Reflector (Umkehrwalze), S. Pairs up all 26 letters (13 swaps) and sends the signal back.
  5. Back through the rotors and plugboard (in reverse, so their inverses act), then the lampboard lights up the ciphertext letter.
σi = P−1 R1,i−1 R2,i−1 R3,i−1 S R3,i R2,i R1,i P        (the notes' formula)

Two consequences worth stating in an answer

  • Encryption and decryption are the same operation. Because of the reflector, σi is a conjugate of S, so σi swaps letters in pairs and σi−1 = σi. Set the machine to the same key and type the ciphertext to get the plaintext. This was the design reason for the reflector.
  • No letter is ever encrypted to itself. A conjugate of S has the same cycle type (2,2,…,2), which has no fixed points. This flaw helped Bletchley Park locate known plaintext (attack details are non-examinable).

Using the machine: the key is the machine settings (plugboard, rotor choice and order, rotor starting positions, reflector). The Germans distributed daily settings in codebooks.

2.2 Counting settings (the computational part)

Formulas (alphabet of n symbols)

  • Plugboard with exactly m plugs: C(n, 2m) · (2m − 1)(2m − 3)…3·1. Choose the 2m letters that are plugged, then pair them up: the first letter has 2m − 1 possible partners, the next unpaired letter has 2m − 3, and so on.
  • Plugboard with up to m plugs: add the exactly-j counts for j = 0, 1, …, m (j = 0 contributes 1).
  • Rotors: choose r rotors from R available in order: R(R − 1)…(R − r + 1) = C(R, r)·r!. Starting positions: nr (each rotor can start in any of n positions).
  • Reflector: use the number of fixed reflectors available. If any reflector wiring were allowed (n even), there would be (n − 1)(n − 3)…3·1 choices: a plugboard with n/2 plugs.
  • Total = product of the independent choices (multiplication principle).

Worked example: the German military machine

Chapter 3 · Exam Q2 (second half)Linear Feedback Shift Registers

The big picture. The One Time Pad needs a long random key. An LFSR builds a long pseudorandom bit string from a short secret (the initial fill), which is then used like a One Time Pad. Its quality is measured by its period: how long before the output repeats.

3.1 Definition and generating the output

Definition

An LFSR of length n with taps b = (b0, b1, …, bn−1) ∈ F2n has state ai = (ai, ai+1, …, ai+n−1) and update rule

ai+n = b0ai ⊕ b1ai+1 ⊕ … ⊕ bn−1ai+n−1

At each step the state shifts left, the leftmost bit leaves, and the new bit enters on the right. The starting state a0 = (a0, …, an−1) is the initial fill. The output string is s = a0a1a2…: it starts with the initial fill itself.

Exam tip: from update rule to taps

an+6 = an+5 ⊕ an means b0 = 1 and b5 = 1, so b = (1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1). Tap bk is 1 exactly when ai+k appears on the right-hand side.

Worked example (2025 paper): fill 11010, rule ai+5 = ai ⊕ ai+2

a0…a4 = 1 1 0 1 0        (the fill is the first 5 output bits)
a5 = a0 ⊕ a2 = 1 ⊕ 0 = 1      a10 = a5 ⊕ a7 = 1 ⊕ 0 = 1
a6 = a1 ⊕ a3 = 1 ⊕ 1 = 0      a11 = a6 ⊕ a8 = 0 ⊕ 0 = 0
a7 = a2 ⊕ a4 = 0 ⊕ 0 = 0      a12 = a7 ⊕ a9 = 0 ⊕ 0 = 0
a8 = a3 ⊕ a5 = 1 ⊕ 1 = 0      a13 = a8 ⊕ a10 = 0 ⊕ 1 = 1
a9 = a4 ⊕ a6 = 0 ⊕ 0 = 0      a14 = a9 ⊕ a11 = 0 ⊕ 0 = 0
…
s = 11010 10000 10010 11001 …

Work in a column so each new bit only uses bits already written. Each new bit ai+5 uses the bits 5 and 3 places back.

Encrypting bit strings and pictures

Method

  1. Convert the message to bits (ASCII for text; for pictures, white = 0 and black = 1, reading pixels left to right, top to bottom).
  2. Generate as many output bits as there are message bits. The stream continues across rows; it does not restart at each row.
  3. ci = mi ⊕ si. Decryption is the same operation: mi = ci ⊕ si, since si ⊕ si = 0.

3.2 Periods, the companion matrix and the Main Theorem

Facts to know (state them; no proofs needed)

  • Period Per(a0): the smallest j ≥ 1 with ai+j = ai for some i. Bigger is better.
  • The zero fill gives 000… with Per = 1: a useless key (it encrypts nothing).
  • Per(a0) ≤ 2n − 1 for every fill (pigeonhole: only 2n − 1 non-zero states).
  • If b0 = 0, the first bit is never used. The LFSR is really a shorter one: s = a0…ak−1s′, where s′ comes from the LFSR with taps (bk, …, bn−1) and fill (ak, …, an−1) (Lemma 3.10). So assume b0 = 1.
  • If b0 = 1: the period is the first j with aj = a0 (the fill itself comes back), and Per(a0) divides ord(Bb).

Companion matrix and characteristic polynomial

ai+1 = aiBb, where Bb has 1s just below the diagonal and the taps down the last column. For b = (1, 0, 1, 1):

0001
1000
0101
0011

Here Bb7 = I, so every period divides 7: it is 1 or 7.

The characteristic polynomial is cb(x) = xn + bn−1xn−1 + … + b1x + b0 ∈ F2[x]. Quick rule: replace ai+k by xk in the update rule and add the left-hand side. For ai+5 = ai+2 ⊕ ai, cb(x) = x5 + x2 + 1.

The Main Theorem (Theorem 3.16)

If cb(x) is irreducible, then every non-zero initial fill has the same period j, where j is the smallest divisor of 2n − 1 with cb(x) | xj + 1.

Method: find the period with the Main Theorem

  1. Write down cb(x).
  2. Prove it is irreducible (table below). A polynomial of degree n is reducible exactly when it has an irreducible factor of degree ≤ n/2.
  3. List the divisors of 2n − 1 in increasing order.
  4. Skip any j < n: a degree-n polynomial cannot divide xj + 1 of smaller degree.
  5. Test the remaining divisors in order by long division (or a known factorisation). The first j that works is the period. If 2n − 1 is prime (n = 2, 3, 5, 7), the period is 2n − 1 straight away.
  6. State the conclusion: "Per(a0) = j for every non-zero fill a0; the zero fill has period 1."
Degree of cbTo prove irreducible, check…
2 or 3No roots: c(0) = 1 (constant term 1) and c(1) = 1 (odd number of terms)
4 or 5No roots, and not divisible by x2 + x + 1. For degree 4 this means c ≠ (x2 + x + 1)2 = x4 + x2 + 1
6No roots, not divisible by x2 + x + 1, x3 + x + 1 or x3 + x2 + 1

Irreducible polynomials over F2 up to degree 4: x, x + 1; x2 + x + 1; x3 + x + 1, x3 + x2 + 1; x4 + x + 1, x4 + x3 + 1, x4 + x3 + x2 + x + 1.

Worked example: Coursework 2 Q3, rule an+5 = an+2 ⊕ an

(a) Taps b = (1, 0, 1, 0, 0), so cb(x) = x5 + x2 + 1.

No roots: cb(0) = 1 and cb(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 1. So there is no linear factor. A reducible quintic would need a factor of degree 1 or 2, and the only irreducible quadratic is x2 + x + 1. Divide:

x5 + x2 + 1  −  x3(x2 + x + 1)  =  x4 + x3 + x2 + 1
x4 + x3 + x2 + 1  −  x2(x2 + x + 1)  =  1
Remainder 1 ≠ 0, so x2 + x + 1 does not divide cb(x).

Hence cb(x) is irreducible.

(b) By the Main Theorem, the period divides 25 − 1 = 31, which is prime, so it is 1 or 31. It is not 1, since cb (degree 5) cannot divide x + 1. So Per(a0) = 31 for every non-zero fill, the maximum possible.

Worked example (2025 paper): cb(x) = x4 + x + 1

Irreducible: c(0) = 1, c(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 1, and c ≠ x4 + x2 + 1 = (x2 + x + 1)2. Divisors of 15: 1, 3, 5, 15. Skip 1 and 3 (smaller than 4). Test 5:

x5 + 1 − x(x4 + x + 1) = x2 + x + 1 ≠ 0,  so c ∤ x5 + 1.

So the period is 15 for every non-zero fill (maximal). Check: fill 1011 gives s = 101111000100110 1011…, which repeats after 15 bits.

When the theorem does not apply: a reducible polynomial

For b = (1, 0, 1, 0), cb(x) = x4 + x2 + 1 = (x2 + x + 1)2 is reducible. Then different fills can have different periods, which need not divide 15. With ai+4 = ai ⊕ ai+2:

fill 0110:  0110 110 110 …     period 3
fill 1000:  100010 100010 …    period 6   (6 does not divide 15)

Note: the strings printed in the notes' Example 3.17(3) do not follow the stated update rule (a period-2 output is impossible for this rule). The periods above are the correct ones. The lesson is unchanged.

Exam tip: no roots is not enough

x4 + x2 + 1 has no roots in F2 but is reducible. For degree 4 or 5, always also rule out x2 + x + 1 as a factor. Also, irreducible does not mean maximal period: x4 + x3 + x2 + x + 1 is irreducible but divides x5 + 1, so its period is only 5.

Easy2.1 Enigma components[4]

Briefly describe the role of the plugboard, rotors and reflector in the Enigma machine, and explain why the same settings can be used to decrypt.

Show model answer

Plugboard: cables swap fixed pairs of letters, a substitution applied when the signal enters and leaves. Rotors: three wired wheels, each permuting the alphabet; the right rotor steps after every key press (middle every 26 steps, left every 26² steps), so the substitution changes with every letter. Reflector: pairs all 26 letters and sends the signal back through the rotors and plugboard in reverse.

Overall σi = P−1R−1SRP (rotors combined into R) is a conjugate of S, so it swaps letters in pairs and σi = σi−1. Typing the ciphertext with the same settings therefore gives back the plaintext.

Unseen3.6 Security of an LFSR stream[3]

Eve knows that a message was encrypted with an LFSR of length 5 whose taps she knows, and she knows the first 5 plaintext bits. Explain why the whole message is exposed.

Show model answer

XORing the known plaintext bits with the ciphertext bits gives si = ci ⊕ mi for i = 0, …, 4. These are exactly the initial fill. With the known update rule Eve regenerates the entire stream and decrypts everything. The output is deterministic, not random, so an LFSR does not have the One Time Pad's perfect security. (Even with unknown taps, about 2n known bits give enough linear equations to find them.)

4.1 Primitive roots and Diffie–Hellman key exchange

Primitive roots

(Z/pZ)× = {1, …, p − 1} is cyclic. g is a primitive root if every element is a power of g, i.e. g has order p − 1.

Quick test: g is a primitive root mod p if and only if g(p−1)/q ≢ 1 for every prime q dividing p − 1. (You can also list powers, as the notes do.)

Worked example: is 5 a primitive root mod 23?

p − 1 = 22 = 2·11. Check 522/2 = 511 and 522/11 = 52.
52 = 25 ≡ 2 ≠ 1
54 ≡ 4, 58 ≡ 16, 511 = 58·52·5 ≡ 16·2·5 = 160 ≡ 22 ≡ −1 ≠ 1
So 5 is a primitive root mod 23.

By contrast 2 is not a primitive root mod 7: 23 = 8 ≡ 1, so its powers are only 2, 4, 1.

Diffie–Hellman key exchange memorise

  1. Alice and Bob publicly agree a prime p and a primitive root g ∈ (Z/pZ)×.
  2. Alice picks a private kA and sends cA = gkA to Bob.
  3. Bob picks a private kB and sends cB = gkB to Alice.
  4. Alice computes cBkA, Bob computes cAkB. Both equal gkAkB, the shared key.

Worked example: p = 23, g = 5, kA = 6, kB = 15

Powers of 5 mod 23:  52 ≡ 2,  54 ≡ 4,  58 ≡ 16
cA = 56  = 54·52 ≡ 4·2 = 8
cB = 515 = 58·54·52·5 ≡ 16·4·2·5 = 640 ≡ 640 − 27·23 = 19
Alice: 196 ≡ (−4)6.  (−4)2 = 16,  (−4)4 = 256 ≡ 3,  (−4)6 ≡ 3·16 = 48 ≡ 2
Bob:   815.  82 = 64 ≡ −5,  84 ≡ 25 ≡ 2,  88 ≡ 4
       815 = 88·84·82·8 ≡ 4·2·(−5)·8 = −320 ≡ −320 + 14·23 = 2
Shared key: 2 ✓ (both agree)

Security: what Eve faces

Eve knows p, g, gkA, gkB. Diffie–Hellman Problem: from gx and gy, find gxy. If she could solve the Discrete Log Problem (given p, g, h, find x = dlogg(h) with gx = h), she could recover kA and then the key. dlogg(h) is only defined mod p − 1. For large p (600–700 digits) the best known algorithms are sub-exponential, so this is infeasible.

Example: 2 is a primitive root mod 11 with powers 2, 4, 8, 5, 10, 9, 7, 3, 6, 1, so dlog2(3) = 8 and dlog2(10) = 5.

4.2 RSA

Background

(Z/NZ)× = {a : gcd(a, N) = 1}; ϕ(N) = |(Z/NZ)×|; for distinct primes, ϕ(pq) = (p − 1)(q − 1). Fermat–Euler: aϕ(N) ≡ 1 (mod N) when gcd(a, N) = 1. So if ed ≡ 1 (mod ϕ(N)), then (me)d = m1 + kϕ(N) = m: the maps fe(m) = me and fd are inverse to each other.

RSA encryption and decryption memorise

  1. Key generation (Alice): secret distinct primes p, q; N = pq; choose e with gcd(e, (p − 1)(q − 1)) = 1.
  2. Public key (N, e). Private key (p, q, d), where d ≡ e−1 (mod (p − 1)(q − 1)), found by Extended Euclid.
  3. Encrypt (Bob): c ≡ me (mod N).
  4. Decrypt (Alice): m ≡ cd (mod N).

Encrypt.

Security

RSA Problem: from (N, e) and c = me, recover m. The only known route is to find d, which needs ϕ(N) = (p − 1)(q − 1), which needs p and q. So RSA is broken by the Semiprime Factorisation Problem: given N = pq, find p, q. Real primes are 300–400 digits; the best factoring algorithms are sub-exponential, too slow at that size.

4.3 El-Gamal encryption on the Useful Information sheet

El-Gamal

  1. Key generation (Alice): finite cyclic group G of size q with generator g; secret 1 ≤ k ≤ q; h = gk. Public key (G, q, g, h); private key k.
  2. Encrypt (Bob): choose a secret 1 ≤ s ≤ q; send c1 = gs, c2 = m·hs.
  3. Decrypt (Alice): m = c2·c1−k. (It works because c1k = gsk = hs.)

Worked example: G = (Z/23Z)×, q = 22, g = 5, k = 6, m = 10, s = 3

Public key: h = 56 ≡ 8, so (G, q, g, h) = (G, 22, 5, 8)
Encrypt:  c1 = 53 = 125 ≡ 10
          h3 = 512 ≡ 6,  c2 = 10·6 = 60 ≡ 14        send (10, 14)
Decrypt:  c1k = 106:  102 ≡ 8, 104 ≡ 64 ≡ −5, 106 ≡ 8·(−5) = −40 ≡ 6
          6−1 ≡ 4 (since 6·4 = 24 ≡ 1)
          m = 14·4 = 56 ≡ 10 ✓

Alternative: c1−k = c1q−k = 1016, which avoids computing an inverse.

Security and choice of group

Eve sees G, q, g, h, c1, c2. To get m she needs k = dlogg(h): the Discrete Log Problem in G. The presentation of G matters. In G = Z/NZ under addition, "gk" means kg, so the DLP is just kg ≡ h (mod N), solved instantly by k ≡ g−1h (e.g. dlog7(9) = 5 in Z/13Z, since 7−1·9 ≡ 2·9 = 18 ≡ 5). Never use an additive group. (Z/pZ)× and elliptic curve groups E(Fp) are good choices. q is included in the public key because |G| may not be obvious.

4.4 Digital signatures

A signature proves the sender knows their private key without revealing it. The signer uses their own private key. The receiver verifies with the signer's public key.

RSA signatures memorise

  1. Alice has public (N, e) and private d.
  2. Given an auxiliary message m0 ∈ (Z/NZ)× (a challenge from Bob, or in practice a hash H(m)), Alice computes m1 = m0d mod N and sends (m0, m1) alongside her message (which is encrypted with Bob's public key).
  3. Bob verifies with Alice's public key: m1e ≡ m0 (mod N)?

Common traps in signatures

  • Signing uses the signer's private key; verifying uses the signer's public key, not the receiver's.
  • In El-Gamal, m1 is computed mod p but m2 mod q = p − 1. Use the integer m1 (e.g. 65) inside m0 − km1.
  • s must be coprime to q, otherwise s−1 does not exist.
  • In a combined question (encrypt with Bob's key, sign with Alice's), keep two separate lists of parameters.

Forgery security: to forge an El-Gamal signature Eve needs a, b, c with hbbc ≡ ga, i.e. b·dlogg(h) + c·dlogg(b) ≡ a (mod p − 1), which needs dlogg(h): a DLP. In practice m0 = H(m) for a hash function H (short, fixed length, hard to invert or forge).

RSA weaknesses

  1. Small e and small message (Coursework 3 Q3). If me < N, then c = me exactly as integers: there is no wrap-around mod N. Eve takes the ordinary integer e-th root of c. With m < 1030 and e ≤ (1/30)⌊log10N⌋: me < 1030e ≤ 10⌊log10N⌋ ≤ N. So every record leaked, despite N being unfactorisable.
  2. Shared prime. If two users' moduli share a prime, gcd(N1, N2) (Euclid, instant) reveals it. E.g. gcd(221, 323) = 17, so 221 = 13·17 and 323 = 17·19, and both private keys follow.
  3. Primes too close together. Fermat's method (Chapter 5) finds N + b2 = a2 with small b = (q − p)/2.
  4. Knowing ϕ(N) (or d) is as good as factoring. p + q = N − ϕ(N) + 1 and pq = N, so p, q are the roots of x2 − (N − ϕ(N) + 1)x + N. Example N = 299, ϕ = 264: p + q = 36, x2 − 36x + 299 = 0, x = (36 ± √(1296 − 1196))/2 = (36 ± 10)/2 = 23, 13.
  5. Same message, same N, two coprime exponents (common modulus). If c1 = me1, c2 = me2 and ue1 + ve2 = 1, then m = c1uc2v. Example below.
  6. Invalid or trivial keys. gcd(e, ϕ(N)) ≠ 1 means no d exists (e = 6 with ϕ = 40). e = 1 means c = m. Messages 0, 1 and N − 1 are always fixed by any odd e.
  7. Signature misuse. (a) Without a hash, anyone can make a valid pair: choose m1 and set m0 = m1e (with (33, 3): m1 = 2 gives the valid pair (8, 2)). (b) If Alice signs any challenge with the same key she decrypts with, Eve can send an intercepted ciphertext c as the "challenge": Alice returns cd = m, decrypting it for Eve. This is why the notes suggest a separate key pair for signing.

Diffie–Hellman and El-Gamal weaknesses

  1. g not a primitive root (small order). If g has small order r, then gk takes only r values, so Eve brute-forces the shared key or the private key among r possibilities. Mod 7, g = 2 gives only {1, 2, 4}.
  2. Small p or small private keys: brute force or Baby-Step Giant-Step solves the DLP.
  3. Additive group Z/NZ: DLP solved by one modular inverse (shown above).
  4. Reusing s in El-Gamal encryption. Both ciphertexts have the same c1 and the same mask hs, so c2′·c2−1 = m′·m−1. If Eve knows one plaintext, she gets the other: m′ = c2′·c2−1·m.
  5. Reusing s in El-Gamal signatures. Same m1 in both. Subtracting, m2 − m2′ ≡ (m0 − m0′)s−1 (mod q) reveals s, and then m2s ≡ m0 − km1 reveals the private key k (practice question 4.8).
  6. Quantum computers: Shor's algorithm solves factoring and discrete logs in polynomial time, so RSA, Diffie–Hellman and El-Gamal would all fall (motivation for Chapter 7).

Chapter 5 · Exam Q4 (first half)Factorisation methods

The big picture. RSA is only as safe as factoring N = pq is hard. Each method below works well for small or badly chosen N, which tells us how to choose keys. Almost all of them rest on one idea: find a2 ≡ b2 (mod N) with a ≢ ±b, and then gcd(a ± b, N) is p or q.

5.1 Trial division

Test divisibility by each prime p ≤ √N (a composite N always has a prime factor ≤ √N). Example: N = 221, √221 ≈ 14.9. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 do not divide; 13 does: 221 = 13 · 17. There are about √N / log √N primes to test, hopeless for large N.

5.2 Fermat's method memorise

Method

  1. For b = 1, 2, 3, …, compute N + b2.
  2. Stop when N + b2 = a2 is a perfect square (check: is √(N + b2) a whole number?).
  3. Then N = a2 − b2 = (a − b)(a + b), so p = a − b and q = a + b.

Excluded: the trivial solution (a, b) = ((N + 1)/2, (N − 1)/2), which gives 1 · N.

Worked example: N = 1147

b = 1:  1148   (332 = 1089, 342 = 1156: not a square)
b = 2:  1151   not a square
b = 3:  1156 = 342  ✓
N = 342 − 32 = (34 − 3)(34 + 3) = 31 · 37

Why it matters for RSA

The successful b is (q − p)/2. If p and q are close, b is small and Fermat finds it almost immediately, whatever the size of p and q. So RSA primes must be large and not close together. (8051 = 83 · 97 falls at b = 7: 8051 + 49 = 8100 = 902.)

Fermat mod N: more generally, any a, b with a2 ≡ b2 (mod N) and a ≢ ±b gives gcd(a ± b, N) ∈ {p, q}. Example: 122 = 144 ≡ 1 = 12 (mod 143) and 12 ≢ ±1, so gcd(11, 143) = 11 and gcd(13, 143) = 13.

5.3 Pollard rho for factoring memorise

Method

  1. Let f(x) = x2 + 1 mod N. Start with x0 = y0 (e.g. 2).
  2. Each step: xi = f(xi−1) (one step), yi = f(f(yi−1)) (two steps). So yi = x2i.
  3. At each step compute gcd(xi − yi, N) and gcd(xi + yi, N). Stop when one lies strictly between 1 and N.

Why it works: mod p the values eventually repeat (a "ρ" shape), so xi ≡ yi (mod p) while usually not mod N. Then p divides xi − yi, and the gcd reveals it. Expected time is around √p steps.

5.4 Dixon's method on the sheet

Method

  1. Choose a smoothness bound B; the factor base is the primes ≤ B. (n is B-smooth if all its prime factors are ≤ B.)
  2. For chosen am > √N, compute bm ≡ am2 (mod N) and factorise. Discard any bm that is not B-smooth.
  3. Write each exponent vector mod 2 as a column of a matrix E (rows = primes).
  4. Find a non-zero v with Ev = 0 (mod 2): a set of relations whose exponents add up to even numbers.
  5. Let a = ∏ am and b = √(∏ bm) over that set. Halve the total exponents to get b. Reduce both mod N.
  6. Compute gcd(a − b, N). If it is 1 or N, try another null vector or more relations.

Common traps in Dixon

  • The linear algebra is mod 2, not mod N.
  • b is the square root of the product of the residues: halve the true exponents (not the parities).

  • Trade-off: smaller B means fewer smooth numbers; larger B means bigger matrices.

Chapter 6 · Exam Q4 (second half)Discrete log methods

The big picture. Diffie–Hellman and El-Gamal are only as safe as the DLP: given g and h = gk in a cyclic group G, find k (mod |G|). Brute force tries every k, which takes O(|G|) steps. BSGS and Pollard rho take about O(√|G|). Index calculus is faster still, but only works in (Z/pZ)×.

6.1 Baby-Step Giant-Step memorise

Method (solve gk = h in G with |G| = q; for (Z/pZ)×, q = p − 1)

  1. m = ⌈√q⌉ (round up).
  2. Baby steps: list gi for i = 0, 1, …, m − 1.
  3. Compute x = g−m (by Euclid, or as gq−m).
  4. Giant steps: list h·xj for j = 0, 1, 2, … (multiply by x each time), checking each against the baby list.
  5. When h·xj = gi, then h = gi + jm, so k = i + jm (mod q). Check it.

6.2 Pollard rho for discrete logs on the sheet

Method (G = (Z/pZ)×)

  1. Track x = gahb together with (a, b). Start x0 = ga0hb0 (e.g. a0 = b0 = 1).
  2. Apply f according to which third x lies in:
    • 1 ≤ x < p/3: x ↦ gx, (a, b) ↦ (a + 1, b)
    • p/3 < x < 2p/3: x ↦ x2, (a, b) ↦ (2a, 2b)
    • 2p/3 < x ≤ p − 1: x ↦ hx, (a, b) ↦ (a, b + 1)
    Reduce x mod p and a, b mod p − 1.
  3. Stop at a collision xi = xj. Then gaihbi = gajhbj, giving (bi − bj)k ≡ aj − ai (mod p − 1).
  4. Solve the congruence (toolkit T4). If gcd(bi − bj, p − 1) = t > 1, you get t candidates. Test each in gk = h.

6.3 Index calculus on the sheet

Method (G = (Z/pZ)×, generator g, target h, bound B)

Step 1: dlogs of the small primes.

  1. Compute powers ga mod p and keep those that are B-smooth: ga ≡ ∏ piei.
  2. Each gives a linear equation mod p − 1: a ≡ Σ ei·dlogg(pi).
  3. Solve the system (rows = relations) for d = (dlogg2, dlogg3, …), working mod p − 1.

Step 2: the target.

  1. Find s with h·gs mod p B-smooth: h·gs ≡ ∏ piei.
  2. Then dlogg(h) ≡ Σ ei·dlogg(pi) − s (mod p − 1).

Common traps in Chapter 6

  • Exponent equations live mod p − 1, never mod p.
  • BSGS: m = ⌈√q⌉ with q = p − 1, not √p. Rounding down can miss the answer.
  • Pollard rho: when the coefficient shares a factor with p − 1, there are several candidates. List them all and test each.
  • Division mod p − 1 (even!) needs care: 2 is never invertible mod p − 1.

Unseen5.4 Compare methods[4]

(a) Why must RSA primes not be close together? (b) Compare brute force and BSGS for the DLP. (c) Why does index calculus not work in an arbitrary cyclic group?

Show model answer

(a) Fermat's method succeeds at b = (q − p)/2, so close primes are found after very few steps, regardless of their size.

(b) Brute force tests up to |G| exponents: O(|G|) time. BSGS uses two lists of length m = ⌈√|G|⌉: O(√|G|) time, but also O(√|G|) memory. (Pollard rho also takes about √|G| steps but needs little memory.) Both are still exponential in the number of digits, so large groups are safe.

(c) It needs to factor group elements into small primes (smoothness). Elements of (Z/pZ)× are integers with prime factorisations; a general group (matrices, elliptic curve points, permutations) has no such notion.

Chapter 7 · Exam Q5Post-quantum cryptography

The big picture. Shor's algorithm on a quantum computer would solve factoring and discrete logs in polynomial time, breaking RSA, Diffie–Hellman and El-Gamal. The schemes here rest instead on problems about short vectors in lattices, which are believed hard even for quantum computers in high dimension. The two schemes in this chapter are toy versions that turn out to be insecure, and seeing why is part of the exam.

7.1 The Knapsack Problem and superincreasing sequences

Definitions

Knapsack Problem: given a1 < a2 < … < an and a sum s of some of them, find which ones. For a general sequence there are 2n subsets to check: hard.

Superincreasing: each term is bigger than the sum of all earlier terms: am+1 > a1 + … + am. Then the problem is easy (greedy algorithm).

Method: greedy algorithm (superincreasing sequences only)

  1. Take the largest ai ≤ s. Include it, and replace s by s − ai.
  2. Repeat with the remaining smaller terms until s = 0.
  3. If you get stuck with s > 0, there was no solution.

Worked examples

Is 1, 4, 6, 20, 34, 70 superincreasing?  4 > 1, 6 > 5, 20 > 11, 34 > 31, 70 > 65  ✓ yes
Is 1, 4, 6, 20, 30, 70 superincreasing?  30 < 1 + 4 + 6 + 20 = 31  ✗ no

Solve s = 53 for 3, 5, 9, 20, 41:
  41 ≤ 53, take it: 53 − 41 = 12
  20 > 12, skip;  9 ≤ 12, take it: 12 − 9 = 3
  5 > 3, skip;  3 ≤ 3, take it: 0
  53 = 3 + 9 + 41,  bit string (1, 0, 1, 0, 1)

The greedy method can fail for non-superincreasing sequences: for 2, 3, 4 and s = 5 it takes 4 and gets stuck at 1, but 5 = 2 + 3.

Knapsack scheme on the sheet

Scheme

  1. Bob's keys: a superincreasing a1 < … < an, a modulus M > a1 + … + an, and w with gcd(w, M) = 1. Public key: bt ≡ w·at (mod M). Private key: (M, w) (and the at).
  2. Encrypt (Alice): message as bits x = (x1, …, xn); send c = Σ xtbt (an ordinary sum, no reduction).
  3. Decrypt (Bob): c′ ≡ w−1c (mod M), then solve the easy knapsack for a1, …, an and s = c′.

Why it works: c′ ≡ Σ xtw−1bt ≡ Σ xtat (mod M), and since M exceeds every possible sum, c′ equals Σ xtat exactly.

Worked example (notes): a = (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32), M = 65, w = 11

Public key: 11, 22, 44, 88 ≡ 23, 176 ≡ 46, 352 ≡ 27   →  (11, 22, 44, 23, 46, 27)
Alice sends x = 100110:  c = 11 + 23 + 46 = 80
Bob: 11−1 mod 65 = 6 (66 ≡ 1).  c′ = 6·80 = 480 ≡ 480 − 7·65 = 25
Greedy on 25: 16 (left 9), 8 (left 1), 1 (left 0)  →  bits for 1, 8, 16  →  x = 100110 ✓

Knapsack as a short vector problem

For public b1, …, bn and target s = Σ xtbt, take the rows of

r1   = (2, 0, 0, …, 0, b1)
r2   = (0, 2, 0, …, 0, b2)
 ⋮
rn   = (0, 0, 0, …, 2, bn)
rn+1 = (1, 1, 1, …, 1, s)

The vector v = Σ xtrt − rn+1 = (2x1 − 1, …, 2xn − 1, 0) lies in the lattice L they span. Every entry is ±1 (last entry 0), so ‖v‖ = √n: very short compared with the rows. Finding the shortest vector of L reveals the bits xt. Lattice reduction algorithms do this well for the dimensions needed in practice (n + 1 > 300), so the Knapsack scheme is insecure. It does show lattice problems are useful in cryptography.

7.2 The Simple Noisy Scheme memorise

Idea: "n is close to a multiple of s" is easy over Z (divide and round), but mod p with a large random-looking multiplier it looks hard. Alice keeps a small s secret and publishes a random-looking v ≡ st−1. Multiplying by t turns the hard problem back into the easy one.

Scheme

  1. Alice's private key: a large prime p and coprime integers (s, t) with √(p/4) < s < √(p/2) and 1 < t < √(p/2).
  2. Public key: (p, v) with v ≡ s·t−1 (mod p).
  3. Encrypt (Bob): turn the message into small noise r < √(p/4), pick a random 1 < a < √(p/2), and send e ≡ av + r (mod p).
  4. Decrypt (Alice): b ≡ t·e (mod p) with 0 < b < p, then r ≡ t−1·b (mod s). Note: t−1 here is the inverse mod s, a different inverse from the one in step 2.

Why it works: te ≡ t(ast−1 + r) ≡ as + tr (mod p), and the size bounds force 0 < as + tr < p, so b = as + tr exactly. Reducing mod s leaves tr, and multiplying by t−1 mod s gives r (since 0 ≤ r < s).

Worked example (2025 paper): p = 199, (s, t) = (8, 3), a = 6, r = 2

Bounds: √(199/4) ≈ 7.05 < 8 < √(199/2) ≈ 9.97 ✓;  1 < 3 < 9.97 ✓;  gcd(8, 3) = 1 ✓
Public key: 199 = 66·3 + 1 ⇒ 3−1 ≡ −66 ≡ 133 (mod 199)
            v ≡ 8·133 = 1064 = 5·199 + 69 ≡ 69.   Public key (199, 69)
Encrypt:    e ≡ 6·69 + 2 = 416 = 2·199 + 18 ≡ 18
Decrypt:    b ≡ 3·18 = 54          (and indeed 54 = 6·8 + 3·2 = as + tr)

7.3 Lattices and Gauss reduction memorise

Definitions

A lattice L spanned by a basis v1, …, vn of Rn is the set of integer combinations α1v1 + … + αnvn. The same lattice has many bases. A "nice" basis has short, nearly orthogonal vectors. Shortest Vector Problem (SVP): find a shortest non-zero vector in L. It is hard in high dimension (believed quantum secure), but in dimension 2 Gauss reduction solves it.

Method: Gauss reduction (like Euclid's algorithm for vectors)

  1. Order the basis so that ‖v1‖ ≤ ‖v2‖ (compare squared lengths).
  2. Compute μ = (v1·v2)/‖v1‖2 and round to the nearest integer α = ⌊μ⌉.
  3. If α = 0, stop: v1 is a shortest vector. Otherwise replace v2 by v2 − αv1 and go back to step 1.

At the end, ‖w1‖ ≤ ‖w2‖ and |w1·w2| ≤ ½‖w1‖2, so |cos θ| ≤ ½ (the vectors are between 60° and 120° apart), and w1 solves the SVP. (The notes print the angle range as 2π/3 ≤ θ ≤ 4π/3; the inequality |cos θ| ≤ ½ in their own proof gives π/3 ≤ θ ≤ 2π/3.)

Rounding

Round to the nearest integer, including for negatives: −7.60 rounds to −8, not −7. Exactly half (like −3.5) may go either way; both choices are valid.

Breaking the noisy scheme with Gauss reduction

Method

  1. Eve needs any coprime S, T with 1 < S, T ≤ √(p/2) and v ≡ ST−1, i.e. Tv = S + Rp for some integer R.
  2. Then (S, T) = T(v, 1) − R(p, 0) lies in the lattice L spanned by (v, 1) and (p, 0), and it is short: ‖(S, T)‖ ≤ √p, while the basis vectors have length about p.
  3. Gauss-reduce the basis (v, 1), (p, 0). The shortest vector (up to sign) is (S, T). Check Tv ≡ S (mod p).
  4. Decrypt exactly as Alice would, using (S, T).

Worked example (2025 paper): public key (p, v) = (199, 69)

Start: v1 = (69, 1), ‖v1‖2 = 4762;  v2 = (199, 0)

  v2 − 3v1 = (199 − 207, 0 − 3) = (−8, −3),  ‖·‖2 = 73  (shorter: swap)

Unseen7.6 Why the conditions?[4]

(a) In the Knapsack scheme, what goes wrong if M ≤ a1 + … + an, or if gcd(w, M) ≠ 1? (b) In the noisy scheme, what goes wrong if r or a are too large?

Show model answer

(a) If M is too small, Σ xtat can exceed M, so c′ (reduced mod M) is no longer the true sum and the greedy algorithm returns the wrong bits (or none). If gcd(w, M) ≠ 1, w−1 mod M does not exist, so Bob cannot undo the disguise, and different messages can collide.

(b) The proof needs 0 < as + tr < p so that b = as + tr exactly. With r or a too large, as + tr can exceed p, b wraps around mod p, and r ≡ t−1b (mod s) gives a wrong value. Also r must be less than s, since decryption only recovers r mod s.

1(e) [6]

Explain why the One Time Pad is perfectly secure, and give three reasons it is rarely used in practice.

Show model answer

With a uniformly random key as long as the message, every plaintext of that length is produced by exactly one key, each equally likely. So every plaintext is equally likely given the ciphertext, and the ciphertext reveals nothing. Practical problems: (1) the key is as long as the message and must be shared securely in advance; (2) generating truly uniform randomness is hard; (3) a key can only be used once, since reuse gives c − c′ ≡ m − m′ and leaks the plaintexts.

5(d) [2]

Why are cryptographers interested in lattice-based schemes, even though these two are insecure?

Show model answer

Shor's algorithm would let a large quantum computer factor and solve discrete logs in polynomial time, breaking RSA, Diffie–Hellman and El-Gamal. The Shortest (and Closest) Vector Problems in high-dimensional lattices have no known efficient quantum algorithm. These toy schemes fail only because their lattices have small rank (2 here), where Gauss reduction (or lattice reduction for the Knapsack) succeeds; schemes like NTRU use high-dimensional lattices.

The ten mistakes that cost the most marks

  1. Reducing exponents mod p instead of mod p − 1 (or mod N instead of ϕ(N)).
  2. Verifying a signature with the receiver's key instead of the signer's.
  3. Reading a MutIoC β as a key letter rather than a difference ki − kj.
  4. Forgetting the LFSR output starts with the initial fill, or restarting the stream at each picture row.
  5. Claiming irreducibility of a quartic or quintic from "no roots" alone.
  6. Forgetting rotor order or starting positions in Enigma counts.
  7. Using m = ⌊√q⌋ in BSGS, or √p instead of √(p − 1).
  8. Dividing by a non-unit in a congruence mod p − 1 instead of handling the gcd.
  9. Rounding −7.6 to −7 in Gauss reduction.
  10. Not checking the final answer: one substitution back into the original equation catches most errors.
