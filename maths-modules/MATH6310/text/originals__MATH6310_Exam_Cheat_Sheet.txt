1     Metrics, balls and sequences

    Definitions

    Metric: finite d : E 2 → [0, ∞), d(x, y) = 0 ⇐⇒ x = y, symmetry, and d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z).
    Norm: definite, homogeneous ∥λx∥ = |λ| ∥x∥, and ∥x + y∥ ≤ ∥x∥ + ∥y∥. Induced metric d(x, y) = ∥x − y∥.

                               Br (a) = {x : d(x, a) < r},       Br [a] = {x : d(x, a) ≤ r}.

                                 diam A = sup d(x, y),           dist(x, A) = inf d(x, a).
                                            x,y∈A                             a∈A

    The distance-to-set formula assumes A ̸= ∅. Bounded means finite diameter, equivalently containment in a
    finite-radius ball.
                                   xn → a : ∀ε > 0 ∃N ∀n ≥ N : d(xn , a) < ε.
                                 Cauchy : ∀ε > 0 ∃N ∀n, m ≥ N : d(xn , xm ) < ε.
    Convergent implies Cauchy implies bounded. Limits are unique. Cauchy plus a convergent subsequence
    implies convergence of the full sequence to the same limit.


    Method: Distance estimates and local radii

                         |d(x, p) − d(y, p)| ≤ d(x, y),   |d(a, b) − d(c, e)| ≤ d(a, c) + d(b, e).
    Derive one side using triangle inequality, then swap roles. The distance-to-set function is 1-Lipschitz. For
    p ∈ BR (a), the radius r = R − d(p, a) > 0 satisfies Br (p) ⊆ BR (a). For an intersection of two balls use the
    minimum of their two spare radii.


    Exam question: 2024 Q1: discrete convergence

    Why does convergence in a discrete metric space mean eventual constancy?


    Worked model answer
    If xn → p, use ε = 1/2. Eventually d(xn , p) < 1/2, so the discrete distance is zero and xn = p. Conversely
    an eventually constant sequence has eventually zero distance from its eventual value, hence converges. The
    same argument for pairs shows discrete Cauchy sequences are eventually constant, so every discrete space is
    complete.


    Exam tips and common mistakes

    Discrete open balls: singleton for 0 < r ≤ 1, whole space for r > 1. Closed balls: singleton for r < 1,
    whole space for r ≥ 1. A metric-verification proof must check finiteness and every axiom, not just triangle
    inequality.

2     Topology, subspaces and continuity

    Definitions and identities
    Open: ∀p ∈ U ∃r > 0 : Br (p) ⊆ U . Closed: limits in the ambient space of sequences from F stay in F .
    Closed iff complement open.

                            p ∈ A ⇐⇒ ∀r > 0 : Br (p) ∩ A ̸= ∅ ⇐⇒ ∃an ∈ A : an → p.

    For nonempty A this also means dist(p, A) = 0.

                                Int A = {p : ∃r > 0 : Br (p) ⊆ A},   ∂A = A \ Int A.

                                       ∂A = A ∩ E \ A,     E \ A = Int(E \ A).
    Open sets: arbitrary unions, finite intersections. Closed sets: arbitrary intersections, finite unions. A ∪ B =
    A ∪ B, but only A ∩ B ⊆ A ∩ B in general.


    Relative topology

    dA = dE |A×A and BrA (a) = A ∩ BrE (a).
    V open in A iff V = A ∩ U for an ambient open U ; F closed in A iff F = A ∩ C for ambient closed C. The
    set [0, 1) is both open and closed in itself, neither in usual R. Always state the ambient space.


    Continuity tests

    At p: given ε > 0, find δ > 0 such that d(x, p) < δ implies d(f (x), f (p)) < ε. Equivalent to preserving
    sequence limits at p.
    Globally continuous iff every open preimage is open, iff every closed preimage is closed. Lipschitz implies
    uniformly continuous implies continuous. Uniform continuity uses one δ for all pairs at each tolerance.
    Isometry preserves distances exactly and is injective. A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection with
    continuous inverse. A continuous bijection from a sequentially compact space into a metric space is a
    homeomorphism onto its image.


    Exam question: 2025 Q3(f): open preimages imply continuity

    Prove this direction of the open-preimage theorem.


    Worked model answer

    Fix p and ε > 0. The set W = f −1 [Bε (f (p))] is open and contains p. Therefore some δ > 0 satisfies
    Bδ (p) ⊆ W . For d(x, p) < δ, membership in this preimage gives d(f (x), f (p)) < ε. This proves continuity at
    arbitrary p.


    Exam tips and common mistakes

    To disprove openness, one bad point suffices. To disprove closedness, use a sequence with a missing limit.
    To disprove continuity, use a convergent sequence whose images have the wrong limit. Images of open sets
    need not be open under continuous maps: a constant function is a counterexample.

3      Density, countability and metric equivalence

    Density and separability

    D dense in E iff D = E iff every nonempty open ball meets D. Separable means admitting a countable
    dense subset. Use Qn in Rn . In sup-metric C([0, 1]), use dyadic piecewise-linear functions with rational
    vertex values: countable by finite rational tuples, dense by uniform continuity and interpolation.
    Countable means admitting an injection into N, including finite sets. Countable unions and finite products
    of countable sets are countable. To prove binary sequence space uncountable, suppose rows s(n) enumerate
                           (n)
    it and form bn = 1 − sn . The new sequence differs from every row, contradiction.


    Exam question: 2023 Q3(a): dense intersect open dense

    Prove D ∩ U dense if D dense and U open dense.


    Worked model answer
    Fix any Br (p). Density of U gives u ∈ Br (p) ∩ U . This intersection is open, so contains a ball Bs (u). Density
    of D provides a point of D in that smaller ball, hence in Br (p) ∩ U ∩ D. Thus every ball meets D ∩ U .
    Without openness the claim fails: rationals and irrationals are disjoint dense sets.


    Three comparisons

    Topological equivalence: same open sets, or same convergent sequences with the same limits. Cauchy
    equivalence: same Cauchy sequences; also implies topological equivalence by interleaving a convergent
    sequence with its limit.
    Lipschitz equivalence: c d ≤ d′ ≤ C d for constants c, C > 0. This preserves boundedness, Cauchyness and
    completeness. Topological equivalence preserves sequential compactness but need not preserve completeness
    or boundedness.
    Finite-dimensional norms satisfy ∥x∥∞ ≤ ∥x∥p ≤ n1/p ∥x∥∞ , hence all ℓp metrics there are Lipschitz
    equivalent.


    Method: Transformed metrics
    If α is definite, nondecreasing and subadditive, α ◦ d is a metric. If also continuous at zero, the standard
    examples preserve topology and Cauchy sequences: min(1, t), t/(1 + t), arctan t, t1/p for p ≥ 1.
    To disprove Lipschitz equivalence, make a distance ratio tend to zero or infinity. To disprove Cauchy
    equivalence, exhibit one Cauchy mismatch. For dN S (x, y) = | arctan x − arctan y|, (n) is Cauchy because
    arctangents converge; it is not usual-metric Cauchy. Thus this topologically equivalent metric changes
    completeness.


    Exam tips and common mistakes

    min(1, d) preserves Cauchy sequences but cannot be Lipschitz equivalent to an unbounded d. Do not confuse
    arctan(d(x, y)) with the pullback | arctan x − arctan y|.

4      Completeness and contraction methods

    Completeness shortcuts

    Complete means every Cauchy sequence converges inside the space. A complete subset is closed in any
    metric ambient space. A closed subset of a complete space is complete.
    For complete E, ℓ∞ (S; E) is complete in the sup metric. Proof structure: pointwise Cauchy sequences give
    pointwise limits; pass to the limit in the uniform Cauchy bound; prove the limit bounded by comparison
    with one fixed bounded term.
    Uniform limits of continuous maps are continuous. Choose fN uniformly within ε/3 of f , then use continuity
    of the fixed fN to bound the middle term in

                            d(f (x), f (p)) ≤ d(f (x), fN (x)) + d(fN (x), fN (p)) + d(fN (p), f (p)).

    Thus Cb (X; E) is complete when E is. On sequentially compact K, continuous maps are bounded, so
    C(K; E) = Cb (K; E).


    Banach’s theorem and estimates
    On a nonempty complete space, a self-map T satisfying d(T x, T y) ≤ qd(x, y) for one 0 ≤ q < 1 has exactly
    one fixed point p. From any start, xn+1 = T xn converges to p and

                                                   qn                                      d(xn , xn+1 )
                                    d(xn , p) ≤       d(x0 , x1 ),           d(xn , p) ≤                 .
                                                  1−q                                         1−q

    Geometric sums make the iterates Cauchy; completeness gives a limit; continuity makes it fixed; d(p, r) ≤
    qd(p, r) proves uniqueness.
    If T K is a contraction, its fixed point p also satisfies T p = p because T K (T p) = T (T K p) = T p. Each
    sequence T mK+r x tends to p; finitely many remainders give convergence of the full iteration.

5     Sequential compactness and products

    The main results
    Sequentially compact means every sequence has a subsequence converging to a point of the set. This implies
    closed, bounded and complete. Closed subsets of sequentially compact spaces are sequentially compact.
    Finite unions and intersections of such subsets remain sequentially compact.
    Continuous images preserve sequential compactness: lift an image sequence to the domain, extract a
    subsequence, apply continuity. Real-valued continuous functions on nonempty compact sets attain extrema.
    Distance to a point and diameter are attained on nonempty compact sets.
    Heine–Borel: in finite-dimensional Rn under any ℓp metric, sequentially compact iff closed and bounded. To
    prove the reverse direction, enclose the set in a compact box and use closedness.


    Products and the diagonal method

    A countable product metric is                   X
                                        D(x, y) =         2−i min{1, di (xi , yi )}.
                                                    i≥1

    Convergence and Cauchyness are coordinatewise. Products of complete factors are complete. Products of
    sequentially compact factors are sequentially compact: take successive subsequences making coordinate 1,
    then 2, then 3, etc. converge, then select the diagonal.
    To turn coordinatewise convergence into product convergence, make the weighted tail < ε/2 and control the
    finitely many initial coordinates to contribute < ε/2. Never take a maximum over infinitely many coordinate
    cutoffs.

    Total boundedness
    For every r > 0, finitely many radius-r balls cover the set. It implies boundedness: a finite radius-1 cover
    bounds every pairwise distance by 2 plus the maximum distance between covering centres.

                        sequential compactness ⇐⇒ completeness and total boundedness.

    An infinite discrete space is complete and bounded but not totally bounded or sequentially compact.


    Exam question: 2025 Q5(c): complete and totally bounded

    Prove these hypotheses imply sequential compactness.


    Worked model answer
    Given any sequence, a finite radius-1 cover has a ball containing terms at infinitely many indices. Select
    those terms. Repeat with a finite radius-1/2 cover inside that subsequence, then 1/3, and so on. Take the
    jth term of row j. For every fixed J, the diagonal tail lies in one radius-1/J ball, so mutual distances are
    < 2/J. Thus the diagonal subsequence is Cauchy. Completeness yields a limit in the set. Every sequence
    therefore has a subsequence converging in the set.


    Exam tips and common mistakes

    For a continuous bijection from compact, prove inverse continuity via closed sets: a closed domain subset
    is compact, its image is compact and hence closed. Complete and bounded alone is insufficient in general
    metric spaces.

6     Cantor constructions and final counterexample bank

    Two Cantor results
    Intersection theorem: in complete E, nested nonempty closed Fn with diam Fn → 0 have singleton intersection.
    Select xn ∈ Fn ; diameters make it Cauchy; closedness puts the limit in every Fn ; shrinking diameters give
    uniqueness.
    Middle-thirds set: Z = n Zn , where Zn consists of 2n closed intervals of length 3−n . It is closed bounded,
                            T
    hence compact. The map                       X
                                          ϕ(c) =    2cn /3n (c ∈ {0, 1}N )
                                                 n≥1

    is a continuous bijection from compact binary sequence space, hence a homeomorphism. First differing digit
    dominates the remaining tail, giving injectivity. Thus Z is uncountable; it has empty interior and is closed,
    so is nowhere dense. Its stage covers have total length (2/3)n → 0, proving length zero.

    Exam question: Additional practice: dropping closedness in Cantor intersection

    Give a counterexample even in a complete ambient space.


    Worked model answer
    In complete R, let Fn = (0, 1/n). The sets are nonempty and nested with diameter 1/n → 0. But their
    intersection is empty: any x > 0 exceeds 1/n for some n, and zero is excluded. Thus closedness cannot be
    omitted.
